Springfield Echelon 4.0C COA: Everything You Need to Know

How Springfield's Plate-Free A-CUT Slide Sets a New Standard for Red Dot Security

 Springfield’s Echelon and Aimpoint’s COA come together to make one superb defensive pistol. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)

July 02, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

In 2025 Aimpoint announced its new COA optic, which was good news for pistol shooters. The bad news? COAs came only on Glock pistols during the first year of production. However, the exclusivity between Aimpoint and Glock ended in early 2026, and the next firearms manufacturer to offer a COA-equipped pistol was Springfield. This year, the Echelon 4.0C, 4.0FC and 4.5F are all available with COA optics.
Springfield’s Echelon pistols have been available with the Variable Interface System slide cut and factory-mounted optics before, so you may be asking, what’s all the fuss about the new COA? Fair question.
When Aimpoint introduced the COA last year, its messaging wasn’t just about the COA optic. Instead, the company also focused on the A-Cut mounting system that holds the COA in place. Aimpoint’s contention—and one you may agree with if you’ve spent much time shooting red-dot optics—is that most mounting systems aren’t foolproof.
I’ve had more red-dot sights fly past my forehead when I’m testing pistols than I’d care to count. Sometimes it was my own fault for using the wrong plate or not checking screw tightness, but sometimes reflex sights simply let go.

A-Cut System
The A-Cut system is designed to eliminate the risk of such failures. Look closely at the mounting interface of the Aimpoint and you’ll see that the front of the sight dovetails into a cutout in the slide itself. Examine the system more closely and you’ll see that the rear iron sight plate screws into the slide and creates a second dovetail at the rear of the optic. The front and rear dovetails mean the optic cannot tip forward or backward and off the pistol, as long as the rear sight plate is in position.
There’s more. A lug on the slide cut that’s as long and nearly as wide as the slide cut itself fits into a corresponding cutout in the base of the optic. That eliminates any lateral movement.
The design is extremely rugged. The rear sight plate (“wedge” in Aimpoint parlance) transfers pressure to the dovetail and away from the screws, which reduces the risk that they’ll loosen. The screws included with the Springfield COA pistol are more robust than the traditional screws that are supplied with red-dot optics, and the design ensures the larger screws have less force applied to them than traditional screws.

The 
COA system
 also eschews plates, which is a blessing. Finding the right plate can be a pain, and plates raise the height of the optic and reduce the chances that your optic will co-witness with iron sights. Because the A-Cut system does away with plates and positions the optic lower on the gun, there’s no need for extra-tall sights.
Installing a sight in the A-Cut is extremely simple and straightforward. Simply position the COA in the A-Cut by inserting the front of the sight in the cutout and lowering it onto the lug. Think of it like a ski boot fitting into a binding. Then tighten the screws on the rear sight wedge to the recommended torque setting, and the sight is locked in position.
The A-Cut system is a piece of brilliant engineering, but don’t forget that Aimpoint has been building excellent optics for more than five decades. This is, after all, the company that launched the current red-dot craze in the first place, and no reflex sight company has a longer history producing red dots.
The COA sight itself is an enclosed red dot with 3.5 m.o.a. dot. The left side is home to intensity and windage adjustments. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Aimpoint COA
The COA sight itself incorporates many of the technological advances Aimpoint has perfected over the last 50 years. And in case you’re wondering, COA doesn’t stand for anything. It’s simply a word Aimpoint liked that wasn’t already trademarked.
It’s an enclosed sight that prevents dirt, dust and debris from blocking the emitter. Its high-strength 7075-T6 aluminum body is rugged yet offers a 15x15mm aperture for a wide field of view and increased awareness while shooting. The COA has a 3.5 m.o.a. dot and 12 brightness settings—four night vision and eight daytime.

Shooters are prone to bicker about dot size, but I think 3.5 m.o.a. is about the sweet spot. It’s small enough to shoot accurately yet large enough to grab the shooter’s eye when they’re focused on an imminent threat.
As easy as it is to install and remove the COA on the A-Cut design, I still don’t like having to remove an optic to swap batteries. Thankfully, Aimpoint designed the COA with a side-opening battery compartment that’s held in position with a single screw, so you don’t have to remove the sight to swap CR2032 batteries. You won’t be swapping batteries very often, either; this optic has a battery life of roughly 50,000 hours.
The COA has been tested to 40,000 rounds of 9mm ammunition without failure, so recoil isn’t an issue. The front lens is hardened to prevent damage, and the squared-off design with beveled edges prevents snagging on the draw. Many companies brag that their optics are submersible to three feet, but the Aimpoint is rated to a depth of 82 feet. So here’s your scuba-diving optic.
The COA is user-friendly as well. Buttons on the left side increase or decrease brightness, and holding the button powers the unit off. The elevation adjustment screw is on the left, and the windage adjustment is on the right. An adjustment tool is included.
The A-Cut interface and COA optic are excellent, but you can only truly appreciate the setup when it’s tethered to a superb pistol like the Echelon. As the COA is making waves in the red-dot world, so the Echelon has staked its claim as one of the best striker-fired, polymer-frame 9mms in a heavily crowded market.
The combination of a sturdy gun and a sturdy sight and sight-mounting system makes the new Echelon COA an excellent, reliable defensive pistol. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Echelon 4.0C
We’ve lauded praise on this pistol in all its forms here at 
Handguns
, and the COA package is available on the C, FC or F variants—giving shooters plenty of options for concealed carry, competition, duty or home defense.
I tested the Echelon 4.0C COA, and that combination offers outstanding versatility. As a compact, the 4.0C sports a four-inch barrel and comes with a compact 15-round flush-fit magazine as well as an extended 18-round mag. On my postal scale the test pistol weighed 25 ounces with the 1.7-ounce optic and an empty 15-round magazine, although the website lists the weight at around 24 ounces.
Either way, the gun is light enough to carry and weighs less loaded than a Commander-size 1911 9mm weighs empty. With the COA in place, I could still conceal the pistol under light clothing, and the rounded edges of the Echelon prevent it from printing.

With an optic like the COA installed, the Echelon’s bold slide cuts come into their own. The forward trench and grooves and rear “ears” on the slide offer all sorts of gripping points if you don’t want to grab the optic while operating the slide. Of course, you can do that because of the robust A-Cut interface, but you’ll find all sorts of anchor points for cycling the gun.
COA models come with the sight installed, and otherwise the gun is standard Echelon. The grip’s microtexturing is excellent, and the gun ships with three backstraps. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Features
Springfield’s micro-texturing is the best in the industry, offering a secure grip without the cheese grater effect and sore mitts you’ll suffer after long shooting sessions with some other pistols. The grip is just right, and a trio of interchangeable backstraps that comes with each pistol allows you to achieve exactly the feel you want. There’s a three-slot rail on the dust cover, and the ambidextrous controls make this pistol suitable for southpaws.
The Echelon sports a flat-face trigger and serialized Central Operating Group, which makes it modular and enables you to swap grip modules. The trigger pull is better than on most other pistols in this segment, with a smooth take-up and smooth break that is very predictable and promotes accuracy.
Aside from the ambi mag release and slide stop, the only other control is a rotating takedown lever on the left side of the pistol that has enough of a shelf built into it that a right-hander’s support-hand thumb can put downward pressure on it for added control.
I do wish the slide stop was just a bit bigger, and I’ve come to prefer the overhand pull-and-release method for dropping the slide on this gun, instead of hunting for the undersize slide stop.

Final Thoughts
This is a fun pistol to shoot for drills, and the gun/optic combo offers accuracy potential that will spoil you. It’s easy to get a high hold on the Springfield because of its grip and undercut design. The grip design manages recoil well, and the optic’s wide window allows you to stay on target or transition to the next one very quickly.
As soon as I learned that the COA was coming to other pistol platforms, I began wondering when Springfield would join with Aimpoint. Thankfully, that time is now. This combination isn’t exactly cheap—suggested retail is $1,119—but it’s worth it to never again fool with optics plates, or watch your red dot fly past your head.
You can run the Echelon COA as hard as you like without the fear that either your gun or optic will quit on you. This collaboration should excite pistol shooters.
