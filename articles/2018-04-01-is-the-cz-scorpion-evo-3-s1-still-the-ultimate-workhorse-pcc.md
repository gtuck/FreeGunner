Is the CZ Scorpion EVO 3 S1 Still the Ultimate Workhorse PCC?

Evaluating Performance, Ergonomics, and Value in a Crowded PCC Market

 The CZ Scorpion EVO still stands out both in performance and sci-fi looks. (James Tarr photo)

April 01, 2018

By James Tarr

In a world increasingly made of polymer-framed firearms, the CZ Scorpion EVO still stands out both in performance and sci-fi looks. The modern evolution of a Cold War classic, the Scorpion EVO is available in several different variations. I secured one of the basic carbine models to test.
Technically, this is the CZ Scorpion EVO 3 S1 Carbine. EVO 3 for third generation and S1 for semiauto. This design was brought to life as a submachine gun, but it has taken well to both pistol and carbine versions. It was first introduced to the U.S. market in 2015.
The gun’s non-reciprocating charging handle is forward on the left side. The safety is ambidextrous. (James Tarr photo)
Features
Chambered in 9mm, the carbine features a 16.2-inch barrel tipped with a muzzle brake. The gun is supplied with two 20-round magazines, and CZ sells 30-round magazines as well. Personally, I don’t think the piece looks right unless it’s got a 30-rounder locked in place. Mags are polymer, translucent, reliable and inexpensive.
Construction of the Scorpion EVO 3 is, shall we say, nontraditional. The entire receiver is a polymer shell—actually, two polymer halves connected by screws. This may sound cheap or disposable, but in fact it is neither. Remember, the EVO was originally designed as an SMG, and it is currently fielded by military and police forces in at least eight countries.

This is a straight-blowback carbine, and roughly 25 percent of the carbine’s weight resides in the bolt. As a result the recoil impulse of the carbine is unusual. There isn’t muzzle rise when you fire; rather, the entire gun jumps in your hand and then settles back down.
New for the carbine version is a polymer handguard featuring M-Lok attachment slots for all your accessory mounting needs. It seems to add a few ounces, and my scale put the carbine at six pounds, 10 ounces. The entire top of the carbine features an uninterrupted polymer Picatinny rail, which is fine since this is a 9mm carbine and not a long-distance rifle.
The rear sight features four different-sized apertures. While the stock and receiver are polymer, the lockup of the folding stock and its mount to the receiver are aluminum. (James Tarr photo)
The iron sights are excellent. Both the front and rear sight bases are constructed of beefy aluminum. The rail height off the stock is higher than with an AR-15, which means you should be able to mount most red dots directly to the rail without a riser.

The carbine features the same folding adjustable stock as seen on the original SMG. It is a polymer piece with an adjustable length of pull from 12 to 14 inches. It has a smooth rubber buttpad.
Press the large button on the left side of the stock near the receiver and it folds to the right. The stock itself is polymer, but the lockup on the folding latch is all aluminum. The carbine can be fired with the stock folded, although I’m not sure why you’d want to, other than to look cool.
The charging handle of the Scorpion is on the left side, forward. It does not reciprocate when firing and locks back on an empty magazine. The charging handle is easily reversible.
The safety lever is polymer and ambidextrous. The magazine release is a serrated polymer paddle on either side of the front of the trigger guard. Move it forward to drop the magazine. I’d recommend stripping the empty magazine out with your left hand, as your left thumb sits right on the magazine release as you grab the magazine (they tend to not drop free when empty).
The bolt catch is a large, serrated steel lever on the left side of the gun, above and slightly to the rear of the magazine well. After seating a fresh magazine into the mag well, just pull down on the mag catch with your thumb to chamber a new round.

It Runs and Runs
I have put a lot of rounds downrange through Scorpion EVOs and have owned a pistol version since 2015, so that should tell you how I feel about the design. They run and run. The only issue I have is with the slightly unfriendly controls. The charging handle is a bit too short, trigger pull is too heavy, the right side of the safety pokes my finger when I shoot, and the pistol grip is both slick and features an oddly aggressive angle.
However, the great thing about the Scorpion design is that if you want to upgrade it, using just 1.5mm and 3mm punches and hex wrenches you can swap out every part on the gun but the barrel in minutes. There are plenty of aftermarket parts (extended charging handles, redesigned safety levers, pistol grips and more), and they’re affordable.
Pistol-caliber carbines
 have always been popular but have seen increased interest in the past few years. The Scorpion EVO has stayed popular because it is light, reliable, comparatively inexpensive and offers a unique appearance.
