Dan Wesson DWX and Specialist Pistols: Full Hands-On Review

The DWX and Specialist 1911s exhibit custom-made quality at two-thirds the price.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

March 18, 2026

By James Tarr

A lot of YouTubers create tier lists, ranking manufacturers of different classes of firearms based on quality. Many of them don’t have relevant experience to support their conclusions, so, as a result, the lists are often based on perception, sometimes influenced by other channels, ad campaigns, conjecture, emotion, and maybe some astrology. I’ve seen lists for AR-15s and 1911s a lot, but one glaring omission of late is Dan Wesson. 
In 2011, I wrote an article and opined, “Dan Wesson provides 1911s equal to many custom gunmakers for hundreds of dollars less.” That statement is still true. Dan Wesson has always produced excellent pistols that are near or equal in quality to custom shops. Where it has struggled is in getting that news to the gun public. To that end, Dan Wesson is, in effect, relaunching the brand in 2026. Its pistols are being shipped in new cases that highlight the product’s “semi-­custom” quality. Guns & Ammo secured a sample each of the 
Specialist 1911
 and 
the full-­size DWX
. Both pistols are optic ready, too.
The full-size DWX has a full-length steel rail that’s flush to the muzzle. Seven slots support most lights and laser sights. The 11-degree recessed target crown and forward serrations are distinctive touches. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The DWX
Let me detail the DWX first. It’s a bit of a unicorn. The single-­action-­only (SAO) semiautomatic is available in compact and full-­size models. It was designed to combine the best features from the Cold War-classic CZ 75 and America’s pistol, the Model 1911. DWX models are made at the Dan Wesson facility in Norwich, New York, but Dan Wesson became part of the 
Colt CZ Group
 in 2021, which is why this combination seems natural. 
The Dan Wesson DWX features dovetailed iron sights. The front has a green fiber-optic insert providing great daytime visibility, and the rear is an all-black, fully adjustable serrated notch atop a tombstone profile.(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The CZ 75 offers great reliability, double-­digit capacity and an ergonomic grip frame, and the Model 1911 still has a trigger against which all other carry guns are judged — as it should be. The DWX Compact has an aluminum frame and a 4-­inch barrel, purpose-­built for concealed carry. That configuration is fed by CZ 75 Compact magazines usually having a 15-plus-1 capacity. The DWX full-­size is what I reviewed for this assignment. The barrel is nearly ­5-­inches — 4.95 inches to be exact — and the frame and slide are machined from stainless ­steel. The full-size model is bigger and heavier than what most would want to carry, but it serves well as a competition or home-­defense gun.

The DWX slide features a continuous serrated flat-top across the “9MM”-marked match barrel. Serrations extend over the removable optic coverplate, also. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Aesthetically, the frame and slide of the DWX are finished in satin black DLC. It’s only available with the red anodized aluminum trigger, grips and basepads. That’s a bit of a misstep, in my opinion, leaving out those who prefer all-­black guns or simply don’t want a DWX with red highlights. (It does accept standard CZ 75 grips and magazine basepads if you want to pursue a more subdued appearance.) 
The full-size DWX is long, measuring 81/2 inches. It’s also tall at 5.85 inches and weighs 45 ounces with an empty magazine in place. Its fed by widely available CZ P-­10F/P-­09 magazines, too, which means it has an amazing 19-plus-one-round capacity.
The DWX optic plate includes a rear dovetail to accept iron sights for co-witnessing as a backup to a red-dot. It accepts standard CZ optic plates, too. For testing, G&A mounted a Trijicon RMR HD. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A 1911’s slide rides outside the frame rails, and the barrel typically has both a bushing and a swinging link. With a CZ 75, the slide rides inside the frame, and the bushingless barrel locks up with the breech without the use of a link. The DWX uses a combination of the two. The slide rides outside the rails like a 1911, but it incorporates a bushingless barrel with a CZ 75-­style lock-­up. It sports a fully ramped barrel unique to the DWX, as well as a CZ 75-­style external extractor.

The red anodized aluminum trigger of the DWX sets these models apart. Whether on the lightweight compact version or the full-size steel model, shooters can expect an outstanding trigger experience. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
On G&A’s sample DWX there was just the tiniest amount of play between the slide and frame, and zero play between the barrel and frame. This is exactly what you want. It results in a gun that is accurate and one that won’t choke as it gets a little dirty.
The top of the slide is flat and serrated, and there are flat-­bottomed serrations forward and rear on the slide. The front sight is steel with a green fiber optic. The front sight uses a 1911-­style dovetail. The steel rear sight is plain black and serrated. There is a click-­adjustable screw on top of the rear sight that allows you to adjust for elevation. The rear sight is secured into the dovetail by two set screws; Loosening them allows us to drift the sight in the dovetail to adjust for windage and then lock it back down. The rear sight is set into a removable plate, also. Take that off and the slide accepts optic plates available through 
the CZ USA web shop
. They offer three different adapter plates for this gun.
To “DW” signature magazine basepads feature eight dimples at the bottom. If you want to track the rotation of one magazine among many or want to monitor feeding malfunctions, simply color in the dimple. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
While you can carry various models of the CZ 75 cocked and locked, most do not have safeties properly shaped or positioned for this. The thumb safety on the DWX, though, is perfect. My issue is the detent on it, which requires too much force to engage. I’ve handled and shot a lot of DWX pistols since Guns & Ammo’s May 2023 first look. I’ve never had a problem, so this is just one of those things that happens and should prove (once again) that gun publications don’t get cherry-­picked guns to review. Working the safety a few hundred times may fix the issue.
The triggerguard is undercut, and the front of it is checkered. The front and back of the frame sport raised checkering, too. Combined with the aggressive texture on the slim aluminum grips, your hand should not move on this gun while shooting. The trigger pull on our sample was 3¾ ­pounds. There was a short takeup, then a crisp wall to break through with near-­zero overtravel. Reset was short and positive.
The aggressive 30-lpi frontstrap checkering and similarly fine texture of the red anodized aluminum grips ensures complete control of the DWX when fired. The red aluminum basepad is the perfect complement. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The magazine well in the frame is beveled, and the steel magazine release is slightly extended and easy to reach. The front of the frame has a seven-­slot rail for mounting lights. If you want to accessorize, the DWX accepts magwells meant for the TS/Czechmate series of pistols.

Bore height off the hand is the same as a standard CZ 75, which means it is quite low. This is important because the lower the bore height — all things being equal — the less muzzle rise. Due to the low bore and the weight of the DWX, it’s just a pussycat at the range.
The full-size Dan Wesson DWX has a long-enough rail that large pistol lights such as the SureFire X300 Turbo that usually feature max output and longer battery life don’t extend beyond the muzzle. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Specialist
A stainless-steel 10mm match barrel offers suberb accuracy. The top slide serrations extend just sightly beyond the ejection port, ostensibly to reduce glare. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Dan Wesson offers several Specialist models, both Commander-­length (4¼-­inch barrels) and full-­size Government models, standard and optic-­ready. G&A received a full-­size Government Model pistol for testing, optic-­ready. These are available in 9mm, 10mm and .45 ACP. Ours was chambered in what some people refer to as “God’s Caliber,” the 10mm. The stainless-­steel magazines provided have an eight-plus-one capacity, but you can find quality nine-­round .40 S&W/10mm magazines on the market, too. With an unloaded magazine in place, weight of this pistol was 42.8 ounces.
The Specialist in 10mm Auto includes two stainless steel magazines with an eight-round capacity. The model will accept larger-capacity 10mm aftermarket mags. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The slide has a traditional rounded shape with just a hint of flat-­top with serrations. I wish the optic plate cover on the back was flat-­topped and serrated as well, for aesthetic reasons, but if you’re running an optic it won’t matter. 
The front sight sports a white ring around a tritium insert for visibility in all lighting conditions. The rear sight is plain black and serrated, with a U-­shaped notch. The front of the rear sight is vertical, so you can rack the slide one-­handed on a hard surface, if necessary. The rear sight — just as with the DWX — is set into a removable plate if you want to mount an optic.
At the front is a dovetailed post with tritium night sight. The Novak-cut rear sight dovetail contains a drift-adjustable Dan Wesson Battlehook rear sight, featuring a U-notch and ledge for one-hand slide racking. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 5-­inch stainless-steel match barrel has an integral ramp and the traditional 1911-­style swinging link. The barrel bushing is a match stainless type, but you don’t need a bushing wrench to remove it. The end of the barrel is slightly flared to mate perfectly with the bushing. Slide-­to-­frame-­to-­barrel fit was excellent, and slide travel back and forth was very smooth. Inside is a stout recoil spring to manage the recoil forces from a 10mm cartridge. The Specialist uses the John Browning’s standard recoil spring guiderod, and the plug under the barrel bushing is stainless and checkered. The slide has forward cocking serrations, and the frame incorporates a single-­slot rail for a light. 
The Specialist utilizes a standard Model 1911 guiderod and recoil spring system. The barrel bushing is fit precisely, but it can be removed without the need for a bushing wrench. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The thumb safety offers nice, oversized, bilateral levers. At the back, there is a Commander-­style stainless hammer that falls after pressing the long, solid aluminum trigger. It’s a bit old-­school. The trigger pull on our sample measured 4 1/2 pounds and was crisp. 
The beavertail grip safety was nicely blended with the frame, and the round triggerguard has a noticeable undercut. You’ll find fine, 30 lines-­per-­inch (LPI) checkering on the frontstrap and mainspring housing, which is aggressive but not so much you’ll regret long range sessions. In that vein, the grips are aggressively textured, too, dimpled G10 panels colored tan and black. At the bottom of the frame is a steel magazine well that significantly speeds up reloads while not extending beyond the magazine’s basepad.
Controls on the Specialist are familiar to 1911 users, including the extended bilateral thumb-safety lever and slide-lock lever, as well as the skeletonized hammer that can be cocked and locked. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
At the Range
Both guns were reliable and exceptionally accurate. Though close in weight, the shooting experience was very different. The bore of the DWX is lower, so, between that and the less powerful 9mm cartridge, I found the DWX produced half the muzzle rise and felt recoil of the 10mm Specialist. The trade is, though, the 10mm hits harder.

A single-slot rail under the dust cover may limit the options when considering a light or laser sight. The rail does add a small amount of weight to the front of the gun, which helps to manage muzzle rise. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The DWX and Specialist arrived in sleeved, deluxe presentation boxes. The Specialist box was blue, and the DWX box was red, matching the gun’s highlights. Open the flip-­top of the box to reveal the first of two layers. The top foam was cutout for the gun and spare magazine, as well as a small booklet detailing the history of CZ. There is also a small polymer object and a Zerust rust-­prevention vapor capsule. Lift out the top foam layer and beneath you’ll find a cutout for a nylon pouch containing the owner’s manual and cable lock.
The solid, long, curved aluminum trigger presents an old-school feel. It includes an adjustable overtravel stop screw. Underneath the mag release is a triggerguard undercut that encourages a high grip. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
I previously wrote that Dan Wesson pistols compare favorably to custom pistols, but what’s custom these days? It seems to have little to do with fit or quality. I wonder if the only thing that’s different between a Dan Wesson and another modernized 1911 is price. No, these pistols are not inexpensive, but even the most experienced 1911 fan will be hard-­pressed to spot quality differences between these and what I’ll call “big box custom 1911s.” However, they’ll sure notice that Dan Wesson’s are two-­thirds the price.
