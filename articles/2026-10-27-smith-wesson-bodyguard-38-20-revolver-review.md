Smith & Wesson Bodyguard 38 2.0 Revolver Review

A Worthy Self-Defense Revolver

 The Bodyguard 38 2.0 was designed to be light, easy to conceal, simple to operate, and accurate enough to stop a threat at close range. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)

October 27, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

Considering the popularity of semiauto self-defense handguns, it’s easy to forget that the modern wheelgun is a viable option for personal protection. But Smith & Wesson hasn’t forgotten about revolvers, and its new Bodyguard 38 2.0 affirms that the company is still committed to building revolvers that meet the needs of modern gun owners.
That’s not to say that the Bodyguard 38 2.0 is radically different than the J frames that have come before it. On the contrary, it embodies the same basic ethos that has made J-frame revolvers a popular choice for decades: They’re easy to conceal, easy to operate and about as fail-proof a personal defense option as exists today. The Bodyguard 38 2.0 is more evolutionary than revolutionary, but it still serves its intended purpose well.
The beauty of this gun lies in its simplicity. It’s a double-action-only revolver with an enclosed hammer. There’s nothing to hang up or foul up, and the layout makes it simple to operate. And all Smith & Wesson fans will appreciate the absence of an internal lock.
It has a 1.875-inch stainless steel, shrouded barrel and a five-shot stainless steel cylinder with PVD finish. The frame is made from aluminum, and weight is a mere 14.2 ounces. It’s chambered in .38 Special +P, and the austere sights are designed with close- range shooting in mind.

This is all pretty standard equipment on a J-frame carry revolver, but the Bodyguard 38 2.0 has some notable upgrades. Foremost is the cylinder release that’s positioned centrally on the tang of the frame. That’s a substantial departure from the left-side cylinder release traditionally found on revolvers, and it makes the gun’s primary control ambidextrous.
Both right- and left­-handed shooters can easily access and operate the cylinder release by simply sliding it forward, and it’s large and serrated for a positive grip and simple operation. Shove it forward, swing the cylinder to the left, and you can load or unload the revolver.
For generations the Patridge-style front sight and gutter rear notch served as the only options for carry revolvers. The Bodyguard doesn’t stray too far from this recipe, but although there’s still a rear U-notch that’s integral to the frame, the pinned front post has a large orange dot. This drives the shooter’s eye toward the front sight, as it should be, and provides a big, bold aiming point when you need to shoot quickly.

The enhanced boot grip is a nice upgrade. It’s easy to conceal but offers a full firing grip—a real benefit on a super-light .38. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
The Bodyguard has a polymer boot grip that provides a full handhold on the gun, and this, in turn, makes for a much more manageable and controlled shoot­ing experience. The soft-touch grip is large but nicely rounded, and that helps reduce printing.
Like most shooters, I am accustomed to left-side cylinder releases, and when I took the Bodyguard 38 2.0 to the range, I wasn’t sure how I would adjust to operating it. But it took no time to fall in love with this gun’s simple operation. That tang-mounted release is wide and every bit as fast as a side release. Southpaws will certainly appreciate the Bodyguard’s engineering tip of the hat, which makes left-handed operation much simpler.
The big, bold orange front dot is a great addition. In realistic terms, a five-­shot .38 revolver is not as versatile as a semiauto 9mm with a red dot, but at the arm‘s-length shooting distances for which the J frame was designed, the orange dot is the best option. When faced with a lethal threat at close range, placing that dot in the center of the target and pressing the trigger is effective.
The grip is excellent, and it seems that through some engineering sleight of hand the boot grip, which is relatively large by carry revolver standards, prints less than the smaller grip on my Ruger SP101. The Bodyguard tucks close to the body and blends in. I suppose the rounded profile looks rather natural around my beltline, which is not an endorsement for my physique.
The bold orange front dot is about as intuitive an iron sight as you’ll find and perfect for close-quarter shooting. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
The double-action trigger is heavy, which is to be expected. Break weight was 9.5 pounds on my Wheeler gauge, so the Bodyguard 38 2.0 doesn’t shoot like a match pistol. Two- to three-inch groups were the norm at 15 yards, and if the shooter’s focus on trigger control lapsed, groups routinely stretched above three inches at that distance. At seven yards offhand, though, I could make consistent head shots on a torso target.

The Bodyguard 38 2.0 was designed to be light, easy to conceal, simple to operate, and accurate enough to stop a threat at close range. It accomplishes all four goals, and does so with the added benefit of an improved front sight and grip and simplified controls. Throw in a modest price tag, and you can see why the newest member of the J-frame clan is a worthwhile option when you’re shopping for your next wheelgun.
