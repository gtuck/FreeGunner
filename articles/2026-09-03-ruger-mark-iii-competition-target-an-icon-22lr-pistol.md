Ruger Mark III Competition Target: An Icon .22LR Pistol

A Classic Model with a Unique Charm and Beauty

September 03, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

You probably already know that Bill Ruger’s first pistol—his first gun of any kind, actually—was the Standard .22 semiauto. Mark I, II, III and IV versions followed over the next seven-plus decades. The Mark III was the last of its kind, as the 
Mark IV underwent a major redesign
 that, among other things, vastly simplified the takedown/assembly procedure.
The Mark III Competition Target was notable for two features. For one, its 67/8-inch barrel is a slab-sided bull with a counter-bored crown. Even more eye-catching are the dark walnut grips. The right panel is flat and checkered with a red Ruger medallion while the left panel sports a prominent thumb rest.
The thumb rest is intended for Bullseye-style shooting. It’s an accuracy discipline where the shooter stands perpendicular to the target—firing arm extended, non-firing hand typically shoved into a pocket.
The sights include a fully adjustable rear sight set in a dovetail and a plain black blade front that’s screwed into the barrel. You won’t find a typical red-dot mounting system on Mark-series pistols. Instead, the top of the receiver is drilled and tapped to accept a rail for mounting a reflex sight with Picatinny-style base or a small scope.

The gun is all stainless steel with a satin finish, and it weighs in at a hefty 44 ounces. The right side of the tubular receiver is engraved with the serial number forward of the ejection port and “Competition Target Model” behind it. On the left side you find a large “Ruger” with “.22 Cal. Long Rifle – Mark III Target–” stacked to the right of it.
Forward of that is a loaded-chamber indicator helpfully labeled “Loaded Chamber Indicator” that protrudes slightly when there’s a round in the chamber. The manual safety is an up/down sliding button, and the bolt stop sits at the top of the grip. The magazine release button at the rear of the trigger guard is slightly relieved to accommodate the grips.
The contrasting black trigger has some takeup and overtravel, but the break on mine is clean and sharp, with a break weight of just over two pounds. The gun is slightly muzzle heavy, which improves stability, and here you’ll appreciate the thumb rest to help support the weight one-handed and keep your trigger finger free to do its job.

Ruger lists a similarly configured Mark IV
 today, and I’m sure it’s superior in many ways. But I gotta say there’s a certain pride that comes with being able to disassemble and reassemble a Mark III successfully—something I’m sure many of you will appreciate and those who’ve shot only Mark IVs will not.
