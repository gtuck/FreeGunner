Geissele's First Bolt-Action: The King Hunter

Geissele first bolt gun is chambered in the new Federal 7mm Backcountry. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

September 23, 2026

By Frank Melloni

When manufacturers dip outside of their lane, we often discount them with a, “Well, what do they know?” attitude. However, if we zoom in closer, a more critical mind will see the relationship and gain appreciation for the finished product. 
Such is the case with Geissele’s King Hunter
, an unsuspecting bolt-action rifle brought to us from a company rooted in AR-15 triggers. 
Cocking is made smooth and easy with a roller system that drastically reduces the effort it takes to lift the bolt. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Geissele Automatics came onto the scene in 2004 when founder Bill Geissele was in search of a better match trigger. Without getting into the nuances of CNC machining and metallurgy, two qualities that make a better bang switch are strength and smoothness. The smoothness is easy to understand and feel. As for strength, it is a requirement to support the reduced engagement surfaces that make a crisp trigger. This becomes doubly true when automatic fire is involved. Excellence in this department 
led to the company’s ARs
. While we get excited when we find a manufacturer capable of making slick-operating manual actions, finding a semiauto maker capable of the same is far more impressive. After all, these need to function without any user interface. This brings us to Geissele’s newest firearm, continuing their tradition of top-end quality through superb craftsmanship.
AICS-pattern magazines are used for the King Hunter. The rifle ships with one 5-round capacity mag. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The King Hunter is not the contemporary amalgam of a preexisting action and barrel thrown together by a manufacturer to enter the bolt game. No, this is a Geissele original, except for supporting components such as the stock made by Manners and the bottom metal by Hawkins Precision. On the other hand, both were made specifically for this rifle. The action is machined from solid bar stock and finished with Geissele’s proprietary Nanoweapon coating. This chrome-nitride surface smooths out the metal’s natural pores and fills in any tool marks left behind by the cutting operations. The result is a bolt that slides along the raceways virtually on its own. Cocking is aided by an ingenious roller system that drastically reduces the effort needed to lift the bolt. The bolt head is of a floating design, allowing it to self-correct with each round it chambers. As this is a 2-lug system, that lift is 90 degrees. The rifle is a long action because Geissele chambered it in the new Federal 7mm Backcountry. This move not only shows dedication to progress, but it also sends the message that their action is built to handle the highest pressure cartridges on the market.
The King Hunter is chambered in Federal’s new 7mm Backcountry, the first round built with Peak Alloy case technology. Geissele notes that the round carries double the energy of a standard .30-06 at 1000 yards. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
As promised, the King Hunter features one of Geissele’s proprietary barrels. These started showing up around a year ago and left the shooting community wondering why. Well, now we know. As the 7mm Backcountry is designed to hit the 3,000 fps with a 20-inch barrel, this is the length that Geissele settled on. I’d describe it as a medium profile, which leaves the gun a touch over 8 pounds. It’s cut with a faster 1:8 twist, which helps it stabilize today’s longer, high-BC bullets. These will leave the factory with a muzzlebrake but also accept a suppressor.

Federal currently offers the 7mm Backcountry in five different loads, three of which were used during testing. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
As metal is Geissele’s true medium, they left the carbon fiber to the professionals. Designed as the OEM stock for the King Hunter, the Manners-built cradle offers an adjustable cheek riser. The oversized grip promotes a thumb-forward grasp that is synonymous with precision shooting. It also offers a ledge to rest your firing hand thumb, which, to my delight, exists on both sides. Butted up against the magazine well is a section of ARCA Swiss rail, making it a snap to affix a shooting tripod. Those who prefer slings have an array of both classic studs and modern QD caps to choose from.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
My initial session with the King Hunter was at one of Federal’s writer camps in October 2024. We were invited to shoot at a variety of steel targets out to 500 yards. The exercise was to show us that the cartridge was capable; however, we didn’t punch paper or measure groups. Back at home, I did 100-yard accuracy testing. Although this is not a match rifle, I was impressed with the results. Sub-MOA groups were regular with the two conventional lead-core ammo types, but the Barnes load just didn’t paint the gun in the right light. That’s not uncommon, as some rifles just don’t agree with all-copper projectiles. During this test, I was reunited with the company’s S700 trigger, which must be one of the cleanest-breaking two-stage triggers on the market. 
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
I finished the day with positional shooting. Overall, I was impressed. For a heart-stopper of a round, it was surprisingly mild to wield unsupported. Geissele’s King Hunter proved to me that the company is up to the task of building precision bolt-action rifles that can handle whatever the industry throws at them. I found it to be undeniably smooth, effortless to handle, and an overall joy to shoot. Apparently, triggers are capable of starting more than just the firing process. In Geissele’s case, they ignited innovation.
