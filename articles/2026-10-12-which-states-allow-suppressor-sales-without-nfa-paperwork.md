Which States Allow Suppressor Sales Without NFA Paperwork?

Excited to buy a suppressor after the ATF's NFA enforcement change? Find out which states allow purchases without federal NFA paperwork

By David Fortier

On October 9, 2026, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) 
made a historic change
. They announced they would 
stop enforcing the federal National Firearms Act (NFA) registration and approval requirements for suppressors
. For many gun owners, this was the moment they’d waited years for. No more waiting months for approval. No more federal tax stamps. But here is the reality check: your state still gets the final say.
Whether you can buy a 
suppressor
 now depends entirely on where you live. The ATF explicitly states that its policy does not override state or local restrictions. That means the rules in Washington, D.C., are not the same as the rules in Texas. Some states have laws that ban suppressors outright. Others have laws that say possession is only legal if it complies with federal registration—even if the feds don't require registration anymore.
To navigate this safely, you need to understand the three categories of states that have emerged under this new policy. This classification reflects state-law guidance available as of October 11, 2026. It is not a substitute for checking the rules in your state before purchasing, but it gives you a roadmap of where the risks lie.
Quick Look
27 states:
 State laws do not tie suppressor ownership to federal NFA registration under current published guidance.
15 states:
 State laws tie suppressor legality to federal registration, creating uncertainty after the ATF change.
8 states plus Washington, D.C.:
 Suppressor possession remains prohibited for most civilians.
Dealers must still complete Form 4473 and conduct a background check when required.
State hunting rules are separate from ownership laws.
Can I Buy a Suppressor in My State?
The simplest way to understand the current rules is to divide states into three groups. Knowing which bucket you fall into tells you what paperwork you can expect and what risks you might face.

States Where You Should Check Before Buying Without Registration
In these 15 states, state law ties suppressor legality to federal registration or compliance. The ATF has stopped enforcing the federal registration requirement, but it has not changed these state statutes. As a result, buyers should not assume that an unregistered suppressor is lawful under state law.
These states include:
Alaska
Colorado
Connecticut
Georgia
Michigan
Mississippi
Montana
Nevada
North Carolina
North Dakota

Wisconsin
This is the gray zone. The statute might say something like "possessing a silencer not registered under federal law is a felony." If the ATF says you don't have to register it, does the state law still apply? Until a state court or lawmaker clarifies this, the risk is on you. The ATF says it cannot determine whether possession under its new federal policy satisfies a state's legal requirements.
Until state officials or lawmakers clarify the rules, check with a knowledgeable dealer or qualified attorney before buying without registration. ATF also continues to accept voluntary NFA registration applications. If you want to be 100% safe, you can choose to register voluntarily to satisfy the state's "compliance" requirement, though this adds time and cost.
States Where Civilian Suppressor Possession Remains Prohibited
The following eight states and the District of Columbia prohibit suppressor possession for most citizens:
California
Delaware
Hawaii
Illinois

New Jersey

Rhode Island 
Washington, D.C.
The ATF's policy does not repeal these state prohibitions. Do not buy or transport a suppressor into one of these jurisdictions assuming the federal change makes it legal. In these states, the ban is written into state code. Federal non-enforcement is irrelevant if the state prosecutor charges you under state law. Moving to one of these states means you cannot bring your suppressor with you unless you dispose of it legally or qualify for narrow law enforcement exemptions.

What Does the ATF Change Mean for Buyers?
In states where state law does not require federal registration, the purchase process is generally simpler. Dealers must still follow applicable federal firearms laws, including Form 4473, background checks when required, recordkeeping and restrictions on sales to prohibited persons. Some dealers may also continue using the old registration process while they adjust their procedures.
It is vital to remember that a Form 4473 is not a free pass. If you are a prohibited person—felon, domestic violence offender, unlawful user of controlled substances—you cannot buy a suppressor even in a legal state. The background check is still required.
In states whose laws still depend on federal registration, the answer is less straightforward. The ATF's policy is a federal enforcement decision; it does not automatically rewrite state law. That distinction is why buyers in those states should confirm the current rules before purchasing. A dealer in a "gray zone" state might refuse to sell until they get legal clarity, forcing you to wait.
Hunting Is a Separate Question
Owning a suppressor is different from using it in the field. A state may permit suppressor ownership but restrict or regulate their use while hunting.
For example, Connecticut and Washington allow you to own a suppressor under certain conditions, but hunting regulations often prohibit using them on game. Other states might allow hunting for predators but ban them for big game seasons.
Check your state wildlife agency's current regulations if that is your intended use. The Department of Fish and Wildlife is the authority here, not the ATF. If you plan to hunt suppressed, verify the rules for your specific game species and season. Do not rely on a firearm dealer's word for hunting regulations.
The Bottom Line
The October 9 ATF policy change made suppressor purchases simpler in many states, but it did not make the rules identical nationwide. Under the current published state-law breakdown, 27 states generally allow dealer sales without federal NFA registration, 15 still have state-law language tied to federal registration, and eight states plus Washington, D.C., prohibit civilian possession.
Check your state's category before you buy. The key question is no longer just whether suppressors are legal in your state, but whether your state's law allows you to possess one without federal registration.

Take the time to do the homework. It is better to spend an hour calling a dealer or reading a state statute than to end up with a felony charge because you assumed federal rules applied to your backyard. If you are in the 27-state group, you're likely clear to proceed. If you are in the 15-state group, tread carefully. And if you are in the prohibited group, respect the line and keep your money for legal gear.
