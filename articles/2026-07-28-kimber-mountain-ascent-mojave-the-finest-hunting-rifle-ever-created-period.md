Kimber Mountain Ascent Mojave: The Finest Hunting Rifle Ever Created. Period.

The Ultimate Ultralight Bolt-Action Performance for Serious Backcountry Hunters

 The author says the revamped Mountain Ascent is the finest hunting rifle ever created. Period. (Photo courtesy David Faubion)

July 28, 2026

By David Faubion

Today, we’re spoiled by a plethora of lightweight rifles, but at the turn of the century (isn’t that fun to say?), Kimber’s 84M Montana was the pinnacle of factory offerings. This dainty gem had a scaled-down Model 70-style controlled-feed action, a blind magazine, a No. 1 contour barrel, a three-position wing safety that locked the bolt closed, and a hand-laid carbon-fiber stock that was stiff and impervious to the elements.
As a teenager and into my 20s, I lusted for Kimber’s 84M Montana in the worst way. Sadly, I couldn’t afford one. Eventually, I was gifted an 84M Classic in .243 Winchester by Shooting Times Editor in Chief Joel Hutchcroft. I was hooked. With a handsome wood stock and stainless metal, it’s a work of art, but after one wilderness bear hunt, I realized the 84M Classic was too pretty for rough hunts. This brought me back to the Montana, a rifle I could use without fear of cosmetic damage.
Eventually, I snagged a Montana in 6.5 Creedmoor and Mountain Ascents in .308 Winchester and .280 Ackley, and I even commissioned a full-custom ultralight Kimber that weighed 3 pounds, 10.6 ounces with scope rings. Each one was superb, but to this day, the Montana in 6.5 Creedmoor is my favorite rifle. It’s reliable, easy to carry, and fits me better than anything I’ve shouldered. Since 2016, I’ve toted that rifle from New Zealand to Alaska and many places in between, taking Himalayan tahrs, chamois, red stags, three species of deer, black bears, and elk.
I mention my long history with Kimber rifles for a reason. During a conversation with Everett Deger, Kimber’s director of marketing, I learned that Kimber was revamping the Mountain Ascent line. I begged him to let me test drive the new rifle on a Wyoming hunt. Last fall, my wish came true when Kimber’s Joseph Krob flew out to join me on a deer and antelope hunt.

The New Mountain Ascent
Kimber’s revamped Mountain Ascent isn’t entirely new. According to Krob, much of the rifle remains unchanged from the version I fell in love with.
“The action, bolt, and trigger of the Mountain Ascent are the same proven designs as before,” said Krob. “The stock is now made by H-S Precision. With an aluminum bedding block, vertical grip, and rear bag cutout, it’s rock solid and offers modern ergonomics that enhance the rifle’s capabilities over the previous generation. It may look different than the prior generation’s hand-laid carbon-fiber stock, but as you’ll soon find out, it performs better, especially from field positions.”
Unlike the original 84M Montana, which was designed to carry first and shoot second, the new Mountain Ascent is designed for both. To prep the rifle for our hunt, I swapped the factory trigger with a TriggerTech Primary, mounted a Leupold VX-5HD Gen II 3-15X 44mm in Warne’s two-piece Maxima mounts and 30mm HyperLite rings, and zeroed the rifle with Winchester’s Supreme Long Range 6.5 Creedmoor ammunition, a load that uses the new 140-grain BC Max bullet optimized for expansion both near and far. Accuracy was great. More important than group size, however, was how good the rifle felt in use. According to Josh Cluff, H-S Precision’s vice president of sales and marketing, this is because the stock was designed for modern shooters.

According to Cluff, “When Kimber came to us with the idea for revamping the Mountain Ascent line, we were delighted to partner with them to build a modern, lightweight stock in Rapid City, South Dakota. The design called for a vertical grip, which excels at long-range precision shooting. It was our first time building a stock with a rear bag cutout. Our Mountain Ascent stock uses an aluminum bedding block on the inside with a Kevlar, fiberglass, and carbon-fiber shell. The complete stock weighs about 2 pounds and performs as well from the bench as it does in the field.”
In the Field
Kimber rifles are built for the field, so I arranged for Tony Chambers of Wind River Air to helicopter Krob and me into a large parcel of landlocked public land for six days of DIY hunting. According to OnX, our longest day was just over 10 miles and consisted of 2,129 feet of elevation gain and loss. Temps in the 70s meant the hip vents in my 
KUIU
 Attack pants were unzipped for most of our hunt, which is rare in mid-October.
On the average day, we’d depart camp before daylight, magazines loaded with rounds and chambers empty. After an hour or two scaling ridges and descending valleys, we’d glass for deer and antelope at daybreak. Midday, we’d move to another location before glassing until dark. Once the sun set, we’d embark upon a multi-hour hike back to camp. Back at our tents, we’d drop the rounds from the rifles’ hinged floorplates, stuff our bellies with food, and go to sleep. On exhausting hunts like this, a lightweight rifle is a critical tool.
Just like 
the original Mountain Ascent
, the components of the new version are built to minimize weight. While the H-S stock is a few ounces heavier, the metal is as dainty as before, with fluting on the 22-inch barrel, bolt handle, bolt knob, claw extractor, and even the bolt body. To protect the Mountain Ascent from the elements, its metal is clad in KimPro II finish, a durable, baked-on coating that wears like Cerakote.
After four days of hunting, Krob christened the Mountain Ascent with his first mule deer. That evening, we glassed a mature buck with the Leupold BX-4 Range HD binos. Following an hour-long stalk, I punched my tag shortly before dark. Just like the rifle, Winchester’s 140-grain BC Max bullet performed perfectly. After hiking dozens of miles and butchering two bucks and packing them to a lone shade tree, our bodies were ready for Chambers and his Robinson R66 to return.

During the helicopter flight back to civilization, I thought back on my history with Kimber rifles. Has the new Mountain Ascent displaced the original Montana as my favorite Kimber? Not quite, but I must concede that today’s Mountain Ascent is a better choice for the modern hunter. This is entirely due to the stock design.
The original stock is a joy to carry, but it’s not a precision shooting implement. In contrast to that, the H-S Precision stock is built for shooting and carries well. It is a tad heavier, but when it comes time to launch a bullet at a living animal, the new stock makes a difference.
Thoughts from a True Kimber Collector
As you can tell, I’m a Kimber fan and have been for a quarter-century. But when I met Ian Padron, a diehard hunter in Kalispell, Montana, I learned that my Kimber passion was merely a hobby. Padron owns 10 Kimber rifles. According to him, the goal was to have enough Kimber Montanas that his wife wouldn’t know when the next one showed up. I think he succeeded.
“They’re spectacular rifles,” said Padron. “I love everything about them. It’s a perfectly sized short action with incredible engineering behind it. The three-position safety is super safe, easy to use, and you can do a quick visual and run the bolt without having the safety off. As an OCD guy, I always take them apart and look for tooling marks. If you look at a bunch of 84Ms, they’re clean on the inside—no burrs or tooling marks. If you keep the bolt lightly lubed, it feels like it’s on rails. In addition, they always cycle and feed.”
Even though I swapped the original trigger for a TriggerTech, Kimber’s factory trigger is excellent. According to Padron, it’s unbeatable.
“If there’s one thing that most hunters can benefit from, it’s a lighter trigger,” said Padron. “Shooting a lightweight rifle with a heavy trigger is a recipe for disaster. Kimber has the best factory trigger out there, especially if you know how to adjust them. With a bit of adjustment, you can get them drop safe down to high 1-pound or low 2-pound range. If you adjust the trigger, be sure to pogo stick it on a wood floor to make sure it’s drop safe. Kimber’s trigger has a deep curve but is flat side to side, so you have a nice surface to grip. It’s almost like shooting a side-by-side shotgun the way the trigger locks you in.”
Today, Kimber has a sub-MOA accuracy guarantee. In the past, however, Kimber was plagued by rumors of inaccuracy. Padron has never witnessed this.
“Guys talk about Kimber roulette,” said Padron. “I’m the first to admit, there were eras when Kimber wasn’t known for stellar accuracy, but I’ve never had one that didn’t shoot. You may have to handload or find the right factory load, but when you find the magical combo, they shoot lights out.”

Perhaps more important than performance is how Kimber rifles carry. Padron likens them to fine shotguns.
“If you carry a Kimber in-hand, it feels like it belongs there,” said Padron. “Much like a shotgun, the rifle disappears during carry. I love that about the 84Ms. In terms of handling, nothing comes close to the original Montana. Any time I hand one to a buddy, their reaction is the same. They say, ‘This is the coolest gun ever.’”
Kimber obviously has more competition in the mountain rifle market today. You can buy a lighter rifle. You can buy a more accurate rifle. You can even buy one with a smoother action. However, I don’t believe any rifle bests Kimber as an overall package. Padron agrees.
“With Kimber, you get the total package,” said Padron. “It’s a modern, classy, superlight sporting rifle with top-shelf ergonomics, fine handling, and performance. They carry great in-hand and sit perfectly on the side of your pack. The first time I used one, I shot a 0.8-inch 10-round group with 
Hornady
’s 147-grain ELD Match ammo. Having confidence in a rifle this light is great.”
For applications where low weight and modern shooting ergonomics matter most, the revamped Mountain Ascent is the best Kimber yet. Then again, I’m biased, because I think this is the finest hunting rifle ever created.
