NEMO Arms Electus: Full Review

NEMO Arms jumps into the bolt-­action market with the Electus. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Alfredo Rico)

March 23, 2026

By Alfredo Rico

There are lots of so-­called “ones” in entertainment history. In “Highlander” (1986), there could be only one immortal. In “The Matrix” (1999), “Neo” is the one that frees humanity from machines. For hunters demanding the best, NEMO Arms is betting that its Electus will be the one rifle for your next big-­game hunt — and the one after that. Electus is Latin for the “chosen” one.
First, a little history on NEMO Arms. In 2011, New Evolution Military Ordnance (NEMO) Arms was founded by members of a Bible study group that included U.S. Army Maj. Gen. (Ret.) Paul Vallely, Clint Walker and Kirk Leopold. God, the Second Amendment and precision-­made firearms are its core values. In 2013, NEMO Arms captured attention for its Omen, the first AR platform to chamber the .300 Winchester Magnum. Its unique recoil-­reducing bolt carrier system lessened perceived recoil to that of a .308.
The folding stock’s hinge is unobtrusive and on the right side. The grip surface is textured, providing a palm swell and thumb shelf. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
NEMO Arms was originally based in Kalispell, Montana, but relocated to Boise, Idaho, in 2016. Initially, it outsourced parts but recently it brought part production in-­house with the acquisition of 
2A Armament Firearms
. NEMO Arms is unapologetically a faith-­driven, premium firearms company — one that doesn’t shy away from using the word “weapon.” 
Hunting Optimized
To prepare for the launch of the Electus, I spent six days hunting elk in the steep, heavily forested Idaho mountains. From sunrise to sunset, we traversed private land around Lake Cascade and Horsethief Reservoir, scouting by ATV and on foot. Unfortunately, there were only hoof prints to track. The beauty of Idaho, though, made for up our lack of success on that field test.

Still, the Electus is an ideal hunting rifle. It’s a beautiful rifle, too. Guns & Ammo’s test rifle was chambered in 7mm PRC, but it is also available in 6.5mm Creedmoor, 6.5mm PRC, 7mm Backcountry, and .300 PRC. 
The rifle weighed 6 pounds, 14 ounces, with an empty magazine, and it includes NEMO Arms’ QC-­1 muzzlebrake. A Savior soft rifle case is also provided with the rifle, which retails for $7,885.
The heart of the Electus is a 
Zermatt Arms Ti3 titanium action
 made to NEMO’s specs, given a 
20-­inch Proof Research cut-­rifled, carbon-­fiber-­wrapped barrel
. The Model 700-footprint action features an integral lug and two small 20-­MOA Picatinny rails for rings. The rails are pinned, ensuring they stay planted even under magnum loads. As with other Zermatt Arms actions, the bolt is a controlled-­round feed (CRF) design featuring an interchangeable floating bolt head.

The conical detent is latched by a spring-loaded locking plate. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
With CRF, the bolt’s extractor latches onto the cartridge’s rim and holds onto it as it strips a round from the magazine. It holds onto the rim until the case is ejected from the chamber. By contrast, a push feed design’s extractor grabs the rim after the cartridge is fully seated into the chamber. The advantage of a floating bolt head is that it compensates for any slight misalignment in the bolt body and receiver. The locking lugs self-­align, allowing the locking lugs to engage the receiver. It also allows the base of the cartridge to sit flush against the bolt head. When everything is straight going into the chamber, precision is enhanced. The bolt body includes a small, spiral-­fluted bolt handle.
Hanging from the receiver is a 
TriggerTech Diamond trigger
 that’s adjustable from 4 ounces to 32 ounces. M5 DBM bottom metal finishes off the stock. The magazine release is cleverly integrated into the triggerguard, too. They are the two dark tabs on the triggerguard. To feed it, the action accepts a flush-­fit three-­round magazine.
The ambidextrous, cleanly integrated black tabs on the triggerguard are the magazine release. Push to release the magazine. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
Proof Research carbon-­fiber-­wrapped barrels are known for being light weight and accurate. NEMO Arms chose a 20-­inch barrel so that when adding a suppressor, the rifle doesn’t feel like a flagpole. Completing the package is the company’s logo emblazoned in red on the barrel’s shank.
Attached to the 5/8-­24 threaded barrel is the NEMO-­designed QC-­1 titanium, quad-­chamber, self-­timing muzzlebrake. The first ports are 90 degrees to minimize the exiting gases coming back toward the shooter. The three forward ports are angled 60 degrees. The company claims a 55-percent recoil reduction.
A front rail design includes three quick-detach sockets for slings. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
Cradling it all is a 
 Manners MCS-­Pro Pack Folder Adjustable (PPFA) carbon-­fiber folding stock,
 which is based on the Manners Elite Hunter stock. The long-­action stock weighs 36 ounces. It is a fully featured, modern hunting stock that will appeal to precision shooters and adventure hunters alike. It includes a Manners M5 folding mini-­chassis, an ARCA-­compatible rail molded within, grip with vertical frontstrap, cheek riser, five quick-­detach (QD) mounts, and a Picatinny-­style rail mount. The length of pull varies from 133/4 inches with a half-­inch buttpad or 14 3/4 inches with a 1-­inch Kick-­EEZ buttpad.

Behind the bolt handle, the stock folds with the press of a recessed, serrated button. It surrounds the bolt handle when folded, protecting it, and it is held to the stock by strong magnets. When extended, a conical tip is captured within a spring-­loaded locking plate that secures it. Extended, the rifle measured 43 inches from buttpad to forend. When folded, the rifle measured 31 inches. Folded, the rifle could be easily strapped to a pack.
The cone-shaped, spiral-fluted bolt handle is distinctive and intuitive to manipulate. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
At The Range
I received G&A’s Electus test rifle two weeks ahead of Idaho’s 2025 elk season. I attached a 
Nightforce NX8 4-­32x50mm F1 with a Horus Tremor 3 reticle
, zeroed it and evaluated different loads. The rifle setup is how I would build a lightweight hunting rifle if I were to choose everything myself. Its implementation of weight, features and hinge design is the best I’ve seen on a hunting stock, but it serves both the hunter and precision shooter. For me, an ARCA-­rail is a must on a hunting rifle. It allows me to lock it into my tripod for a stable support in many shooting positions. The flat-­bottomed forend won’t rock when shooting off a pack or support bag. Mounting a bipod to the rail underneath the forend’s nose lengthens the shooting base offering additional stability, if needed.
OnX (onxmaps.com) kept us on the right property to hunt.
The vertical frontstrap, palm swell and thumb shelf are perfect for how I prefer to shoot. Together, they allow my hand and trigger finger to land in the same spot each time for effortless placement and straight-­back triggerpull. There was zero play in the hinge — not even a little creep — making me forget that this stock was a folder.
I’m used to shooting light triggers, so I found the TriggerTech Diamond just right. I adjusted the trigger to 1.8 pounds, the same as on my competition rifle. The trigger tested smooth and cleanly with no pressure hump at the end. It’s a predictable trigger.
Whether shooting off a bench or tripod, the QC-­1 muzzlebrake did a great job controlling the rifle. The rifle loves to track straight back and jump minimally. Spotting my impacts was a breeze. Despite shooting 60 rounds in a range session, it didn’t feel like I was shooting a magnum cartridge.
The closest the author got to an elk was finding a fresh track. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
Shooting at 1,000 Yards
Light rifles are harder to shoot precisely because they are more sensitive to body or hand pressure than heavy rifles. Accuracy was impressive, nonetheless. My first three, three-­shot groups with 
Hornady
’s Precision Hunter 175-­grain ELD-­X measured .39, .29 and .50 inch. In total, five groups averaged .59 inch! Hornady’s Outfitter 160-­grain CX bullet, Federal’s Fusion Tipped 175-­grain FT and Remington’s Cut 160-­grain CT averaged .77, .74 and .74 inch, respectively. Remington Core-­Lokt Tipped 175-­grainer averaged .81 inch. It didn’t perform as well with Hornady’s Match 180-­grain ELD-­M. The group average was 1.4 inches. I shot the Precision Hunter rounds to 1,000 yards, and the Electus delivered dependable performance. I produced easily repeatable hits to 900 yards. I overestimated the wind at the 1,000-­yard distance.
The downside to a 20-­inch barrel is a velocity loss of 25 to 35 feet per second (fps) per inch of length. The velocities were around 116 fps to 294-fps slower than what’s printed on the box. That the velocities don’t match the box is expected and not a concern for me. I didn’t find it to be a liability, even with the Hornady Outfitter 160-­grain CX ammo I chose for the hunt. It’s effective terminal velocity is above 1,500 foot-­pounds (ft.-lbs.) of energy. According to the 
Applied Ballistics Quantum app
, that limited me to 550 yards with the 160-­grain CX bullet and 700 yards with the 175-­grain ELD-­X. I’m good with those limitations.
The Kick-EEZ buttpad helps soften perceived recoil. (Photo by Alfredo Rico)
During the hunt is where I experienced the joy of carrying a lightweight rifle. With the NX8 scope, the Electus weighs 8 pounds. I spent six days with the rifle in the scabbard of my Eberlestock pack, hanging off the pack or slung across my back. Even when gasping for air on the steep climbs, the rifle felt light.

The Electus is a refined, precision hunting rifle with no compromises. It was confidence-­inspiring and fun to shoot. I have rifles that are more wallet friendly that also get the job done. The older I get, though, the pickier I am about the details. If I added the Electus to my collection, it would be the one.
