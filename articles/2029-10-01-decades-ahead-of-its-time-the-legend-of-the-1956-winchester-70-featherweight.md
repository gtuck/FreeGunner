Decades Ahead of Its Time: The Legend of the 1956 Winchester 70 Featherweight

How Winchester re-engineered its iconic bolt-action design to reduce weight without sacrificing strength or reliability.

 Winchester’s original Model 70 Featherweight was in some ways a gun ahead of its time. (John Haviland photo)

October 01, 2029

By John Haviland 

The old Winchester Model 70 Featherweight in my hands represents many milestones, from the height of rifle workmanship to a turning point in rifle manufacturing and a cartridge that quickly closed the door on other dual-purpose cartridges.
The Featherweight was made in 1956 and chambered in .243 Win. Back then, planned obsolescence was unknown in manufacturing. 
Winchester
 had been building Model 70s for nearly 20 years, and the rifles were at the peak of their success—although some Model 70 fans say rifle quality had started to stumble by that time.
The Featherweight’s bolt handle knob is drilled, while standard Model 70s were not. (John Haviland photo)
Shaving Weight
Standard Model 70
s of the time weighed about nine pounds with scope. Winchester whittled down that heft with a Featherweight rifle starting in 1952. The Featherweight’s tapered 22-inch barrel saved a few ounces compared to the standard rifle’s 24-inch barrel.
Omitting the barrel boss for the rear sight also shaved weight, as did eliminating the fore-end screw. Making the floorplate and trigger guard of aluminum instead of steel trimmed about three ounces. An aluminum buttplate saved another ounce or so. You have to wonder what purists at the time thought of aluminum, which is now the norm.

The point pattern checkering on the Model 70 Featherweight has fairly well cut diamonds. And unlike on the standard Model 70, the Featherweight had no boss for the rear sight. (John Haviland photo)
The Featherweight’s stock is also somewhat different than that of the standard rifle. The Featherweight’s fore-end is slightly thinner, but still a long 10.75 inches. Two seven-inch deep holes drilled lengthways into the buttstock further lightened the rifle. Scopes were becoming common additions to rifles back then, and the Featherweight’s Monte Carlo comb raised the comb half an inch, compared to a standard rifle.
Weight
The Featherweight .243 weighs an ounce under eight pounds with a Kollmorgen 4X Bear Cub scope in Buehler steel bases and rings. That’s seems heavy compared to some of today’s rifles. However, a current Model 70 short-action Featherweight weighs only two ounces less, wearing a Leupold 2.5-8 in one-piece aluminum rings.
A look with a Lyman Borecam digital bore scope into the Featherweight’s bore shows only a slight amount of wear at the start of the rifling. Over the length of the bore, a few places had tool marks across the top of the lands. Mainly, though, the bore’s surface was smooth. Short streaks of copper fouling had formed in only a few spots after firing 50 rounds.

Such a pristine bore should answer the question of whether yesterday’s bullets or rifles were the source of accuracy deemed mediocre today. We have to cut the Featherweights some slack due to the flat top of the post reticle in its Bear Cub 4X scope that spans slightly more than an inch at 100 yards. Yes, I could’ve replaced the Bear Cub with a more modern scope, but the Buehler base and rings require three hands to assemble. Plus, the Bear Cub looks appropriate on the rifle.
Performance
The rifle shoots fairly well from the bench, the trigger tripping perfectly at three pounds. Several handloads hovered around the one m.o.a. mark, and the overall average of five-shot groups for a dozen loads was 1.6 inches.
Shooting offhand, even with my front hand far forward on the fore-end a good three inches of the fore-end protrudes past my hand. The grip seems a bit thick. But its curve keeps my wrist straight. From sitting, the comb is just high enough to lightly support my cheek while looking through the scope. The action is tight but smooth. The bolt requires some force to operate, and I have to lower it from my shoulder in order to get sufficient purchase on the bolt handle.
The old Winchester sat idle for decades and is in its original condition, except for the addition of a ventilated recoil pad.
Final Thoughts
Perhaps the rifle would best serve as a collector’s item to be put by in a safe. However, that would be unfair to the Winchester wood and metal craftsmen who built the rifle. I plan to put the rifle to good use. It will acquire some scratches and dings along the way, as a hunting rifle is meant to be seasoned.
