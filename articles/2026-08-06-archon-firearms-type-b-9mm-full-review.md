Archon Firearms Type B 9mm: Full Review

This new handgun from Archon Firearms has the capability to perform well in self-defense or competition. Here's a full review. 

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

August 06, 2026

By Guns & Ammo Staff

There are a variety of pistol designs available to us. They vary in size, weight, materials used and finishes applied. Nearly all of them operate using a recoil-­operated, locked-­breech design. Whether we are talking about a G17, a Model 1911, a Hellcat or P365, they all employ a variation of the tilt-­barrel system. 
Las Vegas-­based Archon Firearms
 took a different approach. 
The Type B
 is a capable striker-­fired handgun that uses a unique recoil system to improve the placement of rounds on target.
The Type B is a recoil-­operated semiautomatic featuring a unique locking-­block and breech design, and a triple recoil spring and guiderod assembly. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A locked-­breech handgun maintains the relationship between the barrel and the slide until the bullet has left the bore. The timing is a crucial element toward accuracy. Most popular handguns achieve this using the tilting barrel system, thanks John Browning. The barrel and the slide are mated for the initial travel and then the barrel is arrested, usually by a locking block or pin, unlocking it and tilting upward at the muzzle to allow the slide to cycle. This system works, but it has disadvantages.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
Archon Firearms’ handguns, including the Type B, utilize an entirely different method. Its “AF-­Speedlock” system employs a Y-­shaped locking block fit to the underside of the barrel. When the handgun is fired, the barrel and slide travel a short distance rearward to allow
the block to
unlock.
The block then drops down into the frame and allows the slide to continue its recoil cycle without disturbing the bore vertically or horizontally. This approach was first seen on the Bergmann Model 1903 designed by Louis Schmeisser. It employed a similar style of vertical locking block. Other than than being used on Japan’s Type 94 Nambu, the concept sat dormant for nearly a century. Then, in the sequel that no one saw coming, the Bergmann system reemerged on the Russian-­made Strizh handgun around 2012. Videos of Russian counter-­terror operators using the distinct handgun created international buzz among handgun enthusiasts, and the pistol even had a cameo in Stephen Hunter’s 2014 novel “Sniper’s Honor.”

The Strizh’s selling point was its extremely low bore axis, made possible by the locking system. Muzzle rise was reported to be minimal due to the physics of the bore. Some Italian-­made variants of the Strizh known as the “Strike One” made it to the U.S., but it never really caught on. In 2015, G&A contributor Keith Wood had a sample, found it innovative but too large for daily carry. It showed promise, though.
Archon Firearms refined the platform, improving ergonomics and adding practical features. All production was brought in-­house and the design has reached maturity in the second-­generation Czech-­made and German-­proofed Archon Type B.
The Details
Roughly the size of the G19, the Type B is a compact handgun. At 7.7-­inches long and 5.2-­inches tall, it fits comfortably in the “small enough to conceal yet large enough to shoot well” category. Unloaded, the Type B weighs 1 pound, 10.5 ounces. Four variants are offered, as well: standard, optic-­ready (OR), suppressor-­ready (SR), and optic and suppressor ready (OSR).

The Type B has a polymer grip frame built around an internal steel chassis. The polymer grip module can be configured to better fit hands of various sizes using a combination of backstraps and grip extensions. Using a 3mm punch, the backstrap and grip modules can be added or subtracted for one of three different grip lengths. Think of it as the ability to select between a subcompact, compact, and full-­length pistol. The owner can configure it in the sub-­compact length for concealment and then add a grip extension for a shooting match on the weekend. The entire grip module can also be replaced, opening the door to aftermarket options.
The triggerguard incorporates a high-­grip undercut to keep the gun low in the hand. The textured grip panels enhance the shooter’s control of the frame. The panels wrap around the grip to provide a generous amount of surface area and extend to the side of the frame to interface with the shooter’s support-­hand thumb. The standard grip surface is rough enough for regular use without being too abrasive for concealed carry. The textured sections are adhesive backed so they, too, can be changed out by the user. Panels with more aggressive gradients and even alternate colors are available for sale on the company’s website for around $25 apiece.
A safety lever is absent on the Type B trigger. Instead, the pistol offers a smooth, flat-­face trigger. The triggerguard also has an undercut. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Type B’s controls are standard for a striker-­fired handgun. The trigger is a hybrid design with an angled flat face. There is no external trigger safety, but the trigger articulates several degrees before the firing mechanism is engaged. If the trigger does not pivot, it will not fire. The G&A sample’s trigger tested at 43/4 pounds with minimal creep. The reset was short and tactile, as well.
Are you a lefty? The magazine release is easily accessible, but it is reversible for left-­hand use. The slide stop sits on the left side of the frame, though, and there is no manual safety.
Visually, the slide is the most striking design element of the Type B. The top of the slide dips down behind the muzzle, which may be a tip of the hat to the Strizh’s distinctive profile. Since the feature coincides with the slide’s front cocking serrations, it actually adds a tactile surface that supports working the slide. The Archon Firearms Type D is identical to the Type B, but it deletes this shape for those who might prefer a more traditional slide profile.

The Type B is equipped with a red fiber-­optic front post, and a serrated steel rear notch adjustable for windage. The rear sight dovetail and front sight mortice are cut so that the Type B is compatible with sights designed for Glock handguns. (Hello, aftermarket.)
The slide cut is optic specific, but low enough that the standard sights can be aligned at the bottom through the window. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The optic-­ready version of the Type B is machined to accept a single optic format, either the Acro, RMR, or RMS. We chose to mount an Aimpoint Acro P-­2 with a 3.5 MOA dot to evaluate this feature. The optic mounts directly to the slide, so no adapter plates or conversion charts is needed. The system is simple, rugged, and keeps the optic as low as possible on the slide. Though this is certainly less versatile than some other footprints, it leaves little room for failure.
The Type B has a 4.3-­inch black nitride-­finished barrel. The barrel dispenses with the traditional locking lugs found on most Browning-­style handguns. Instead, a steel Y-­shaped locking block engages with a cylindrical section of the barrel when in ­battery. The 9mm twist rate is 1-­in-­10 inches, and the muzzle has a 40-­degree crown. A 5-­inch suppressor-­ready barrel with a ½-­28 thread is available as an accessory for $160.
The Type B also sports a recoil system with three different springs. Two are captive coil springs and a third is a non-­captive spring. The system manages slide velocity during cycling and helps make the pistol as soft-­shooting as possible regardless of bullet type or standard versus high-­velocity ammunition. The Type B uses an external extractor and a fixed frame-­mounted ejector.
The beavertail extends over the hand. From the shooter perspective, the slide’s backplate and rear sight are serrated. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Magazines for the Type B are produced by Mec-­Gar for Archon Firearms. They have steel bodies containing polymer basepads and followers. Two magazine length are provided, including a 15-­round mag for compact configuration, and a longer 17-­round mag for an extended grip. Additional magazines are available at $34 each.
Much of the hype surrounding the Type B relates to its low bore axis. “Bore axis” is the relationship between the center of the bore and the web of the shooter’s grip, i.e., the fulcrum. All things being equal, a lower bore axis helps the shooter with leverage to counteract muzzle rise. A lower bore axis makes keeping the sights on the target easier and also means the potential for manufacturing a lighter slide to creates less reciprocating mass during the firing cycle.
At .47 inch, the Type B has one of the lowest bore axes on the market. For comparison, the G17 measures 1.26 inches, a M1911 averages 13/4 inches, and the M9 is 1.8 inches. In fairness, there is a bit of gamesmanship at work. The Type B grip uses an angular rather than a radial surface at the top of the backstrap, which raises the measuring point on the grip by roughly a quarter inch. Still, the bore axis on the Type B is very low.
It seems that every part of the Type B was engineered to take advantage of the low bore. For example, the ejector is at a 45-­degree angle to limit its vertical protrusion. Even the striker system has a minimalist profile to keep the slide as low as possible.

At The Range
Measurements are fine but live-­fire results are what matters, and the Type B did not disappoint. Every handgun has a distinct recoil impulse, but the Type B was notable for its uniqueness. The combination of the low bore axis and the lack of barrel tilt due to the AF-­Speedlock system produced a real reduction on muzzle rise. Put simply, the Type B recoiled on a more horizontal plane than most handguns we’ve tested. It kept the bore better aligned with the target and allowed for fast hits on target.
The combination of the low bore axis and the optic mounting system also had the effect of positioning the red-­dot sight on a lower plane than with other handguns. For those slower to master a red-­dot sight, the Type B made using a reflex sight more intuitive.
Being a relatively new handgun, it is impressive that there are already several holsters available. We evaluated the Type B in a Blackpoint Tactical OWB holster range work, and within a few rounds we were able to draw, acquire the target and make fast hits. The gun pointed well and the controls were all in familiar positions.
Accuracy was good with each of the three load, as well. We want handgun accuracy to average less than 21/2 inches at 25 yards for a five-­shot group. The Type B easily exceeded G&A’s benchmark, most notably with Federal Premium 124-­grain Punch, a defensive load. Reliability was 100 percent for all loads tested, too.

Controls are within reach. The slide lock is shaped to be pressed down as a slide release, and the mag release is reversible. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Shootability came down to three factors: trigger, gripping surface and sights. The trigger was good for a striker-­fire handgun. The user-­configurable grip fills most hands and supports leverage over muzzle rise; once configured though, you’ll likely never change the backstraps again. The sights are fine for being a red fiber-­optic and a black serrated notch, but at least they’re still and mounted in a Glock pattern. The Acro P-­2 enhanced the performance of the Type B overall as red-­dot users would expect.
G&A’s staff loved the way the Type B shot. We appreciated the innovative operating system, and were happy by the forward thinking in allowing it to accept interchangeable parts such as the grip module, threaded barrel, grip texture, sights, and so on.
It is not often that we get to evaluate a firearm that stands apart in the polymer-­frame, striker-­fire 9mm pistol category, but the Archon Firearms Type B certainly does. Whether it catches on as a defensive sidearm or a competition pistol, it certainly has the capability to win in either environment.
