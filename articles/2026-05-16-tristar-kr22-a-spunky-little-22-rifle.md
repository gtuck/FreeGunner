TriStar KR.22: A Spunky Little .22 Rifle

TriStar KR.22 is an affordable rimfire rifle that's great for plinking tin cans, hunting small game, and competing in fast-action steel plate. 

 The KR.22 handles as light as a feather and a speedy shooter. (Steve Gash photo)

May 16, 2026

By Steve Gash

Often, it’s said that you can’t have too many .22 rimfire guns. They are available in myriad forms and at widely disparate prices, from beginner rifles to refined, super-accurate match guns. This cascade got started in about 1845 by a Frenchman named Louis-Nicolas Flobert. He created the first rimfire cartridge, and at the Great Exhibition in London in 1851, Flobert exhibited a small .22-caliber rifle. Smith & Wesson patented the cartridge on August 8, 1854, and the company’s first revolver chambered for it was introduced in 1857. That ancient cartridge is still in use today as the .22 Short.
The round had features that persist to this day. The hollow rim held a dry primer mixture, and the propellant was a measly 4 grains of fine blackpowder. Like today’s .22 Shorts, it was loaded with a 29-grain bullet with a tapered heel that fit inside the case, the bullet was crimped onto the case, and the exposed bullet was outside lubricated. In about 1887, the .22 Short was updated with smokeless powder. The hollow rim with a spun-in wet primer brought together the .22 rimfire that persists today. However, one design feature of the rimfire cartridge is the fact that the bullet has a very tapered base onto which the case is crimped. This base can vary, and it can be damaged when the crimp is applied. None of this helps accuracy. These bullets are also outside lubricated.
But even back in those good old days, bigger was better. In about 1871, the .22 Long appeared. It also featured a 29-grain bullet but in a longer case at a higher velocity. This longer case was loaded with a 40-grain bullet in 1887 as the .22 Long Rifle (LR) we all know and love today. Numerous additional forms of .22 rimfires appeared later, but the good old .22 LR soldiers on.
Today’s .22 rimfire rifles offer just about anything a shooter could want. Many are inexpensive. A staple of the .22 rimfire world is the low-cost plinker rifle. Costs are kept down by the use of synthetic stocks (which, we must admit, have improved by leaps and bounds since the ghastly early ones) and by having the rifles made overseas. For example, Turkey has a modern and sophisticated firearms industry that serves both military and foreign commercial customers. A prime example is Kral Arms in central Turkey, and it has a modern, 19,000+ square-foot factory, where more than 250 skilled workers make a wide variety of quality firearms.

Enter the KR.22
One of the newest products to come from Kral is the KR.22 semiautomatic .22 LR rifle that is imported by TriStar Arms in North Kansas City, Missouri. This sleek little .22 has an attractive and functional synthetic stock, an 18.75-inch barrel, and a built in optic rail on the molded synthetic receiver. A great feature is the fact that the KR.22 comes with two 10-round rotary magazines, and the rifle uses readily available Ruger 10/22 magazines. Ruger sells a neat three-pack of 10/22 magazines that fit right in the KR.22.
The new KR.22 is a lightweight plinker with a proven blowback action. As I just said, the barrel is 18.75 inches long, and it has the .22’s standard twist of one turn in 16 inches. The muzzle is threaded 1/2-28, so if you want to install a suppressor, you can very easily.
The little KR.22 weighs a mere 4.83 pounds; with the scope and mount I used, it tipped the scale at 5.94 pounds. It handles as light as a feather. The stock has a length of pull of 13.38 inches.

Rimfire Rifle Features
The receiver just holds the parts, and it is made of a tough, black synthetic material. The multi-slot optic rail molded into its top makes mounting a scope or a red-dot sight a snap. Open sights also are provided. The rudimentary rear sight is a square notch pinned into the rear of the optic rail. The front sight is a red fiber-optic rod on a ramp at the muzzle. The fiber-optic rod is pinned in, and it appears to be replaceable. Also, it looks like the front sight ramp can be removed by unscrewing two front sight screws.
The stock has sling-swivel studs installed in typical front and rear locations, and the cheekpiece is easily adjustable for height. Just loosen one hex screw and adjust away. The stock has molded-in gripping surfaces on the pistol grip and the fore-end.
A super-neat feature of the KR.22’s action is the reversible cocking handle. If you’d like it to be on the left side, just unscrew it from the right side and reinstall it in the provided threaded hole on the left side. This is handy even for right-handed shooters.
A two-position crossbolt safety is located in front of the trigger guard, and the trigger proper has the now familiar trigger safety lever that must be pressed in as you squeeze the trigger. Operation is as natural as falling off a log, but the pull weight on my sample varied from about 7 to 12 pounds, depending on where I placed my finger pad. For close-range plinking and fast matches, it does just fine.
The owner’s manual has detailed instructions for how to take the rifle apart down to the last nut and bolt, and the exploded parts diagram lists 76 parts, so I passed on doing that.

Rimfire Shooting Performance
I have recently been lured into shooting local informal “Steel Challenge” matches. These fun events are shot with .22 rimfire guns—no long hours at the loading bench, no expensive powders and bullets, just mount a red-dot optic, load up with 
.22 LR ammo
, and go have fun. The shooting is fast—and fun! Our matches require five magazines, so with the aforementioned Ruger three-pack and the two magazines that came with the KR.22, I am all set. As soon as the weather is conducive, I’ll be giving the KR.22 a serious workout on those steel plates.
In the meantime, I recently spent quite a bit of time checking out the KR.22 at my shooting range. I tested the new rifle for accuracy with a Burris 2-7X 35mm Droptine scope mounted on the integral rail. This scope is designed for the .22 LR and has half-MOA click adjustments. I’ll likely use a Bushnell RXS-100 red-dot sight for the matches.
I fired five-shot groups from a Lead Sled DRT rest at targets placed at 25 yards. Bullet velocities were measured with an Oehler Model 35P chronograph. As is typical of .22 rimfire ammo, there were some really wide swings in velocities. In addition, like a lot of .22 rimfire rifles, the little KR.22 was a somewhat picky eater. It liked some loads and disliked others, but with the great variety of .22 LR ammo available these days, finding one that your rifle likes is part of the fun.
I partitioned the ammo for testing with the scope into two groups. Just for the heck of it, I shot several high-dollar match loads and compared them to what I called “everyday loads.” The performance of both groups was illustrative.
Some of the match loads had really low velocities and blew the breechblock back just far enough to eject the fired case but not far enough to cock the hammer, so the next trigger squeeze was a no go. Also, some of the match loads were reluctant to enter the chamber and jammed. Then there were the occasional misfires, never mind that the KR.22’s firing pin put a healthy dent in the cartridge rims. Most of these went off on the second “whack.” Obviously, these occurrences were ammo related. This is only of academic interest, as we’re not going got use such expensive loads in this rifle anyway. Interestingly, the average group size of the match loads was slightly larger than that for the everyday loads.
Such results were recently explained by writer Gil Sengel. He noted that match chamber dimensions are critical to .22 accuracy. Match guns will shoot almost any load accurately, but if a load doesn’t shoot well in the match gun, it is unlikely that it will shoot well in any gun. It is unrealistic to expect an excellent but inexpensive rifle like the KR.22 to have match chamber dimensions. This means that buying $20-a-box match ammo will not improve the group sizes of standard-production guns.
At 25 yards, the everyday loads averaged 0.78 inch, whereas the match loads averaged 0.83 inch for five-shot groups. (Steve Gash photo)
This was proven with the results with the everyday loads. They had higher velocities and functioned better, and the overall group average was slightly smaller than for the match loads. The groups certainly were adequate for the rifle’s intended purpose—shooting fun. Plus, the average group sizes of most of the everyday loads were well within minute-of-steel-plate at 10 yards.
A chronograph is essential for load selection, as we want to pick ammo that not only shoots well, but also has a low standard deviation (S.D.). Another metric I like to compare is the coefficient of variation (COV). This is the S.D. expressed as a percent of the average velocity. Obviously, lower is better. A rough index of ammo accuracy is the group size times the S.D.; again, lower is better. If one factor is more important to you, weigh that factor by multiplying it times 10 (or 100), then times the other factor.

Here are the results, by the numbers. The average for the seven match loads for five-shot groups was 0.83 inch, S.D. was 20, COV was 5.34, and accuracy index was 14.0. The averages for the 11 everyday loads were 0.78 inch for group size, 17 for S.D., 3.72 for COV, and 13.9 for accuracy index.
Accuracy and other statistics varied all over the place, but several stood out as potential plate ringers. First of all, I eliminated the match loads because 100 percent reliability is essential; a misfire, and you’re done for in such competitions. The lower-velocity everyday loads were suspect too, for the same reliability issue.
A longtime favorite .22 LR load of mine is the Winchester 40-grain Super-X High Velocity. It did well, as did the Remington Ranch Hand 40-grain HP. And I have to mention Federal’s Champion 36-grain CPHP “Bring Your Own Bottle” ammo because the KR.22 liked it, I have a lot of it, and it never hiccuped once.
There are a lot of fine .22 LR rifles on the market. Some are better than others, and price and performance are the keys. At an MSRP of $289, the KR.22 certainly competes on price, and with the proper ammo, it can serve well for tin cans, small game, and steel plates.
