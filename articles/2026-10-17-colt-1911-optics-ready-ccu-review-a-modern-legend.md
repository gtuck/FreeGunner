Colt 1911 Optics-Ready CCU Review: A Modern Legend

Bringing John Browning's iconic design into the modern optics era.

 Colt has a new line of 1911s called Optics-Ready; this is an Optics-Ready CCU Government. (Robert Sadowski photo)

October 17, 2026

By Robert Sadowski

Colt wasn’t the first to offer an optics-ready 1911, but as the original creator of the platform, their adoption carries weight. With the new Optics-Ready CCU Government, the legendary maker brings a feature-rich classic squarely into the 21st century.
Out-of-the-Box Quality
Out of the box, the Optics-Ready CCU Government felt good in hand. CCU stands for Colt Combat Unit in case you were wondering. It is a Series 80 1911, which means the pistol is equipped with a firing pin block making the pistol drop-safe. My sample arrived in .45 Auto and uses a GI-style muzzle bushing. I prefer this type of setup because you can field strip the pistol without tools. The CCU also uses two nested recoil springs, which help with felt recoil. Colt tricks out the CCU 1911 family with features that make shooting a 1911 faster and easier.
Adding a red dot to the CCU Government tightened up groups and made the full size 1911 easier to shoot. (Robert Sadowski photo)
The slide is the star of this Optics-Ready CCU. The slide is domed like traditional Colt 1911s, and the optic cut swipes off a layer of metal at the rear of the slide offering a flat surface to install an optic plate. Optic plates are sold separately and are available from Brownells.com and MidwayUSA.com. Plates are compatible with Leupold DeltaPoint, Shield RMSc, Holosun 507k series optics, and Trijicon RMR. Installing the plate and optics is simple. No real mechanical ability needed except knowledge of what end of the hex wrench to use.
The cover plate incorporates the rear sight which is dovetailed into the plate. The rear sight is flush with the rear of the slide for maximum sight radius, and the shooter facing side is serrated to reduce glare. It has a large square-shaped notch to drop in the Night Fision tritium dot on the front sight post. I like these iron sights. The front sight pops and the ears of the rear sight are chamfered so your eye naturally goes to the notch. The rear sight can be removed from the cover plate and tapped into the optic plate in the event you want back up iron sights to complement your optic.

I mounted one of my go-to red dots, a Trijicon RMR, to the Colt. I can’t count how many pistols, shotguns, and carbines I have used this sight on. I particularly like the Trijicon RMR for its durability, larger unobstructed field of view, and it is constantly on 24/7. Battery life is two years with typical use, such as conceal carry. Another feature about the RMR is that the adjustment screws do not need a special tool. Use the rim on a fired case to adjust windage and elevation.
The rest of the slide wears course, angled front and rear serrations that offer plenty of grasping surface to rack the slide or do a press check.
The front strap wears 25-LPI checkering that provides an excellent grip even with harder recoiling .45 Auto. The trigger guard is also undercut for a slightly higher grip and better recoil management. (Robert Sadowski photo)
Grip Features
The frame and grip safety are nicely blended and smooth with no sharp edges. The grip safety features a decent speed bump to deactivate the grip safety once the pistol is grasped. The beavertail flares up nicely with no worries about slide or hammer bite. The front grip strap wears a toothy 25-lpi (lines per inch) checkering that allows for a sure grip even under recoil. The flat mainspring housing also wears the same checkering. In a 1911 chambered in 9mm and .45 Auto, texture on the front strap is mandatory in my opinion. This texturing offers a lot of adhesion without any of abrasion especially after running boxes of ammo through the pistol. The trigger guard is also under cut for a slightly higher grip and better control. These small features all work together allowed me to better run the optic-ready Colt.

Ambidextrous thumb safety paddles are sized just right and flick on and off without having to adjust your grip. The low inertia or skeletonized hammer has plenty of serrations for sure, no-slip cocking. The long trigger is solid and sports serrations on the trigger face. The magazine release is checkered and protrudes just enough for conceal carry.
The magwell makes loading skinny single-stack magazines faster and smoother. (Robert Sadowski photo)
The magazine well is equipped with a funnel that makes it easier and faster to insert skinny single-stack magazines into the pistol. It takes finesse to quickly reload a single stack 1911 and the magwell funnels allows you to quickly reload. I particularly liked this feature.
Range Time
First, I set up an IDPA target at 10 yards and used it to zero the RMR. It also allowed me to get to know the Optics-Ready CCU. It was pretty much like running into an old pal. I ran both training and defense ammo. Of the training ammunition, I used Browning FMJ with a 185-grain FMJ bullet, Remington UMC with a 230-grain bullet, and inexpensive Blazer aluminum case with a 230-grain bullet. The defense load was Winchester Silvertip with a 185-grain JHP. The rim of a fired .45 Auto case was used to dial in the Trijicon. I wasn’t pushing myself with the Colt because I wanted to see how it would perform. A bunch of different 1911 magazines were used—Wilson Combat, Chip McCormick, and other aftermarket magazines. All ran without any issues.
I next moved the target out to 25 yards to see what type of accuracy I could squeeze out of the Colt CCU. I soon found the Colt had a preference for 185-grain ammo producing average 5-shot groups that measured 1.67 inches with the Browning and 1.75 inches with the Winchester. With 230-grain ammo it offered decent 5-shot groups; 2.39 inches with Remington and 2.65 inches with Blazer. The Colt is a real shooter. While shooting from a rest provides potential accuracy, the real deal is shooting for speed.
CCU models are equipped with an easy-to-remove, GI-style muzzle bushing. (Robert Sadowski photo)
I ran few BAER Solutions Standard Drills. Ideally, you shoot this 13-round drill cold with 10 rounds in one magazine and 3 in the other. You start with 5 rounds in the left rectangle and transition to the right rectangle with 5 more rounds. You do this as fast as possible. When the slide locks back, reload the 3 round magazine and place 3 holes in the center circle. The Colt CCU chambered in .45 Auto holds 8 rounds, so I loaded up eight rounds in one magazine and 3 in another. I ran this drill at 7 yards. You can time yourself if you have a timer. I like this drill because it forces you to drive fast then slow down for precision shots. The Colt ran great. No hiccups. No glitches. The pistol cycles nicely and the textured grips make the pistol easy to control even when slinging big bore .45 Auto lead. The thumb groove in the G10 grips allowed me to reach the magazine catch faster and smoother. I ran the slide release and used the Trijicon as a handle to cock the Colt. The optic mount is a rugged setup and keeps the optic sight low on the slide.
