Henry H16 Golden Boy Deadeye Review: Best Modern Old West Plinker?

A steel-and-brass, 36-ounce rimfire wheel gun built for precision, cowboys, and pure backyard fun.

 The two Henry H16 Golden Boy Deadeye revolvers differ only in the grip shape. Top, the Birdshead, bottom, the Gunfighter. (Patrick Sweeney photo)

August 19, 2026

By Patrick Sweeney

Some things are so American that they are self-explanatory. While revolvers have been designed and used globally, they became truly defined in America. Henry knows this heritage and paired their line of American-made H16 Golden Boy Deadeye double-action revolvers with its iconic American-made lever-action rifles.
The Deadeye models of the H16 Golden Boy feature adjustable rear sights. (Patrick Sweeney photo)
Features
Starting with an all-steel frame, barrel and ten-shot cylinder, the H16 Golden Boy Deadeye revolvers offer all the modern touches that new shooters expect. The sights are adjustable, and the front is a red fiber optic blade. Shooters old and new respect nods to tradition, but they also expect modern details. To make the revolvers easy to shoot, the front sight is a red fiber optic while the rear is adjustable. That means that your H16 Golden Boy Deadeye can be dialed-in and not require “Kentucky windage” to get hits on target. The four-inch barrel provides a long enough of a sight radius for easy aiming without making the revolver muzzle heavy or too large for a new shooter to handle.

These revolvers employ a swing-out cylinder which quickly ejects all empties simultaneously and makes reloading fast enough to keep new shooters in the loop. Old West single-action revolvers were loaded and emptied a single round at a time which can be tedious. Opening the action on the Henry revolvers is as simple as pressing the latch on the left side forward and pivoting the cylinder out of the frame on its crane.

Double Action
The H16 Golden Boy Deadeye revolvers are a double action. The terminology might seem odd to new shooters, because “double action” means you can shoot your Henry revolvers both double and single action. By thumb-cocking the hammer, the trigger gets staged into single action. This is good for new shooters because they can learn to aim and get good results with a relatively light and short trigger pull. Once they have a handle on it, they can then advance to double-action trigger, that is simply pressing the trigger to fire.
The steel trigger is inside of the brass trigger guard which is part of the brass frame. The frame holds the grips, and the brass is a nod to Henry lever-action rifles. The first lever-action rifles had brass frames, “gun metal” was the term used for it, and Henry continues that tradition today. However, unlike the soft brass of the 19
th
 century, Henry uses alloys fully up to the task of being components of a firearm. And just like the rifle/handgun combinations of the Old West, your Henry H16 Golden Boy Deadeye revolvers use the same ammunition as your Henry H4 Golden Boy rifle in .22LR.
Both the Gunfighter and the Birdshead grips frames are made out of brass. The American walnut grips are marked with the Henry logo. (Patrick Sweeney photo)
Grips
The H16 Golden Boy Deadeye's are offered in two models, both in .22LR, with one difference: the grip shape. One utilizes the Gunfighter grip and the other a Birdshead grip. The Gunfighter is the shape you’d expect if you think of a single action revolver used in the Old West by, yes, a gunfighter. Tapering up, the grip design allows the revolver when chambered in larger calibers to roll up in the shooting hand when fired, soaking up recoil, and bringing the hammer closer to the hand to be cocked to fire again. But as much as Hollywood has cemented that image in our heads, a lot of handguns in the old west were made with the Birdshead grip. This shape curves the backstrap down at the bottom and forward, instead of flaring out like the Gunfighter. Many shooters find the Birdshead more comfortable in their hand than the Gunfighter.

Now, there’s not really any recoil to speak of with a .22LR handgun, especially an all-steel one like the H16 Golden Boy Deadeye. Tipping the scales at 36 ounces, the heft of the revolver is comforting enough that even a new shooter will feel like this is a real firearm.
Fully Loaded
Now, one knock on the Old West six-shooters was that they were actually five-shooters if you wanted to handle and carry them safely. The 19th-century single action had to be carried with the hammer down on an empty chamber. If you fully loaded it with the hammer down, the firing pin would rest directly on the cartridge primer. One “oops” and a cowboy of the old west would be walking the rest of the time with a limp, having inadvertently shot himself in the leg or foot. There is no worry about that with these Henry revolvers. The firing mechanism uses what is called a transfer bar. A transfer bar takes the impact of the hammer and transfers it to the firing pin. It gets in between them only when you press the trigger. If you do not pull the trigger, the transfer bar cannot be in between the hammer and firing pin. So, you can safely carry any of the Henry H16 Golden Boy Deadeye .22LRs with all ten chambers holding a live round.

Yes, that’s another big reason the Henry H16 Golden Boy Deadeye can be so much fun, and such a useful tool to teach shooting. In this day and age of modern high-capacity magazines, a pistol can hold fifteen, twenty or twenty-five rounds of ammunition. Anyone who has spent more than five minutes looking into shooting knows that and expecting your new shooter to be enthused with “just six rounds?” is setting them up for disappointment.
Build Quality
I learned to shoot with a double action .22LR revolver, it was made in Germany. You might expect that I still have it. Alas, no, because not everything made in Germany is an epic feat of engineering. This one was inexpensive and lightly constructed. I wore it out learning how to shoot. Had it been built as robustly as the Henry H16 Golden Boy revolvers, I’d still have it, and it would work just as expected. In fact, when I pulled the two Henry H16 Golden Boy Deadeyes out of their boxes, my first thought was “These are made well enough to be chambered in .38 Special”. And that is the case, as Henry also makes the H16 Golden Boy revolvers in centerfire. Called the H17 Big Boy Deadeye, those two (with the same Gunfighter and Birdshead grips) are chambered in .38 Special/.357 Magnum.
And just to round out the line, both the H16 Golden Boy Deadeye and H17 Big Boy Deadeye revolvers can be had in a fixed-sight version.

Final Thoughts
The question you might be asking is: which of the two grips is the better one? When planning range sessions with the two H16 Golden Boy Deadeye revolvers, I fully expected to prefer—and shoot better with—the Gunfighter grip. Strangely, whichever model I held became my instant favorite. It didn't matter how often I switched back and forth; the one in my hand was always the one I liked best. My advice is to find a gun shop with a rental range that carries Henry firearms and try them yourself. Fair warning: you’re likely to take one home, if not both.
