The Best Hybrid Rifle for Hunting or Competition

One Precision Rifle that Excels on a Hunt or in a Match

August 20, 2026

By Jack Hennessy

Tikka’s line of T3x rifles launched in 2016 as an upgraded overhaul to their original T3, which debuted in 2002. Within 3 years, 1 million Tikka T3x rifles had entered the market and the consensus among rifle shooters—both hunters, competitors, and recreational shooters alike—became as simple as, “The Tikka T3x rifle platform can just plain shoot.”
Tikka guarantees sub-MOA performance, which has become a fairly commonplace pledge from most reputable bolt-gun manufacturers. But for anyone who took time to put a shoulder behind a Tikka T3x, this platform was so much more than the redundant promise of “sub-MOA.”
In January 2025 at SHOT Show, Tikka introduced their line of T3x Ace rifles: Ace Game, Ace Target, alongside T1x Ace rimfire models. The T3x Ace Game was “designed for tactical precision shooting as well as hunting purposes.”
Despite listing tactical precision shooting first, it’s important to understand this is a hunting rifle firstly with the capabilities to secondarily serve as a match gun or bench-rest gun in a limited capacity. Weighing 8.8 pounds, it’s a little heavy for a lightweight hunting rifle, and a little light for a competition. Make no mistake, this rifle will anchor big game beautifully and shoot dutifully during the course of a full-day competitive precision-shooting match.

Background Behind Tikka T3x Rifles
The Tikka T3x rifle platform owes its roots to Tikkakoski, a Finnish company founded in 1893 that began making gun parts and barrels in the 1910s and 1930s. The company merged with Sako in the 1980s, when the brand launched the revolutionary T3 bolt-action rifle in 2002. Designed by Kari Kuparinen, the original Tikka T3 broke industry norms by using a single, uniform action length for all calibers, relying on variable bolt stops and magazines to reduce production costs without sacrificing tight tolerances.
The T3x, released in 2016, took things a step further by replacing the aluminum recoil lug with a steel one, adding a greater deal of modularity to the rifle’s stock and grip, while also redesigning the ejection port and adding a sturdier bolt shroud.
Luxury firearm manufacturer Sako builds Tikka rifles in its historic Finland factory. Both companies’ rifle barrels come off the same line, though Sako rifles come with a bit more premium bells and whistles while Tikka rifles are considered more-streamlined, with a less bespoke look to them.

The Tikka T3x Ace Game offers a free-floating, cold hammer-forged, medium contour barrel, which is designed to enhance accuracy and reduce vibration. Caliber options range from .223 Rem to .300 Win Mag. The barrel also features a 5/8”-24 muzzle thread.
The rifle comes with an adjustable rear stock, a customizable length of pull, cheek rest, and recoil pad adjustments. The skeletonized rear stock supports additional weights for increased stability. An adjustable, ambidextrous thumb rest sits above the grip. The standard pistol grip is adjustable in angle and position. The AR-compatible grip further enables customizable grip options. The Tikka T3x platform is well-known for its compatibility with an extensive selection of proprietary and third-party accessories, and the Ace Game is no different.
The adjustable, ambidextrous thumb rest provides a solid and repeatable platform for thumb-forward grips. (Jack Hennessy photo)
The rifle features an interchangeable magwell. The magazine release button is conveniently integrated into the trigger guard. The rifle includes a 20 MOA Picatinny rail and also comes equipped equipped with M-LOK and Arca-Swiss RRS® rails. A Cerakote finish is also an option for all SKUs (the model featured here for this review came Cerakoted in Midnight Bronze). The stock itself is designed to seamlessly integrate with T3, T3x, and T1x barreled actions.
Main Difference Between the Tikka T3x Ace Game and T3x Ace Target
It’s the barrel. The Ace Game, best described as a crossover rifle and comes equipped with a medium, fluted barrel, The flutes save weight but remain accurate over several rounds in short succession. The Ace Target sports a heavy, non-fluted barrel and clocks in at an extra pound and a half.
How does the difference affect extended-fire sessions? Both rifles can easily run NRL Hunter or PRS matches. The Ace Target’s extra pound-and-a-half is heavier to lug around. The Ace Game will struggle if a shooter is hoping to send hundreds of rounds downrange over the course of a day. The barrel will grow hot, throwing rounds, while groups will open up, making it more difficult to hit smaller targets at extended distances.

Sanctioned precision-rifle matches rarely exceed 120 rounds over a full day. NRL Hunter matches rarely exceed 80 rounds per day. The Ace Game handles that count and that workload just fine (and we tested this, but more on that later).

Accuracy Test
For testing, I added SilencerCo’s Scythe Ti suppressor tipped with their Bravo Anchor Brake. I secured Warne’s 7912M Lite Bipod to rifle’s ARCA rail and spent an afternoon testing various factory-loaded ammo as well as my own homebrew, which I developed ahead of the March 2026 NRL Hunter Caldwell Whitetail Madness match. Below are the results.
Across the board, 140-gr. bullets seem to be the sweet spot for this rifle’s 1:8 twist. My custom load edged out the Hornady 140-grain ELD Match by a .10-inch. The components for my custom load: Berger 140-gr. Hybrid Target bullets; Hodgdon H4350, 39.8 grains; Starline Brass LRP 6.5 Creedmoor brass; CCI large rifle primers; all loaded on Frankford Arsenal’s Precision Turret Press. It is important to note every rifle barrel is different—as unique as our own fingerprints. What rounds work the best for this very specific rifle may not work the best for your rifle, even the exact same model. Nevertheless, all this info is a great starting point.
At the end of testing, I loaded up two 5-round magazines with my custom load and the Hornady 140-gr. ELD Match ammo. The average group size between those two 5-shot groups was .53-inch.
A Note on Federal Premium +Peak
The ammo is something special with its PEAK alloy casing
, meaning the round can be loaded to a higher pressure, resulting in significantly higher velocities and more energy delivered on target. However, the 130-gr. Terminal Ascent bullet loaded in the ammo I received—combined with extra velocity shot through a 1:8 twist barrel—just did not produce great accuracy. I am confident a heavier bullet would stabilize better, and thus shoot better, through this Tikka T3x Ace Game. Federal has plenty of plans to load an assortment of bullets into their +PEAK ammo, as well as release unprimed cases for those looking to handload using the PEAK alloy.
Field Testing
While I tested this rifle outside of hunting season (and was not making it south for hogs), I did take it to an NRL Hunter match, specifically the March 2026 Caldwell Whitetail Classic in Atlanta, MO, hosted by Isaiah Curtis.
With all gear attached (Scythe Ti suppressor and Bravo Anchor Brake, Warne’s 7912M Lite Bipod, Vortex Razor HD LHT 4.5-22x50), the rifle weighed 12.3 pounds, easily making it under the 13-pound Factory division weight limit. That was without taking advantage of the 10-ounce suppressor credit (which would raise the total max weight of a Factory rifle to 13.5 pounds).
I never attached a sling. Didn’t feel I needed to. The rifle traveled well from stage to stage during some walks as long as a quarter mile. Then, at the start and during stages, I was able to seamlessly set up, get on target and transition, making hits on targets I was able to spot. Any missed targets (often ones I was never able to locate) or dropped shots were the result of the shooter, not the rifle.

At the end of the first day, on the final few stages, I felt I had gotten into a groove and walked away with more points—a major credit to this hunting-rifle-first after letting fly a little north of 40 rounds with really no notable amount of time to cool off between stages, especially as the day grew hotter in Missouri.
The skeletonized rear stock offers an adjustable cheek rest and supports additional weights should a user wish to change the overall balance of the rifle. (Jack Hennessy photo)
The only negative during field testing (and, really, the only con for this rifle): Both the buttstock and its pads kept coming lose during both days of competition. This also happened during ammo testing, further supporting the need for me to carry a T25 bit to secure end of buttstock and 1/8-in Allen key to fasten down spacers and recoil pad. Once tightened, everything buttstock-related was solid for another few dozen rounds.
In a competition setting, gear that requires constant maintenance can clutter your headspace and detract from performance on the clock. Still, seasoned hunters and competitors know that what can happen will happen—making on-person tools an absolute must. After arriving home, I applied Loctite to all the buttstock fasteners and thoroughly tightening them.
Final Overall Impressions
This is a rifle that can be taken out of the safe and enjoyed during every season. The more one practices with it, the more confidence a shooter will have that it will maintain its sub-MOA accuracy. That’s confidence building when it’s time to put a trophy whitetail in the dirt.
At its core, it’s a Tikka T3x—nothing incredibly special, because it doesn’t have to be. It’s reasonably engineered, and it’s reasonably priced, especially considering its dual-purpose capabilities.

The trigger is fine—again, nothing incredibly special, but still comfortable and crisp. It’s worth noting that I made zero adjustments to the factory setting, as I felt the trigger was perfectly fine as is, out of the box. The barrel is where these rifles really shine. The legacy of accuracy behind these rifles is proven and speaks for itself. My time with this rifle, including a dedicated ammo test, further proved what level of quality is coming out of the Sako factory in Riihimäki, Finland.
