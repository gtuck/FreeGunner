Smith & Wesson Performance Center M&P 10mm M2.0 Carry Comp: Reviewed

A generous barrel port compensator and strong control surfaces tames the 10mm for use in everyday carry. Here's a full Review

 (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)

November 09, 2026

By Guns & Ammo Staff

“Smith & Wesson Performance Center Military & Police 10mm M2.0 Carry Comp” is a mouthful. For the sake of brevity, G&A will refer to this gun as the “
10mm Carry Comp.
” Though it is essentially a line extension to the full-­size M&P9 M2.0, its 10mm chambering is inherently attractive to some on our staff since the cartridge’s pedigree traces back to our most famous handgun editor, Lt. Col. Jeff Cooper. In his words from Guns & Ammo’s January 1987 issue, “The 10, using a proper bullet shape, is theoretically slightly superior to the .45 in stopping power, and demonstrably superior in reach.” With modern defensive ammo, the 10mm has more effectiveness than most .45 ACP loads Cooper had access to in 1987.
Smith & Wesson’s PowerPort compensator is cut into the barrel. Gases escape through it and through the cut atop the slide. It’s noticeably effective at leveling the slide during cycling when using high-velocity, light-for-caliber ammuntion. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Choosing a 10mm handgun for everyday carry (EDC) is no small decision — particularly for concealment. The 10mm is more dramatic than the 9mm in recoil impulse and muzzle flip, so quick, efficient, follow-­up shots and target transitions are what make it more challenging to shoot and carry. Typically, larger-­framed individuals can more easily carry the 10mm Automatic since they have more mass to help control and conceal what are usually full-­size handguns. Few compact 10mm pistols are available.
The Smith & Wesson C.O.R.E. system covers most available optic footprints. G&A noted that the Leupold DeltaPoint Pro does not co-witness with the factory sight configuration. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Enter Smith & Wesson’s 10mm Carry Comp. It would be considered a “full-­size” pistol for a 9mm, but it’s arguably compact when compared to other 10mm autos. It is also one of the few handguns that can justify an integral compensator. Comps require greater velocity to be effective, whereas it’s unlikely that many comp’d sub-­4-­inch-­barrel 9mm EDC pistols achieve the velocity necessary for effect. Black Hills Ammunition’s 115-­grain HoneyBadger realized a velocity greater that 1,500 feet per second (fps) out of the 4-­inch 10mm Carry Comp during our evaluation. The benefits of Smith & Wesson’s integral PowerPort compensator were immediately noticeable.
Easy Exchange
The 10mm Carry Comp has more features of note that make it appealing to current M&P9 2.0 owners interested in trying a 10. The 10mm Carry Comp shares almost identical slide dimensions to the full-­size M&P9 M2.0 models. G&A confirmed the 10mm Carry Comp fitment with several holsters designed for the full-­size M&P9 M2.0, including those by Crossbreed Holsters, Fobus and Blackpoint Tactical. It’s likely that most full-­size M&P9 2.0 holsters will also work with this gun, the only thing changing to daily carry the 10mm Carry Comp being the cartridge. The 10mm Carry Comp offers essentially the same attributes as the M&P9 M2.0, and the manual of arms is uniform.

Three additional grip panels were included with the 10mm Carry Comp. They are exchanged in the same manner as other M&P models, filling a grip’s void and countering recoil energy. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
For non-­M&P users, the 10mm Carry Comp is a great platform to evaluate the 10mm cartridge, and it possesses most of the modern characteristics we’ve come to expect on an EDC pistol. For example, the 10mm Carry Comp is optic ready and comes with a plastic slide cover mounted in place. In the cardboard box there is a baggie marked “CORE” containing six optic plates — one metal, five plastic — that help to make the shallow optic cut compatible with most compact and micro-­compact reflex sight footprints. However, any mounted optic is going to sit higher than the factory iron sights and will not likely co-­witness with the dot. Smith & Wesson enjoys an extensive aftermarket for suppressor-­height sights, though it’s worth considering using the standard tritium night sights factory installed on the 10mm Carry Comp. (They are marked “USAH3 PX,” which are manufactured by AmeriGlo.) They were easy to acquire and faster for us to employ than a red dot from concealment at close range.
Should you prefer a red dot, though, the S&W ClearSight slide design was effective in diverting gases away from the 
Leupold DeltaPoint Pro 
 used for accuracy trials. Across the 100 rounds used for documenting accuracy potential, the objective lens of the DeltaPoint Pro remained clean with no visual occlusion from dirty accumulation. The 10mm Carry Comp produced no malfunctions or reliability concerns during G&A’s evaluation.
The trigger and safety lever are made of polymer. Overtravel is stopped by a bump on the frame. The disassembly lever and slide lock/release are serrated, and the mag release is textured. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The grip module was consistent in terms of quality for the M&P M2.0 models, and the grip texture was distinctly functional in aiding control through each recoil impulse. The M2.0 sandpaper-­like abrasiveness secured the 10mm Carry Comp in our hands.

The striker-­fire trigger was unaffected during recoil control. It measured a respectable 3 pounds, 11 ounces, and the release point was repeatable. It’s light enough for accuracy, but not too light that the shooter might experience an unintended discharge due to its recoil. Knowing exactly when the trigger releases the striker helped prevent flinching, and the sub-­4-­pound trigger made it possible to predictably shoot targets at distance, which is one of the great advantages to using a 10mm pistol. The 10mm Auto round maintains more energy than a 9mm and .45 ACP beyond 50 yards.
More Than Speed
In short, the 10mm Carry Comp was an objectively reliable, quality handgun. To use a hypophora, the question most will have is why carry a 10mm pistol at all? The 10mm Auto is the handgun cartridge for those capable of carrying and managing the performance of a high-­energy load. Velocity from a sub-­4-­inch 9mm pistol typically maxed out at 1,000 fps. For a 115-­grain full metal jacket (FMJ) load, the kinetic energy measured was roughly 255 foot-­pounds (ft.-lbs.). A 124- to 135-­grain defensive bullet should produce between 275 and 300 ft.-lbs.
The tritium front day/night sight and serrated rear sights are superb. The front iron sight is located behind the comp, preventing visual obstruction. To aim, nest it in the rear U-notch. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Kinetic energy for the loads tested through the 10mm Carry Comp started at 335 ft.-lbs. Remember the 115-­grain Honey Badger that clocked at more than 1,500 fps? The 10mm round’s kinetic energy measured just more than 600 ft.-lbs., similar to a .357 Magnum shot from a 4-­inch barrel. The difference, though, is two 15-­round magazines instead of only six rounds and a slower reload. That’s a lot of energy potential. Of course, such energy has a tradeoff. 
There’s no escaping the recoil impulse of a 10mm being fired, but it can be mitigated. The 10mm Carry Comp features an integral compensator that reduces the muzzle flip to that of a comparable 9mm M&P. The felt recoil was still there, but it was less of a factor when getting the sights back on target. It was even more beneficial when shooting the pistol with a red-­dot sight. Without the comp, even highly experienced handgun shooters among us occasionally lost the dot.
Even after several range sessions, the 10mm Carry Comp was not excessively dirty. The dissassembly process mirrors other M&P and is similar to popular striker-fire pistols. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The comp was most effective with the faster Hornady American Gunner 155-­grain XTP load measured at 1,294 fps and Black Hills’ 115-­grain HoneyBadger that was recorded at 1,535 fps, respectively. The 180-­grain Fiocchi Range Dynamics FMJ load had a velocity of 1,155 fps. The comp produced a controlled advantage for the heavier FMJ round, but to a lesser degree. Time splits when shooting double-­taps and transitioning between targets were no different when compared to the M&P9 because the muzzle flip was so greatly reduced.

For testing and carry, 
a SureFire XC3
 light was attached to the three-­slot rail. It only added 2.6 ounces of weight, but even that little bit reduced muzzle rise. Regarding weight — with a full magazine plus light and red dot mounted — the 10mm Carry Comp was 2 pounds, 8 ounces. Managing Editor Jack Oller concealed and carried this gun as his EDC (sans optic) for more than 60 days. Oller reported that the weight was more comfortable than uncomfortable when worn with a quality belt and holster, usually 
Crossbreed’s Lightguard
. With a relatively loose shirt, the 10mm Carry Comp hardly printed on his 6-­foot-­4-­inch frame at all. A t-­shirt concealed the 10mm Carry Comp at an acceptable level.
Two 15-round magazines plus one in the chamber equates 31 rounds of 10mm available for everyday carry (EDC). Additional 10mm mags are available from the S&W website. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Parting Shots
Realizing the performance benefit, it’s hard to go back to carrying a 9mm when a familiar-handling 10mm handgun is just as easy to carry. We compared pricing for both training and defensive loads and found that ammunition costs were not substantially more than 9mm within the same production lines, either. When loaded with Hornady’s American Gunner and Black Hills’ HoneyBadger loads, the 10mm Carry Comp was just as easy to shoot as similar full-­size M&P 9mm models. There is a confidence that comes with carrying a 10mm that you know you can shoot well. The Smith & Wesson Performance M&P 10mm M2.0 Carry Comp is one that offers said confidence to more shooters than ever before. 
Forward slide serrations are useful for press-checks of the chamber while adding a pleasing aesthetic. Two slots on the sides of the dustcover reveal the full stainless-steel chassis. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
