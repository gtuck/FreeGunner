Inside the Savage Arms Factory: How They Build Hunting Rifles

An exclusive walkthrough of the manufacturing process behind America's go-to hunting rifles.

 A group of writers with their assembled rifles and Savage Arms employees. (Photo courtesy of Brad Fitzpatrick)

September 28, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

Savage Arms is a familiar name to hunters and shooters. For over a century, Savage has been manufacturing guns for almost every shooting discipline, and these guns have earned an impressive reputation for accuracy and durability. I suspect that most hunters and shooters have, at one time, owned a Savage product.
Recently, I had the opportunity to travel to Westfield, Massachusetts where I visited the Savage factory and met the people who make Savage firearms every day. The employees in Westfield are unmistakably proud about the products they produce, and they should be. Savage rimfire and centerfire rifles are known for their accuracy, and the Model 110 is the longest continually produced American bolt-action rifle.
Savage’s reputation was largely built on their rifles, from the Model 99 to today’s 110 and Axis 2 centerfire rifles and rimfire like the Revel, A22 and B22. However, Savage’s product catalog extends well beyond their rifles. Today, Savage offers shotguns and handguns as well. No matter the discipline, Savage offers a firearm to fit every shooter’s bill.
How has Savage maintained such an impressive reputation for accuracy and performance, especially at a time when other gun manufacturers have faltered? The answer to that question lies behind the company’s doors in Westfield.

A Brief History of Savage Arms
Firearms designer Arthur Savage established Savage Arms in Utica, New York in 1894. When Savage set up shop in New York they were virtually next door to some of the top firearms manufacturers in the world, a list that included Colt, Smith & Wesson, Springfield Armory, Winchester Repeating Arms, and others. The competition among firearms manufacturers was fierce, and Savage Arms would only survive if the company was able to manufacture products that competed against the biggest names in the gun business.
The mid- to late nineteenth century was a time of unmatched innovation in firearms and ammunition manufacturing. Arsenals that included muzzleloading muskets in the 1850s were replaced by automatic weapons in the early 1900s. Arthur Savage stepped onto the firearm stage at this revolutionary time, and Savage developed some of the period’s most popular designs. His 1892 hammerless rifle design was considered for a U.S. military contract, eventually losing out to the Krag-Jorgensen. In 1895 he redesigned that hammerless lever gun which featured an innovative rotary magazine that allowed the use of pointed bullets. Four years later, the 1895 was revised into the Savage Model 1899, which would become better known as the Model 99. That rifle cemented Arthur Savage’s reputation as a firearms designer and remained in production for eight decades.
Savage wasn’t simply focused on rifles, though. One of the finalists in the U.S. Military’s sidearm trail was Savage’s single-action autoloader, which was produced in conjunction with designer Elbert Searle. The pistol did not win the contract (Browning’s design, which would eventually become the M1911, won). But by that time Arthur Savage had moved on from his namesake brand to grow oranges and sell ties in California.

In 1915, Charles Newtown developed the 
.250-3000 Savage (known more commonly as the .250 Savage) 
which became the first centerfire rifle to break the 3,000 feet per second barrier. The .300 Savage followed in 1920, and that same year Savage purchased Stevens Arms in Chicopee Falls, Massachusetts. Savage manufactured military weapons during both World Wars (Including the Thompson submachine gun and both .30 and .50 caliber Browning machine guns), and in 1946 moved their operations to the Stevens plant in Chicopee Falls. In 1960 the company moved again, this time to Westfield. While other companies have ditched New England for politically greener pastures elsewhere, Savage has stayed put in Massachusetts for 65 years.
A Savage Arms employee cleaning a part. (Photo courtesy of Brad Fitzpatrick)
Today’s Savage
Savage currently employs around 300 people at their Westfield facility, and those employees fulfill vital roles in the manufacturing and shipping of Savage firearms. That list of tasks includes everything from warehouse technicians to quality control experts, assemblers, machine operators, and customer service professionals. Many of Savage’s employees have been there for decades.
There’s an unmistakable sense of camaraderie and pride at Savage as well as a sense of pride in their products and a loyalty to their customers. My tour started on the factory floor, the backdrop of which is a massive American flag. My guides that day were Beth Shimanski, Savage's vice president of marketing, and Wayne Kratochvil, the company's senior materials supervisor. Kratochvil is another long timer at Savage, having worked at the company for 42 years. He’s quick to point out, however, that there are other employees who have been with the brand for over a half-century.
We began our tour where every Savage firearm begins—the materials area. Here steel, polymer, aluminum, and other materials are ferried to points throughout the factory where they will become stocks, receiver, barrels, slides, magazines, and other components.
One of the most interesting aspects of Savage production is the seamless blending of modern technology with classic American machine tools. There are bays of CNC machines alongside early twentieth-century milling machines that only one or two people in the world truly know how to run and repair. Every Savage firearm starts life inside high-tech, precision cutting machines as well as classic equipment from an era when Savage and other gun companies were churning out weapons for World Wars.

Hands on Assembly
Rather than simply walking through the company and watching workers ply their trades, I had an opportunity to actually build a rifle with the help of Savage employees. The gun came to me as a collection of loose parts on a rolling cart—a stock, trigger components, bolt, carbon fiber barrel, pins, and others—and I would have to try to piece together a Savage rifle from scratch.
I’ve shot, cleaned, assembled, and disassembled firearms for most of my life, but I’ve never pieced every component of a rifle together from scratch. I began by building the bolt, although I deserve none of the credit. I was helped through most every step of the process by a wonderfully patient employee, and under her tutelage I inserted the firing pin, spring, and pins and then tested the function of the bolt. Next came the addition of the carbon fiber barrel, and as I was preparing to install it I was taught the process by which Savage ensures that every barrel that leaves their rifle is perfectly straight. There’s no high-tech equipment but rather a single employee and a beam of light projected down the bore. If the bore is perfectly straight, two black parallel lines appear like train tracks. If that’s not what the technician sees when he looks down the bore he straightens the barrel manually until it is perfect. If you own a modern Savage rifle, it’s safe to say that one of the Westfield employees straightened your barrel by hand to ensure that it shot accurately.
The author installing an AccuTrigger. (Photo courtesy of Brad Fitzpatrick)
Savage’s AccuTrigger revolutionized the shooting industry when it first appeared in 2002, and, with minor modifications, that design has remained untouched for nearly a quarter-century. I’ve written about the design several times and had graphics of the design published in numerous articles, but piecing together an AccuTrigger from scratch is delicate and sophisticated business. It took me multiple attempts to get the trigger assembled properly, and then it was adjusted and function/safety tested by gun cranks far more adept than I am. It gave me an entirely new appreciation for the trigger and the ease with which the customer can make fine adjustments to pull weight.
The last component was the AccuFit V2 stock, Savage’s latest development. Using the Quick Set Dial (QSD), owners can customize comb height and length of pull in eighth-inch increments for a perfect fit. Swappable pistol grips allow for even more customization of the rifle.
The action screws were inserted and tightened, and what looked very much like a finished 110 Ultralite Pro rested in my hands. I had a bit of that Savage pride of my own now, knowing that I had built, or at least been coached to build, the newest version of the oldest American centerfire bolt-action rifle still in production.
The author testing the hunting rifle he built on the factory tour. (Photo courtesy of Brad Fitzpatrick)
Final Thoughts
Savage is, to some degree, a victim of the company’s past success. For years, Savage was billed as a value brand, a “good gun…for the money.” However, guns like the Ultralite Pro, the KLYM models, and precision rimfire and centerfire models should remind everyone is that, yes, Savage makes affordable guns, but their products are premium and high-quality. The 110 Ultralite Pro carries an MSRP of just under $2,000, which places it firmly in the premium rifle category. And this American-made rifle has premium components throughout including a carbon fiber wrapped barrel, a two-pound adjustable trigger, M-LOK, adjustable comb and length of pull, a standard 20 MOA one-piece top rail, and many other features that you’d expect to find on a top-shelf rifle in this class. Savage knows how to build premium rifles, and their products hold their own against the best competitors in the segment.
Perhaps that’s where some of that Savage pride comes from. The team in Westfield continues to design and 
manufacture products for every hunter and shooter
, including me. The DIY 7mm PRC 110 Ultralite Pro I built managed to shoot sub-MOA groups to 200 yards, and I’ll be taking it to Newfoundland to hunt moose in two weeks. It’s good to know that while I’m wading through bogs and forests that if a bull appears I will be able to make the shot. That’s part of the pride of Savage.

Brad Fitzpatrick

Since then, Fitzpatrick has hunted in 25 states, Canada, Argentina, and Spain. He has a special love for Africa and has hunted there nine times.
