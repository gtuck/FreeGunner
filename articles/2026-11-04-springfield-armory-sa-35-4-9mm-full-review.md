The Backstory in Brief
The P.35 was evolved from a John Browning design that was completed by his assistant Dieudonné Saive at FN in Belgium several years following Browning’s death in 1926. Though not as well-­known outside of enthusiast circles, Saive was incredibly accomplished. He later invented the FAL and helped develop the MAG 48 machine gun.
A matte blue is a standard finish, complemented by checkered walnut stocks fastened by a sub-flush flat-head screw. The ring-type hammer, short beavertail, exposed extractor and flat-steel magazine floorplate contribute to the model’s classic look and feel. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The P.­35 story began in 1921 at the behest of the French military who requested a 9mm service pistol. Saive designed the dual-­column 13-­round magazine, somewhat dismissed by Browning at the time, which became the heart of the P.­35. In 1934, Saive was tasked to complete the pistol for a Belgian military contract, which was adopted in 1935. The nicknames “High Power” and “Hi-Power,” referring to its 13-round magazine capacity, stuck after a 1950s marketing effort.
The P.­35 became one of the most successful service pistols of all time, adopted by the militaries of more than 90 nations. It was used to fight on both sides of World War II, as well as the Falklands War.

The core attributes of the P.35 have influenced subsequent handgun designs, particularly those that utilize a double-column magazine — nearly all modern service pistols.
The SA-35 trigger system lacks the magazine disconnect of the P.35, which most consider an improvement. The smooth curved trigger was tested at 3.8 pounds, exhibiting a short travel. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Variants were manufactured under license by several nations including Argentina, which began its own production in 1969. The version that inspired the pistol in this review was the Detective model built by Argentina’s Fabricaciones Militares (FM). With its shorter 4-inch barrel and 13-round magazine capacity, the Detective earned a coveted reputation. It was ideal for concealed carry at a time when few options existed.
Springfield Armory’s Version
The SA-­35 4" handgun is a short-recoil, locked-breech, single-­action semiautomatic, pairing the full-­size forged-steel frame with a 4-­inch barrel and shorter slide. (For the sake of history, it’s worth noting that the Detective had a nominally shorter 3.9-­inch barrel.) The frame is identical to the full-­sized SA-­35 in both size and capacity. By two rounds, the SA-35 bests the older P.35 and Hi-Powers with a 15-round magazine. In terms of weight, the SA-­35 with shorter slide and barrel shaved 1.7 ounces from the standard model’s stats, weighing just 1 pound, 13.8 ounces.

The SA-35 4-inch 9mm model features an extended thumb safety lever and pronounced slide-release lever. The thumb safety means that the SA-35 can be safely carried with the hammer cocked and locked with a round in the chamber, just as on a Model 1911. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The grip of the P.35 is well-regarded for its handling, and the SA-­35 maintains that feature. Custom grip maker Craig Spegel is known for producing some of the finest aftermarket grips available. The SA-­35’s factory walnut panels were clearly inspired by Spegel’s, which complement the slim profile of the frame. The grip panels are checkered, which helps the shooter maintain positive contact with the pistol during recoil.
The SA-­35 trigger feels superior to most factory Hi-Powers. Guns & Ammo’s test sample’s was tested at 3.8 pounds for an average of 10 pulls, having a touch of creep. It bears noting that, like the P.­35, the SA-­35 trigger reset is longer when compared to the Model 1911 or some striker-fire autos. Shooters accustomed to a short reset must consciously account for this reality during rapid fire or risk encountering a so-called “dead trigger.”
The SA-­35 4" 9mm handgun maintains a strong-­side manual safety. The lever is slim and adds little to the gun’s profile, yet it is easy to manipulate positively.
The SA-35 4” handgun is available with a matte-blue finish. The suggested retail price for the pistol is $799. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The SA-­35 also sports the long and distinctive-­looking slide-release lever found on the classic Hi-Power. The position of the pad makes it easy to use without shifting one’s grip around the handgun. The forward pivot point of may complicate the placement of the support hand thumb while gripping the SA-­35, though. (Some shooters actually use that part as an index point for the thumb.)
The strong-­side-­only magazine release on the SA-­35 is familiar and easy to reach. The button is lightly checkered, too. Unlike Hi-Powers with non-drop-free magazines, the SA-­35 4" 9mm handgun magazines fall easily.

The most common complaint about older Hi-Powers was hammer bite. A short beavertail combined with a long hammer spur often resulted in a cut on the web of the strong hand. Many suffered this malady and were ridiculed by some for having a “weak grip.” Some gunsmiths mitigated the issue with a bobbed hammer spurs or by welding extended beavertails to the frame. The SA-­35 eliminates hammer bite by utilizing a ring-­style hammer. It looks appropriate for the design and does not ask for blood.
The ring hammer concept was an alternative to the spur hammer and designed to eliminate hammer bite. It was also serrated to assist with thumb-cocking for single-action operation. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The compact barrel measures 4 inches as the “4"” in the model name suggests. Instead of the 4.65-­inch length of the standard SA-­35. A difference of .65 inch might not seem significant, but the reduced length makes the SA-35 more concealable and practical for most of us to carry.
Browning designed the P.35 with an internal extractor, just like the Model 1911. In 1962, though, the Hi-Power design was updated to use an external extractor. It operates using power from a coil spring. This system was not only easier for production but it improved reliability. In 2026, nearly every modern handgun introduction features an external extractor. The SA-­35 4" 9mm handgun also pairs the external extractor with a frame-­mounted, fixed steel ejector.
The SA-­35 barrel is cold hammer forged (CHF) stainless steel containing a 1-in-10-­inch twist. The barrel is polished and lacks a barrel hood that usually extends beyond the chamber. The breech face is flush with the back of the barrel — a simpler design to mass produce than fitting the Model 1911’s barrel hood — but the barrel still locks into the slide using three lugs at the top of the barrel, as well as the lower lug that locks with the frame. Like the P.­35 and unlike the Model 1911, the SA-­35 uses a fixed barrel bushing.
An extended feed ramp reinforces the barrel and supports modern 9mm bullet profiles for increased reliability with a variety of ammunition. Disassembly was eased with the P.35 by eliminated the need for a separate barrel bushing and swinging link used in the Model 1911. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Below the barrel is a full-length guiderod assembly and a flat-wire recoil spring. When assembled, the slide-stop pin holds the recoil spring guide in position. (Though functioning similarly in concept, such subtle changes helped Browning avoid violating his patents on the Colt M1911 and reduce hand-fitting requirements during production.) During the firing cycle, the barrel’s lower lug moves away from the guiderod to allow the breech to cam downward so the slide can complete its travel.
Jacketed hollowpoint handgun ammunition was not common until Lee Jurras and Super Vel Ammunition created a commercially viabe product for self-­defense use in the 1960s. Browning and Saive would not have contemplated anything but round or flat-­nosed bullet profiles when developing the P.­35 barrel. When Springfield Armory created the SA-­35, its engineer team changed the feed ramp geometry to accommodate modern bullet profiles and designs to ensure reliability with most ammo.
The top of the slide is rounded and dovetailed for iron sights. The front sight is a white dot set in a square black post while the rear is Springfield Armory’s Tactical Rack design with serrated blade and U-­shaped notch. The rear sight features a vertical surface that can be used to rack the slide in a pinch. The arrangement provides an excellent sight picture, balancing precision and speed.
Steel, drift-adjustable post and notch sights are practical for defensive shooting. The white dot up front is quick to align within the serrated U-notch of the Tactical Rack rear sight. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
When the SA-­35 was developed, Springfield Armory’s engineers managed to extend the capacity of Saive’s 13-rounder to 15 rounds. Steel SA-35 magazines are made by Mec-­Gar in Italy, a respected name in the business. The magazine bodies are heat-­treated for strength, and high tensile strength music wire is used for the springs. The followers are made from high impact polymer, too.

At The Range
G&A fired the SA-­35 4" 9mm handgun with a mixture of ball and defensive ammunition. Three bullet weights and profiles were used to consider both accuracy and reliability. We shot the pistol from a rest at 25 yards and offhand at ranges from 5 to 100 yards. Mechanical accuracy ranged from good to great. Most five-­shot groups measured around 2 inches. The sights were well-­regulated with all three bullet weights. Clearly, the shorter barrel and sight radius of the compact SA-35 did not have a demonstrable effect on dispersion downrange.
For a pistol designed for defensive use, reliability is far more important than raw accuracy potential, though. So, we also function-­tested our sample pistol with additional loads to evaluate its reliability with other types of ammunition. The SA-­35 4" 9mm handgun fed, fired, extracted and ejected perfect reliability. Our evaluators credited the feed ramp design. There are many 9x19mm loads on store shelves, and the SA-35 seems to digest all of it.
Produced by Mec-Gar, SA-35 magazines are cross-compatible between the compact and full-size models. Additional mags are available from springfield-armory.com. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Maintaining the Hi-Power’s comfortable grip profile, relatively low-bore axis, and updating it with excellent sights and a good trigger, the SA-­35 with 4-inch barrel is enjoyable to shoot. Recoil and muzzle rise were slightly more pronounced than our full-­sized SA-­35, but the 4-inch-barreled version was controllable enough due to the all-­steel construction. All things being equal, heavier guns exhibit less felt recoil. The checkered grips do a good job of supporting control, and are a classy feature to an understated design.
With the exception of the barrel, all the metal components on the SA-­35 4" 9mm handgun were blued. Parts were fit well, and there were no rattles or binding. Fit and finish were good. In a market awash in polymer, stainless steel and camouflage coatings, the classic simplicity of the SA-­35 stands out as attractive.
The handgun market has come full circle, and shooters are discovering (or rediscovering) the merits of an all-­steel hammer-­fired handgun. The SA-­35 epitomizes these virtues, and the 4-inch model does it in a more concealable package. It is a reliable, ergonomic and shootable handgun that has adapted one of the most enduring designs in firearms history to modern manufacturing.
