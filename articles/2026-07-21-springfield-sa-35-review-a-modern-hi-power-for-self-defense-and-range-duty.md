Springfield SA-35 Review: A Modern Hi-Power for Self-Defense and Range Duty

A compact-size Hi-Power clone that shoots like a full-size handgun.

July 21, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

What’s old is new again, and that’s certainly the case with the 
P-35/Browning Hi Power
, a high-capacity single-action 9mm begun by John Browning and finished by Fabrique Nationale’s Dieudonné Saive. The design has seen a resurgence in the past few years, and in 2021 Springfield Armory introduced its excellent SA-35. Now the company brings to market a four-inch model.
Hi Power aficionados may recognize this configuration as reminiscent of the Detective model, a gun developed in Argentina almost half a century ago under license from FN. It retained many of the characteristics that made the original design so effective, but in a more compact, easier-to-carry format.
The U.S.-built four-inch version of the SA-35 does exactly the same thing. When Springfield developed the gun, it made key improvements to the original, and these carry over to the new, shorter pistol: an increased magazine capacity to 15 rounds instead of 13; the elimination of the magazine disconnect safety; an improved trigger; and a recontoured hammer to prevent hammer bite.
The SA-35’s modified feed ramp handles modern defensive ammo reliably, and the four-inch version has a redesigned guide rod and spring to account for the shorter, lighter slide. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Features
The four-inch barrel is forged steel, and it features an improved feed ramp to handle today’s defensive loads, which can have bullet shapes that don’t always play well with some semiautos. Further, with the new four-inch model, Springfield’s Mike Humphries told me the company redesigned the guide rod to make it full length and also incorporated a new recoil spring to ensure reliability with the slide, which is shorter, lighter and has less mass compared to the full-size SA-35.

The slide is forged carbon steel with a matte blue finish. The rear slide serrations are finely cut, but they’re sharp enough to provide plenty of traction for racking the slide. The front of the slide carries the distinctive, so-called “Hi Power cut.”
Sights include a drift-adjustable Tactical Rack rear that’s serrated on its face and incorporates a deep U-notch. In a pinch, it can be used to cycle the slide on a stout belt or horizontal surface. The front sight is dovetailed and sports a white dot.
The gun’s single-side thumb safety is larger than that on the original P-35, and it’s easy to operate in both directions. The slide stop is well-shaped and large enough to use as a slide release, if that’s your technique. The round magazine release button is serrated and of normal length.

The curved trigger is factory tuned, and it has a short take-up before breaking relatively cleanly at a light 3.5 pounds. As I mentioned, the hammer has been recontoured. One of the complaints about the original Hi Power was the hammer coming back to bite the web of the shooter’s hand, and Springfield’s hammer prevents this.
The SA-35’s thumb safety is bigger than on the original P-35, and the hammer is recontoured to prevent bite. The trigger is factory-tuned for a nice single-action pull. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
The rounded hammer is serrated to keep your thumb from slipping, should you find the need to cock or uncock it manually. It also incorporates a half-cock stop that, while not an actual safety, halts the hammer from striking the firing pin if the trigger is not pulled.
The walnut grips are attractive. Their color and subtle figuring go well with the frame’s matte finish and give the gun a classic yet business-like look. The panels are checkered at 15 lines per inch and provide a good gripping surface, so the setup is as functional as it is good looking.
And make no mistake: This is a functional, versatile pistol. For those who want to carry it, the gun’s 29.8-ounce weight is easy to carry, and the 15-round magazine provides plenty of firepower. The weight is also ideal in terms of shootability, providing a lot more control and faster follow-ups than you’d get with a similar size polymer-frame striker-fired pistol.
Unlike the original P-35, the SA-35 feeds from a 15-round magazine instead of 13, and the mag supplied with the gun is easy to load to full capacity. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Performance
The SA-35 four-inch showed it can be really accurate with a load it likes. As you can see in the accompanying chart, 
Hornady
 Critical Defense was far and away the most accurate at 25 yards, with the other loads turning in groups more in line with what I get shooting iron sights with a relatively short barrel/sighting radius. There were zero malfunctions with any load, either from the bench or during practical drills—thanks no doubt to the feed ramp tweak and the redesigned guide rod and spring.

I shot the four-inch side by side with my own SA-35 full size, and the only difference I noticed involved the sights. The sight picture provided by the 0.13-inch front blade and 0.14-inch rear notch was tougher for me to see clearly with the four-incher’s reduced sighting radius.
Other than that personal problem, the SA-35 four-inch was a joy to shoot. The pistol will certainly appeal to collectors who are fans of this historic design, but that’s selling the gun short. While it lacks modern touches like a frame rail and a slide cut for optics, its size, capacity and dependability make it a fine defensive choice in addition to being a great gun to take to the range.
