Wilson Combat Bulwark 9mm: Full Review

Hammers are back, just hidden inside the Wilson Combat Bulwark. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Michael Anschuetz) 

Published: 

October 08, 2026

By Patrick Sweeney

Not everyone is enamored with striker-­fire pistols. Hammers have advantages. While polymer has advantages, too, it also has drawbacks. Bill Wilson set out to craft a duty and everyday carry (EDC) size pistol that had all the advantages and none of the disadvantages. 
The end result was the Bulwark
.
The starting point was overall size. The Glock 19 is considered by many to be the best combination of size, weight and capacity for defensive carry. The Bulwark corresponds to the G19 in size, while the weight — since the Bulwark is made of metal, aluminum and steel, — is 33 ounces to the G19’s 29 ounces, hardly enough to consider the Bulwark to be heavy.
The Wilson Combat Bulwark is an internal-hammer-fired 9mm semiautomatic pistol sharing similar dimensions to a Glock 19. It is compatible with many G19 pattern holsters, but it has a thinner profile and all-metal construction. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
All Metal
Like the G19, the magazine holds 15 rounds plus one in the chamber. Bulwark magazines are all-­steel and the same as Wilson Combat’s EDC X9 magazines. Though proprietary to Wilson Combat, the company’s history shows it has no problems producing enough magazines for its pistols and meeting demand for spares. 
Inside the aluminum grip module, the stainless-steel fire control unit is the serialized component of the firearm. The number is viewable through a slot in the chassis on the top right side of the grip. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The frame of the Bulwark is aluminum, machined from billet as a single piece with an internal stainless-­steel chassis that holds the Fire Control Unit (FCU). It also incorporates the rails that the slide moves along, so the 7075-series aluminum shell does not take the wear and tear of the tens of thousands of rounds the Bulwark is fully capable of handling.

Unlike some other chassis designs, the fire control unit (FCU) in the Bulwark is machined from billet stainless steel. It is also the serialized firearm, as the serial number of the Bulwark is on the FCU, visible through a slot machined in the aluminum frame and shell.
The Bulwark trigger is exceptional, providing a 1911-style feel when pressed straight and to the rear. The front of the triggerguard is squared without texture, and the safety lever is distinctively red. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The trigger is not pivoting but slides like that of the 1911. It has a trigger-­pad safety as one of four built-­in safety systems. The trigger weight is set to a weight between 33/4 and 41/4 pounds. The other three safety mechanisms include a grip safety that blocks the sear from movement. The grip safety also blocks the trigger bar until depressed, and when depressed it levers the firing pin block out of the path of the firing pin.
Even though the hammer is internal, a bright red portion of the hammer can be seen through the viewing port, and it is not easily visible when uncocked. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The aluminum frame and shell incorporate an accessory rail on the dust cover for lights or lasers. It has a squared triggerguard, and the frontstrap and backstrap are machined in the Wilson Combat “brick” pattern. This is an aggressive non-­slip texture that won’t slice up your hands just from a day’s practice. It won’t let your hand slip on the grip, either. The grips are made from G10 and are also machined in the brick pattern. (Yes, G10 is that tough.)

The magazine release is reversible, and the slide stop is flush-cut into the metal frame. The release protrudes slightly in a half-oval shape and is rounded to prevent snagging during the draw. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The bottom of the frame features a magazine well with an integral funnel to make reloads faster and smoother. The steel magazines are slimmer than the polymer-­coated magazines used by Glock. As a result, the X9 magazines permit the Bulwark to be slightly more compact in the grip area. If a Glock feels a bit large or blocky to your hands, the Bulwark will not. On the left side, the slide-­stop lever is recessed into the machined outline of the Bulwark grip frame. This makes it much less likely to catch on clothing during carry or to be inadvertently manipulated during firing or normal gun handling.
The magwell is flared slightly and was machined as part of the frame. G10 grip inserts share the same Wilson Combat Brick pattern. The Bulwark grip is narrower than the standard G19 grip, too. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Internal Hammer
The internal hammer of the Bulwark is there both to provide the advantages of a hammer and to avoid the shortcomings of a striker system. The spring-­loaded hammer acts to soak up some of the slide movement, and thus dampen felt recoil. It also provides more of a margin of firing force than a lightweight striker can. With its enclosed design, the Bulwark protects the hammer and its fitting lines from entry by dust, debris or clothing lint. One issue for those who carry the G19 daily is clothing lint getting into the firing mechanism and keeping the G19 (and other striker-­fire-type pistols) from firing. The weight and force of the Bulwark hammer — and its being enclosed — greatly reduce that likelihood.
Though it resembles common striker-fire pistols, the Wilson Combat Bulwark is similar in proportions only. It is a hammer-fired design where the hammer is shrouded by the slide, and the slide, barrel and guiderod assemblies are removed in a similar manner as the Model 1911. The 15-round magazine features witness holes to visually confirm its status. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Moving up to the slide, the first detail to note is that the rear of the slide has a small port machined into the bottom edge. The hammer is coated red, and when cocked, you can clearly see the red visible through the port. When the hammer is down the red is barely visible, and then only in good light. Otherwise, it isn’t there. While the hammer is enclosed, it is still possible to ascertain if it is cocked or not.
The grip safety is reminiscent of Springfield Armory’s XD pistols. It protrudes more than a standard Model 1911 grip safety, but positively deactivates the internal hammer block when pressed. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The slide starts as a billet of stainless steel and is given extensive and aggressive serrations for ease of handling. There are cocking serrations on the front of the slide and behind the ejection port. The extractor is external and quite large. The sights on the Bulwark are a fixed front with a red fiber-­optic insert and a U-­notch rear, with the rear sight at the back edge of the slide in order to make room for a red-­dot optic. The Bulwark slide is machined for optics, and the method Wilson Combat uses is to machine a large flat on the top of the slide. If you wish to just use irons, then the slide comes with a filler plate bolted down in the recess. To mount an optic, remove the plate and use the provided Combat Pin Technology (CPT) pins to secure the red-­dot sight in place. These pins take the shear forces of recoil that on other designs are borne by the screws holding the optic in place. In the CPT system, solid steel pins take that load. As a result, there is no need for filler or adapter plates.
The grip is akin to a chassis and is machined from billet aluminum. The one-piece design is strong, providing a more precise mounting underneath the dustcover for lights and lasers. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The slide also features top serrations, and the rear of the slide is serrated for both good looks and to cut down on glare when shooting. The engineering and ergonomics of the Bulwark have combined to produce a low bore axis, which helps to reduce muzzle rise during recoil and to bring the dot back into view upon recovery.

Inside the slide, the stainless-­steel barrel uses an angled cam surface to lock and unlock, eliminating the link found on 1911 pistols. At 4 inches, it provides sufficient velocity from the 9mm ammunition (standard or +P) it is chambered for, while still staying compact enough to be easy to use as an everyday carry pistol. The Bulwark comes with two 15-­round magazines and will fit most G19 holsters. The fit depends more on how tightly molded the particular holster is than anything else.
Sights are made by Wilson Combat and are tall enough to co-witness through many optics. The front includes a red fiber-optic insert, and the black rear sits far back to accomodate an optic. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
At The Range
In testing, the Bulwark delivered on all promises. It proved to be accurate, soft in recoil, utterly reliable in function and fit a selection of Glock-­specific holsters. One might question if the world needs a Glock-­sized pistol with a larger-­than-­Glock price tag. That answer is only one each shooter can provide for themselves, but the big differences of the steel magazines and the internal hammer cannot be overlooked. While it is possible to improve on the Glock trigger (and there are many who offer their services to do just that), it will always be at best a step behind even an average 1911 trigger. The Bulwark, meant to replicate the 1911 trigger (and succeeding at that), is a great advantage. While the Glock trigger upgrade doesn’t cost as much as the extra price of the Bulwark, the Glock still falls short, regardless of the savings. The slimmer Bulwark magazines make the grip smaller. Combined with the replaceable grips, you can customize the Bulwark to fit your hand exactly at less cost and risk.
Cut for the Shield RMSc and Leupold DeltaPoint Pro optic footprint, reflex sights are supported with steel pins. These CPT pins bear the force of recoil so mounting screws don’t have to. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Wilson Combat has brought us to a new plateau, improving on what many consider the standard EDC pistol. With a suggested retail price of $1,899, the Wilson Combat Bulwark is not inexpensive, but then quality never is.
