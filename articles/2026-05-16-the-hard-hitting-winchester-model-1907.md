The Hard-Hitting Winchester Model 1907

Intended as a hunting rifle for medium game, the 1907 saw a surge of success as an early police carbine thanks to it's hard hitting blowback design.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

May 16, 2026

By Dave Emary

Winchester dominated the early 1900s sales war within the hunting rifle segment with its lever-­action Model 1894. Winchester didn’t completely own the market, but it had the lion’s share. Gun designs advanced rapidly during the Second Industrial Revolution (1870-­1929), as manufacturing technique enabled more complicated concepts, and conflict throughout the world pressed for improvements in accuracy, ballistic effectiveness, increased capacity, reliability and rate of fire. New types of rifles were introduced, including the semiautomatic rifle. The first successful semiautomatic rifle design is often credited to Ferdinand von Mannlicher’s 1885 gas-­operated prototype. Other semiautomatic rifles followed in tandem with smokeless powder cartridge development, including the Madsen Model 1896 and Roth-­Haenel Model 1899.
The Model 1907 was an improved version of Winchester’s Model 1905 and supported the more powerful .35 WSL cartridge. The M1907 was a blowback operated semiautomatic firearm that was often sought after by law enforcement until the 1960s. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Thomas Johnson, Winchester engineer, designed the Model 1903 .22 rimfire. It was a blowback-operated rifle that fed from a tube magazine in the buttstock. The design was basic and quickly enlarged into a centerfire that fed from a detachable magazine: The Model 1905. The Model 1905 chambered the .32 and .35 Self Loading (SL) cartridges. The .32 SL fired a 165-­grain bullet at nominally 1,400 feet per second (fps), which was a little less performance than the .32-­40 Winchester but with a lower ballistic coefficient (BC) bullet. The .32 SL was not considered a significant medium-­game cartridge.
The .35 SL fired a 180-­grain bullet at 1,400 fps, offering somewhat better ballistics than the .32 SL. The .35 was considered adequate for deer and black bear at short ranges. With the introduction of the excellent Remington Model 8 semiautomatic in 1906 — which chambered serious medium-­game hunting cartridges — Winchester needed a semiauto with more power to compete.
The Winchester Model 1907 has a unique, unfamiliar charging assembly. The rod protruding from the steel forend cap is pushed to the rear and then released to charge the gun or eject a round. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Johnson simply stretched and beefed-­up the Model 1905 to accept a much more powerful .351 Winchester Self-­Loading (WSL). With it, Winchester finally had a gun that could legitimately compete in the semiautomatic hunting rifle market. The .351 WSL fired the same 180-­grain round-­nose bullet as the .35 SL, but at 1,860 fps for nearly twice the muzzle energy of the .35 SL. The .35 WSL was competitive with the .30-­30 Winchester for 75 to 100 yards. The Model 1907 was eventually strengthened even more for the Model 1910, which chambered the powerful .401 WSL. The introduction of the Models 1907 and 1910 rifles spelled death for the Model 1905 and its two cartridges in 1920.

New & Improved
The Model 1907 Winchester evolved from the blow-­back-operated Model 1903 rimfire and Model 1905 centerfire rifles. The Model 1907 was designed around a very heavy and robust one-­piece recoiling mass, housed under the forend. When fired, the mass continues back into the receiver, which includes the bolt. A very stout recoil spring is trapped between the front of the recoil mass and the receiver, and it is guided by a rod that passes through the recoiling mass before being fastened to the receiver.
The .35 WSL is a lengthened, high-pressure derivative of the Model 1905 .35 SL. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The rod protruding from the front of the forend is the operating rod for the action. Pushing on the rod pushes the recoiling mass and bolt to the rear, and bringing it forward picks up a round from the magazine. The rod can be turned 90 degrees at its most reward travel to lock the bolt open for inspection or cleaning. If you want to know what the trigger group looks like, look at the trigger group in the M1 .30 Carbine. The design of the M1 Carbine trigger group was copied from the Winchester Model 1905 and 1907 designs.
The Model 1907 features a takedown screw at the back of the receiver. The screw is held in place by a spring-­loaded button that detents into the screw. Pushing the button releases the screw, and it can be unscrewed. The buttstock and trigger assembly can then be tapped downward and separated from the receiver, barrel, bolt and recoiling mass. The barrel on the 1907 is 20 ­inches long.

The 1907 features a detachable, single-­stack magazine released by pushing the small tab on the right side of the magazine inward and pulling the magazine out. The tab is attached to the receiver. The 1907 was available with five-­, 10-­ and 15-­round magazines. Larger-capacity magazines can still be found on eBay or gunbroker.com, but are very expensive.
The Model 1907 was only chambered in .351 WSL. The .351 WSL is a straight-­walled cartridge that is just a little shorter than the .357 Remington Maximum. In fact, cases for the .351 WSL can be made from .357 Maximum cases by trimming the .357 Maximum a little and re-­cutting the rim and extractor groove, and reforming slightly. Handloads for this article were made using modified .357 Maximum cases. The .351 WSL was loaded with either a 180-­grain round-­nose soft-­point or full-­metal-­jacket bullets. The 1922 Winchester catalog lists the 180-­grain bullet at 1,856 fps for a muzzle energy of 1,378 ft.-­lbs. This performance put the .351 WSL at slightly below the 170-­grain .30-­30 load of the time. However, it was well behind the 200-­grain .35 Remington bullet traveling at 2,020 fps. The .351 WSL was considered acceptable for deer and black bear inside 100 yards. It would not keep up with the .30-­30 beyond 100 yards.
The Model 1907 featured an ejection port on the side of the receiver. The ejector is visible on the opposite receiver wall. When loaded, the magazine positioned cartridges behind the chamber. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 1907 was a popular gun with law enforcement, and it was used widely as a firearm for prison guards. In 1935, Winchester offered a special “police rifle” variant featuring a non-­adjustable rear sight moved further to the rear on the barrel, sling swivels, a larger magazine release, and a thinner, lighter, barrel contour. A barrel sleeve with a Krag bayonet mount and front sight was an option with the police rifle.
In the late 1930s, Winchester made several modifications to the Model 1907 to solve several nagging problems and make it easier to operate. The Model 1907 had a problem with the forend frequently cracking. The forend was made much thicker to solve this. A redesigned charging handle replaced the original round disc handle, which made turning the operating rod 90 degrees to lock the bolt back easier, too. A 20-­round magazine was also added for police variants. The Model 1907 was available in a standard grade, but a deluxe grade with semi-pistol grip, upgraded wood and checkering was also offered.
The Model 1907 did see some military service during World War I. France was the largest user, purchasing about 5,000 rifles by the end of the war including more than 1.5 ­million rounds of ammunition. Great Britain ordered 120 rifles, too, and about 80,000 rounds of ammunition between December 1914 and April 1916. British rifles were used by aircraft observers and modified with a larger trigger and triggerguard loop for use with gloved hands.

Under the authority of Tsar Nicholas II, Russia ordered 500 rifles and 1.5 million rounds of ammunition in May 1916. The February Revolution of 1917 overthrew the Tsar, preventing further orders.
The United States ordered 19 rifles and 9,000 rounds of ammunition, delivering theirs to the 1st Aero Squadron in Columbus, New Mexico. Some have surmised that they were used during the Pancho Villa Expedition into Mexico.
The Model 1907 was also a favorite of John Dillinger’s notorious gang during their bank and police station robberies throughout the Midwest from 1933 to 1934. Most of the guns were modified to fire full-­automatic and featured shortened barrels, extended magazines, and Cutts compensators. These were made by Texas gunsmith Hyman Lehman.
Unscrewing the spanner nut at the front of the forend allowed the removal of the forend. The spanner nut was secured by a spring-loaded pawl in the steel forend cap. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 1907 was regarded as a well built, robust and reliable rifle for its time. The rifle and .351 WSL it chambered were considered adequate for short-­range hunting, but it was never considered a very accurate combination. Production of the Model 1907 ceased in 1957 after 58,733 rifles were produced. The follow-­on Model 1910 Winchester, which chambered the more powerful .401 WSL, was manufactured from 1910 to 1936 with 20,787 rifles produced. The Models 1907 and 1910 series rifles were said to have sold for less than the Remington Models 8 and 81, but they never sold as well with almost twice as many Remingtons sold as Winchesters. The Winchesters were hampered by poor accuracy, a marginal cartridge in the .351 WSL, a very heavy recoiling cartridge in the .401 WSL, and a poor trigger.
The triggerguard incorporates a crossbolt safety that later appeared on the M1 Carbine. The trigger shoe is short, and the serial number appears under the receiver above the trigger. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
At The Range
Guns & Ammo’s test rifle was produced in 1909 and is in excellent condition. The wood on the rifle is excellent with a forend possessing some attractive figure. When I received the rifle, the forend had the usual crack in it but I fixed this and refinished it. The bore is in like-­new condition, too. The gun had been upgraded at some point with a fold-­down rear sight and a Lyman tang sight. The operating rod was upgraded with the later charging handle, which does make it much easier to turn the operating rod to lock the bolt back. The gun has the standard 20-­inch barrel and a checkered steel buttplate.
The rear sight on G&A’s test rifle is a factory-optioned folding V-notch with diamond that’s dovetailed to the barrel. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The trigger pull is mushy, having noticeable creep and a heavy pull measured at 9 pounds, 8 ounces. The gun was the heaviest of G&A’s 100-­year-­old hunting rifles, weighing 8 pounds, 10 ounces. The gun balances well, though, especially when carried at the receiver. It swings well, too. It was probably a good woods rifle.
When the 1907 was purchased, it included a box of 1970s-­vintage Winchester 180-­grain soft-­point ammunition. I modified .357 Maximum cases to .351 WSL specs and made some loads with a Redding die set (redding-­reloading.com). The bullet I chose was a .351-­inch 180-­grain TMJ offered by both Buffalo Arms (buffaloarms.com) and Graf & Sons (grafs.com). They were loaded with IMR 4227 and proved to be even less accurate than the factory ammunition! Hence, only factory ammunition was used for this evaluation.
The M1907 could be equipped with a Lyman or Marbles tang sight. Factory sights were marked “SL” underneath the base. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Winchester ammunition appeared to be loaded with a WC 296/297-­type propellant. The ammunition velocity uniformity was quite poor with accuracy also being rather poor. The accuracy would have been sufficient for hunting deer-­size game at 100 yards or so. Table 1 shows test results at 100 yards. Editor-­in-­Chief Eric Poole — a former collegiate rifle shooter and high-­power competitor — did all the group shooting. For this series, he fired three five-­shot groups at 100 yards and one group at 200 yards. We had limited out-­of-­production ammunition for this rifle, hence the reduced number of groups.

The barrel of G&A’s Model 1907 was roll marked “.351 CAL” after the patent information, rather than the more common cartridge nomenclature of .35 WSL. This rifle was manufactured in 1909. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
We also wanted to explore the maximum effective range of the gun using its period sights. The sight picture with the Lyman tang sight was quite good.
The 1922 Winchester catalog lists the .351 WSL 180-­grains load at 1,856 fps. The bullet has a BC of about .200 (G1), which would give it a retained velocity of roughly 1,355 fps and retained energy of 730 ft.-­lbs. at 150 yards. This is probably about the ballistic limit of the cartridge on deer-­size game. It is also about the limit of the ability of the rifle to hold the kill zone on deer. We used a steel target with a 10-­inch circle to represent a deer’s kill zone at 200 yards. We were unable to hold the 10-­inch kill-­zone circle on our 200-­yard steel target with the Model 1907. In fact, we were unable to hold the 16-­inch-­wide piece of steel at 200 yards. The recoil of the 1907 was noticeable but not objectionable. As the trigger pull numbers showed, the trigger remained heavy and hard to manage.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
Though heavy, the Winchester Model 1907 was an easy rifle to carry at the range with good balance to it. The accuracy was disappointing, unfortunately, as well as the trigger. With some work to the trigger, and some loading with quality Hawk bullets (hawkbullets.com), it might be possible to get better performance from the rifle. Our lasting feelings were that the Model 1907 probably made a good short-­range woods rifle, but it was not competitive with the Remington Model 8.
