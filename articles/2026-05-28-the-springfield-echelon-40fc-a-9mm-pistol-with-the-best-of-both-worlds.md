May 28, 2026

By Frank Melloni

Until recently, the concealed-carry pistol market has always been pretty black and white: Either you dealt with the bulk of a full-size handgun, or you learned to live with the limited capacity and real estate of a compact. Blending the benefits of both seemed logical, but it did take some time for the industry to warm up to the idea. The newest to the fold is the Springfield Echelon 4.0FC, which carries over the finest features of the series in a blended footprint—combining the F frame of the full-size model with the C of the compact model’s 4.0-inch barrel.
The 4.0FC combines a four-inch barrel with a full-size frame—creating a relatively compact gun with plenty of firepower courtesy of 17- and 20-round mags. (Frank Melloni photo)
If you’re already familiar with the full-size Echelon, you’ll recognize that the grip length of the 4.0FC is the same. This means that the whopping 17+1 capacity is maintained, and it can be increased to 20+1 by using the supplied extended magazine.
To me, capacity is king, but the smaller the package, the more likely it’ll be on me. This brings us to the abbreviated slide assembly that houses the four-inch barrel. It stretches what I would consider a compact, but it provides quite a bit of surface purchase for manipulation.
Both the magazine release and slide-lock lever are fully ambidextrous. Textured ledges forward on the frame help you apply downward pressure to tame muzzle flip. (Frank Melloni photo)
Slide Features
Some people believe you should turn your hand inward to get as much of it as possible on the operative areas of the slide. Others believe you should pinch and pull, as it’s more natural. The good news is that this Echelon 4.0FC slide has features to accommodate both—with a flared rear for the pinch-and-pull crowd and a forward “trench” for those who prefer the other style.

I’m a pinch-and-pull guy because I don’t have to navigate around an optic, which brings us to what I believe to be the most outstanding feature of the Echelon family. Springfield’s VIS or Variable Interface System is the most flexible optics cut on the market. You simply choose the pegs that correspond with your optic for direct mounting of more than 30 different optics models.
Chassis System and Grip Module
The Echelon is a modular design, and the serialized component is 
Springfield’s Central Operating Group, or COG
. This is held in place by the takedown lever and a system of grooves cut into the polymer frame. Removing the COG doesn’t require any tools, and once separated from the rest of the gun, it can be dropped into other Echelon grip modules.
Springfield worked on the Echelon with its longtime pro staffer Rob Leatham, and you can see how the gun’s fast-paced pedigree has taken form. The most obvious of Leatham’s influences are the textured ledges on each side of the pistol. On your support-hand side, the ledge allows the shooter to add downward pressure to better tame muzzle flip. The opposite side serves as a tactile reference point for your trigger finger when it’s indexed away from the trigger.

Further control is produced through the double undercut trigger guard, as well as a deep recess for your thumb and forefinger. This is joined by a generous cut under the beavertail that gets you as close to the bore axis as possible. And the Echelon comes with a trio of interchangeable backstraps.
The ambidextrous theme continues across all the controls, including the magazine release, which is rare since mag releases are reversible at best on most pistols. The design team did a great job gating the release, too. With an ambidextrous release there’s always the possibility that the firing hand’s middle finger can accidentally engage the release, something this lefty learned the hard way through years of shooting right-handed guns.
The VIS slide cut makes it a snap to add nearly any optic you want, and the excellent iron sights will co-witness with many models, like this Trijicon RMR. (Frank Melloni photo)
Testing the Echelon 4.0 FC
For testing I mounted a green-dot version of Trijicon’s RMR on the Echelon’s VIS, and the factory sights easily co-witnessed through its window. Bench accuracy was more than adequate for a pistol that’s not explicitly made for target work, as you can see in the accompanying chart.
With that done, I began with some basic transitions between Caldwell IPSC silhouette targets. With a full magazine, the weight stays back in your hand, making the gun point effortlessly. This also played a role in taming muzzle flip.
Switching to Bill Drills, I was able to gauge the trigger’s reset, which felt short enough for rapid engagement but not too short. The forward frame ledges helped me maintain my grip, but I think the angle could be a little less steep, since some of the punchier fodder caused my thumb to slip.

The only malfunction was failure to go into slide lock on the first few magazines. However, after a quick oiling and a few more runs, that worked itself out.
I carried the gun in a universal appendix holster, and the compact barrel made it noticeably less obtrusive than a full-size model. I am wider than I am tall, and I was able to keep even the extended magazine from printing.
The Echelon 4.0C ought to appeal to shooters who are on the fence between capacity and concealability. It’s one of the few models on the market that draws for both camps without bastardizing either.
