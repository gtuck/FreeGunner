CMMG Mk4 V2 Review: A Deadly Accurate .22 Nosler

The CMMG Mk4 V2 rolls the dice with the .22 Nosler, a fast cartridge with a light bullet.

 Author J. Scott Rupp field testing the CMMG Mk4 V2. (J. Scott Rupp photo)

February 01, 2018

By J. Scott Rupp

How accurate can an AR be? Based on my admittedly limited experience with them (living with one for three months during Army infantry training doesn’t count since I carried it a lot more than I shot it), I have been quick to say “not very.” The CMMG Mk4 in .22 Nosler has made me change my tune. They can be very accurate.
Many knowledgeable AR fans out there are saying “Told ya,” but for those of you who believe only bolt guns can be counted on to deliver sub-m.o.a. accuracy, let me introduce you to CMMG.
Formed in 2002, it’s a family-run company based in Missouri (and in case you’re wondering, the letters CMMG don’t stand for anything). It recently moved its factory to Boonville, where it employs more than 50 people. It offers uppers, lowers, .22 conversions and parts and accessories. The company has also shown a flair for doing the unconventional, such as its hard-hitting .458 SOCOM Anvil and the Mk47 Mutant, an AR that feeds from AK magazines.
CMMG’s Mk4 V2 is a dedicated varmint rig that pairs an accurate rifle-length AR platform with the flat-shooting, hard-hitting .22 Nosler cartridge. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
.22 Nosler
The choice of the .22 Nosler fits that pattern. “There was a lot of buzz about the caliber coming out of [the industry’s 2017 trade show],” said Tyson Bradshaw, CMMG’s product development manager. “We were impressed with the ballistics and found chambering the round to be relatively easy.”

Before getting into the rifle’s particulars, a brief recap of the Nosler cartridge is in order (we reviewed the round in the May/June 2016 issue, and if you don’t have that copy, you can find the article at RifleShooterMag.com).
In recent years, Nosler has been at the forefront of the charge to offer new, souped-up cartridges that raise the bar for velocity. These include rounds in 6.5mm, 7mm, .30 and .33, and all deliver speeds that are at or near the top of their respective categories.
The 
.22 Nosler
 isn’t merely at the head of its class, it’s the teacher. Intended to provide a good-size step up in performance over the .223 Rem. while still operating at the same pressure as the tried-and-true AR chambering, it is roughly similar to the 6.8mm SPC and feeds from magazines designed for that cartridge. It has a 30-degree shoulder, and case volume is 34.2 grains of water, 25 percent more than the .223 Rem.

You want speed? The Nosler round has it in spades. As you can see in the accompanying chart, it can push a 55-grain bullet at 3,500 fps out of a 24-inch barrel. That’s a couple hundred feet per second better than you’ll get from the .223, and it’s a useful boost for those who shoot varmints—providing flatter trajectories, faster flight times and less wind drift.
The Mk4 V2 sports a 24-inch, button-rifled fluted stainless-steel barrel. It lacks a muzzle device and instead boasts a target crown. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Features
Speed is great, but it doesn’t mean much if you can’t hit what you’re aiming at. This is where a well-built rifle comes into play. CMMG’s .22 Nosler rifle is built on its Mk4 platform, and in this case it is the V2 variant, which sports a tapered, fluted 24-inch barrel made of 416 stainless steel. It’s button-rifled and made in the U.S. to CMMG’s specs, according to Bradshaw. It lacks any sort of muzzle device and instead features a target crown. Twist is 1:8.
It is free-floated within the company’s RKM14 handguard, a 6061 T6 aluminum unit with KeyMod slots at three, six and nine o’clock. QD mounting holes are located at the rear of the handguard at three and nine o’clock. It’s 14 inches long, and it’s railed at the top for easy scope mounting. I was curious about the choice of KeyMod over M-Lok because the latter seems to be the most popular option these days, but Bradshaw said that for his company, KeyMod is the better seller.
The slim RKM14 is so tightly fitted I was unable to budge it with several raps from a polymer tap hammer. It is further secured by T25 Torx screws. Underneath is a rifle-length gas system, which makes sense because with its 24-inch barrel this is very much a rifle and not a carbine or some in-between configuration.
The receiver is forged 7075 T6 aluminum, and my test sample featured an optional flat dark earth Cerakote finish, which costs an additional $150. The safety selector is single-side, and the charging handle is your standard single-latch model as well. The mag release and bolt release are everyday mil-spec too.

What is special is the Geissele SSA trigger, a two-stage affair with a super-clean break. First-stage weight is right at three pounds, and sear release comes at the four-pound five-ounce mark. I shot two-stage triggers for years as a smallbore rifle competitor, and they’re second nature to me. I still prefer two-stage over single-stage for precision work. The two-stage allows you to take up the first while ensuring the crosshairs are where they’re supposed to be, the wind is right and everything looks good. Then just a little more pressure to break a clean shot.
The bolt carrier group is comprised of a 9131 steel bolt, chrome-lined 8620 steel carrier and 4140 steel extractor—all threated to a phosphate finish. The gas key is properly staked.
I find it hard to get excited about pistol grips, but props to CMMG for going with Magpul’s MOE. It’s got stippling on both sides and serrations fore and aft for a sure grip, with a compartment hatch in the base.
The Magpul MOE Rifle buttstock is a fixed stock with a comfortable comb. It has QD pockets (but no adapter for attaching swivels) as well as a slot for a utility sling. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Magpul MOE Buttstock
Of more importance to me is the buttstock, which is the Magpul MOE Rifle. As a hunting rifle, this Mk 4 doesn’t need a six-position collapsible, and what I really liked about this stock is its nicely shaped comb, which feels comfortable and makes it easy to get a good cheek weld. And while there’s no comb-height adjustment, I didn’t feel like I was breaking my neck to see through the scope.
The stock features QD pockets on both sides, front and back—although the Type 1 adapter ($15 from Magpul) necessary for installing a QD sling swivel in the MOE is not included. However, there’s also a molded-in 1.25-inch loop slot at the bottom, so you’re not totally out of luck. If you have a strap sling and a QD swivel for the front, you can still install it in two-point fashion—which, let’s face it, if you’re hunting is how you’re going to carry it anyway.
Pushing in on a latch in the left side of the butt swings it open to reveal a large storage compartment. The butt itself has a rubber pad. It’s not soft rubber, and it almost feels like plastic, but it does a decent job of keeping the gun in your shoulder. A flat in the toe has two knockout bosses so you can add a rail section there if you want.
As mentioned, the .22 Nosler feeds from a standard 6.8mm SPC magazine, and the mag’s gray anti-tilt follower is stamped “6.8”. The 10-rounder furnished with the rifle is made of stainless steel with a black finish. I prefer polymer magazines because they’re quieter to use and carry in a pocket (I don’t go around with a mag pouch on my belt), and I like polymer’s lack of sharp edges. But, alas, polymer mags for the 6.8 don’t exist—at least not yet.
The rifle feeds from standard 6.8mm SPC magazines, and it features the excellent Geissele SSA two-stage trigger, which really contributes to the gun’s accuracy. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Testing
To test the rifle I mounted Nikon’s new Black X1000 4-16x50mm riflescope in that company’s one-piece M series mount. It’s a good companion for a long-range rifle like this, with fully multicoated air-to-glass surfaces, resettable target turrets, side-focus parallax and a lighted reticle. I borrowed the MRAD version, which is mil-based, and clicks are 0.1 mil at 100 yards. It’s built on a 30mm tube and offers 6.75 inches of mountable tube length. At 23.8 ounces it wasn’t as heavy as I expected, and it balanced well with the Mk4.

The reticle itself features 0.5 and 1.0 mil hash marks for both windage and elevation, but unfortunately the reticle is in the second focal plane, so any holdovers or Kentucky windage needs to be done at 16X to be truly accurate. You can use the company’s Spot On app to generate a dope chart, and it will give you yardages for the elevation hashes, but the app hasn’t been updated to the point that the windage hashes are in mils; they’re presented in inches.
Since the .22 Nosler is new, it’s still in the proprietary stage and only Nosler is loading it. I got the opportunity to shoot all three loads: 77-grain Custom Competition hollowpoint; 55-grain Ballistic Tip Varmint, which features a thinner jacket than the big game version; and a 62-grain flat-base hollowpoint. All three turned in stellar performances at both 100 and 200 yards, as you can see in the accompanying chart. I did note the cartridge was more wind-sensitive at 200 yards than I expected, but even speed can’t overcome a lack of bullet weight when the wind is changing velocity significantly.
With the range work completed, I took the Mk4 for a stroll in hopes of crossing paths with a coyote. I attached a utility sling to the slot in the toe and put a UTG KeyMod QD swivel adapter at six o’clock, nine inches out on the handguard. It carried surprisingly well considering its 10-pound, one-ounce weight with scope aboard and a full 10 rounds of 55-grain ammo in the magazine.
I say surprisingly well, but this still isn’t a “walking varminter” unless you’re a lot tougher than I am. After a couple of hours of hiking, that 10-pound weight was starting to tell on my shoulder. On the bright side, I appreciated the weight when set up and watching for coyotes. The rifle was super stable, whether perched on a Bog Pod or simply braced against my knees in an open sitting position. The crosshairs locked on to whatever spot I wanted and stayed there.
I think this rifle would be a worthy addition to any serious varminter’s arsenal. Like the idea of a souped-up .22 in the AR platform but aren’t an avid varmint hunter? CMMG has you covered with several other complete rifle or upper receiver options with 18- or 22-inch unfluted stainless barrels with a CMMG SV brake or flash hider, depending on what you choose.

Final Thoughts
Time will tell whether the .22 Nosler catches on, although with several rifles already on the market—ARs and bolt guns both—I’m betting it will. And with the CMMG Mk4 V2 you not only get a flat-shooting, hard-hitting .22 centerfire, you get bragging-rights accuracy to boot. It’s hard to see how you could go wrong.
