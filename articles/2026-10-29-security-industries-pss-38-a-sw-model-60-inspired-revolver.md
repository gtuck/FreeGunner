Security Industries PSS 38: A S&W Model 60 Inspired Revolver

The 1970s stainless steel pocket pistol that challenged Smith & Wesson's J-frame dominance.

 While the Security Industries PSS 38 revolver may not be a first-class firearm, it was reasonably well made and was at one time relatively popular—which was especially true for its .357 brother. (Bob Campbell photo)

October 29, 2026

By Bob Campbell

When I was a teenager reading gun mags 50 years ago, a young writer who would later become my friend seemed very enthusiastic about a revolver that rose above the cheap guns of the time: the Security Industries of America PPM.
It was a five-shot double action based on the Smith & Wesson Model 60. The PPM featured a bobbed hammer and hand-filling grips, and it was chambered to .357 Magnum. There were two other guns in this lineup: the PM 357 with 2.5-inch barrel; and my example, the PSS 38 in .38 Special that, like the PM 357, sported a hammer spur.
These revolvers were moderately popular at the time. The manufacturer was located in Little Ferry, New Jersey. The factory was rumored to be no larger than a service station building.
The Security Industries revolvers were introduced in 1973, and the company went out of business in 1978. Production figures are difficult to verify, but 5,000 revolvers seems accurate.

The example here was found in a local shop in late 2025. It has the worn look of a handgun carried often but fired seldom, as is typical of snubnose .38s. The stainless finish is nicely worn to the point of being gray. There is no pitting. There are no cast or MIM parts, a testament to how guns were built then. That doesn’t mean all guns of the era were well made, but the materials were good.
Like I said, the gun was modeled on the S&W Model 60, with some small changes. The cylinder release is a redesign that is less likely to catch the thumb in recoil—a worthy modification on the .357 models that carried over to the PSS 38.

The Security Industries guns features a frame-mounted firing pin but lacks a transfer bar. The barrels were not pinned in place, and I have seen one example where the barrel could be clocked by hand—even though it had been fired little.

The double-action pull isn’t as smooth as a Smith & Wesson of the era, but it isn’t gritty either and is fairly typical for revolvers of its day. The sights are typical open fixed sights with post front.
The revolver cost a little less than a S&W Model 60 at its peak of popularity. However, when it comes to the .357 Magnum versions, for several years the Security Industries of America revolver was the only show in town. But Smith & Wesson eventually upgraded its magnums, and apparently Security Industry of America revolvers did not prosper with those “men among men” strong enough to fire .357 extensively. As for my .38 Special, it kicks enough for me.
This is a revolver that has scarcely held its value. I saw several auctions closed at less than $150—about what it cost new in the early to mid 1970s. An average-condition revolver seems to bring about $160, although I found one terribly overpriced sample at over $300.
From a useful but second-rate new handgun to a modestly price, used revolver, the Security Industries of America gun is an interesting example of a small company making a play for a huge market.
