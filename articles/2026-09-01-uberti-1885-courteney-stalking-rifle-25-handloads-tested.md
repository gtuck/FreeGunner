Uberti 1885 Courteney Stalking Rifle: 25 Handloads [Tested]

This .303 British Chambered Rifle is Prime for Handloading

 The .303 British has an enviable place in world history and cartridge development. (Steve Gash photo)

September 01, 2026

By Steve Gash

It was often said that the sun never set on the British Empire. The .303 British cartridge was a big factor that made that statement true, and it served for nearly a century, when it was replaced in 1957 by the 7.62x51 NATO.
The .303 British was developed in 1888 and adopted by the British military on February 20, 1889. It was designed for the Lee-Metford Mk I bolt-action rifle, and many projectile and propellant variations ensued.
The British developed and popularized a style of rifle called the “stalking rifle.” These were 
lightweight single shots used for deer-size game
 and were carried by the intrepid hunter as he stalked game. A typical chambering of the day was the 7x57 Mauser, called the .275 Rigby in Europe and Africa. Numerous famous African hunters employed stalking rifles, including the famous W.D.M. Bell. Perhaps the most famous of the old-time African hunters was Captain Frederick Courteney Selous. He hunted Africa from 1871 and served in the Boer War. He was killed on January 4, 1917, by a German sniper on the banks of the Rufiji River, the area known as the Selous Game Reserve in what is now Tanzania. Selous is reported to have hunted with a .303 single shot.
The .303 replaced the blackpowder 577/450 Martini Henry cartridge and was the British service cartridge for 68 years. Its initial loading was with 71 grains of blackpowder under the 215-grain Mk II FMJ bullet. Muzzle velocity was reported to be 1,970 fps, but the muzzle energy was 1,853 ft-lbs. This load has an interesting place in military history.

In combat, the Mk II load was not particularly effective in Britain’s war, which at that time was in Afghanistan. Thus, the Superintendent of the British arsenal at Dum Dum, India, had a bright idea. Captain Bertie Clay developed some new bullets with hollow and soft points. They were used in the Mark II, IV, and V loads, and as one would expect, they expanded and were much more effective on adversaries. But the days of these bullets were numbered, as the politics of the time decreed that these loads did too much damage in the Boer War in Africa. At the Hague Convention in 1899, Dutch and Swiss representatives complained about the “increased suffering” caused by the bullet. The result was that the parties agreed to stop using expanding bullets, and they were deemed unacceptable under international law. Thus, the moniker of the “dum dum” bullet was born, and the term was forever sullied by an unknowing press.
Developed in 1888, the .303 British cartridge has an interesting history, and many major U.S. ammo makers still offer ammunition. (Steve Gash photo)
A reader informed me that the Mk I powder of 1910 was followed by the Mk II powder, and then cordite in 1910. The Mk VIIz in 1918 was loaded with nitrocellulose. The .303 was one of the few cartridges to make the transition from blackpowder to smokeless. In fact, the .303 was the first cartridge to be loaded with the new smokeless propellant. The Mark VII 174-grain bullet was adopted with a muzzle velocity of 2,440 fps, which produced a muzzle energy of 2,301 ft-lbs—obviously, a significant increase. This loading continued to serve the Empire until 1957.
My introduction to the .303 British was with a used British military rifle of unknown lineage. (I thought it was an SMLE but was later informed that it was in fact a shortened “Long Lee,” known these days I think as the No. 1 Mk III.) Interestingly, I also was informed that there was no No. 1 Mk I, just the No. 1 Mk III. Be that as it may, I can attest that this old veteran is a real shooter. With its 12-pound trigger and crude sights, 2-inch groups at 50 yards were the norm. It is a fine battle rifle. I wouldn’t want anybody shooting at me with it!

The .303 cartridge features what today would be termed an old-fashioned case. The case length is 2.222 inches, but it’s not fat, except maybe at its base, and it has a modest 14-degree shoulder angle and a neck length of 0.332 inch, so there is plenty of tension on the bullet. The .303 is very popular in several countries, but the United States is not one of them. In Canada, many moose, caribous, and black bears are taken annually by hunters with their .303s.
Uberti’s Stalking Rifle
In the December 2008 issue of Guns & Ammo, I wrote that it would be great if someone made a modern rifle for the .303. Well, someone did. In 2020 Uberti USA started importing a slick single-shot rifle chambered for this classic British round. It is called the Courteney Stalking Rifle in honor of Selous.
Uberti’s stalking rifle is made in Italy and is quality ordnance. It is based on the Winchester High Wall Model 1885 single-shot action, with slight modifications. Lowering the lever on the Model 1885 cocked the hammer. On the Uberti rifle, after inserting a cartridge and raising the lever, the hammer must be manually cocked. However, if you don’t fire, just lowering the lever safely “decocks” the rifle. The rifle has a medium-contour 24-inch barrel with a 1:10 twist, which is appropriate for the .303’s bullets. The barrel quarter rib and sights are nicely polished and blued. The action, lever, tang, and trigger are nicely casehardened. It’s really classy. Of course, Selous’s rifles had open sights, and the Uberti rifle is faithful to that. The bead front sight is hooded, and the rear sight has a shallow “U” that is adjustable for windage and elevation. It looks rather period correct and right at home on the sleek arm. In deference to modern times, a quarter rib with four “Weaver” slots in it is made for mounting an optical sight. I tried to obtain one but was unsuccessful. The buttstock and forearm are of walnut, and they are nicely checkered. The forearm is short, measuring 8.5 inches, and has a neat African heartwood tip.

When I first read of the Courteney Stalking Rifle in late 2024, I was smitten and ordered one. None were to be had. But I proceeded to accumulate .303 British dies, factory ammo, bullets, and load data. Finally, in January 2026, my rifle arrived.
Surprisingly, it was deemed a “demo rifle,” and it had a few quirks. First, the quarter rib did not have the Weaver cross-slots like the ones described and shown in various publications. I eventually received another rib, also without cross-slots, but it had been drilled and tapped and a had one-piece Weaver base attached to it. However, the small screws (4mm metric, to be exact) for the rib would not stay tight and came loose after only a few shots. So, with the loose scope, the rifle produced buckshot patterns instead of groups.

I “punted” and used the open sights, which consisted of a nice gold-colored bead front and a blade rear that is adjustable for windage and elevation. I was not optimistic, but the open sights proved to be entirely usable, as long as the right size target was used. At 100 yards, with my eyesight, the bead wandered all over a 5.25-inch black bullseye, but at 50 yards, it centered beautifully on a 3-inch blaze orange target and centered perfectly in the U-notch of the rear sight blade, so I did the accuracy testing at that distance. The results were outstanding. The old .303 round still has pizzazz.
The rifle’s trigger was a revelation. The pull weight was a delightful 2 pounds, 11 ounces. It is a “double stage trigger,” sorta. When the trigger is squeezed, there is a very slight movement and a distinct “click.” Then a little more pressure releases the hammer.
The .303 British is a prime candidate for handloading because plenty of powders and component bullets work well in it. (Steve Gash photo)
Shooting the .303
The production of .303 ammo in the U.S. began in 1892, and factory ammunition is still listed by several firms, including Federal, Hornady, Remington, and Winchester. The bullet diameter is either 0.310, 0.311, or 0.312 inch, depending on the manufacturer. A company representative told me that the stalking rifle’s bore is for “0.311-inch ammo.”
Modern domestic loads for the .303 feature 150-, 174-, and 180-grain bullets. Federal lists velocities at 2,690 fps (150-grain) and 2,460 fps (180 grain) bullets. The 150-grain load from Hornady is listed at 2,685 fps. In addition to a traditional 180-grain load at 2,460 fps, Remington offers a 174-grain “Metal Case” bullet at 2,475 fps and the 180-grain softpoint at 2,460 fps. The point is there is a decent selection of factory-loaded ammo.
Of course, the .303 British cartridge is a prime candidate for handloading. Bullets from Speer and Sierra are 0.311 inch in diameter, Hornady’s mike 0.312 inch. I pulled a bullet from a sample round from each of the six factory loads, and all measured 0.310 except for the one load I had from Hornady, which measured 0.312 inch.
Most loading manuals have recipes with current powders for the .303. The Hodgdon Annual Manual and the Hornady and Speer manuals have dozens of loads. The maximum average pressure (MAP) for the .303 in the U.S. is set by SAAMI at 45,000 CUP and 49,000 psi, in deference to the many older rifles still in service, and the data shown in the accompanying chart adhere to that standard. While the Courteney rifle is based on the strong Model 1885 action, you should resist the urge to hot-rod handloads, as the case design is still from long ago. Speaking of cases, my buddy Drew Holtz of Double K Brass located 100 new factory primed Frontier (Hornady) cases.
I used Redding dies in a Redding T-7 turret press to assemble handloads, and all powder charges were weighed on an RCBS Model 1500 electronic scale. For a cartridge of this vintage, there is a surprisingly large group of component bullets available. The aforementioned manuals state the cartridge overall length (COL) for their loads, but Guns & Ammo writer Dave Emary told me many moons ago that bullet pull is critical. So for my handloads, I seated the bullets so that the base of the bullet was about even with the start of the neck. Thus, the COLs of my handloads are somewhat shorter than those listed in the manuals. Also, my loads tested three bullet diameters: 0.310 inch, 0.311, and 0.312 inch, but there were really no differences in the group sizes by bullet diameter. The 0.311-inch handloads averaged 1.89 inches, and the 0.312-inch loads averaged a flat 2.00 inches. The factory loads (all 0.310 inch except for Hornady’s 0.312-inch load) averaged 2.06 inches. In my tests, bullet diameter was a non-factor.
Steve found the .303 British Courteney Stalking Rifle to be nicely accurate and the cartridge to be more than adequate for deer-size game. (Steve Gash photo)
To assess ballistic uniformity, I calculated the coefficient of variation (COV) for factory ammo and handloads. COV is the standard deviation (SD) expressed as a percentage of the average velocity. COV allows an apples-to-apples comparison of velocities. The average COV for all handloads was 0.9 percent, and it was 0.8 percent for factory loads. Those numbers are very good. Since I pulled a bullet from each of the factory loads, I also weighed their powder charges and calculated their recoil, right along with the handloads. The rifle weighed exactly 7 pounds, and the average recoil was a very tolerable 14.6 ft-lbs. That’s not much more than the classic .30-06.

The .303 British has an enviable place in world history and cartridge development. Shooting the .303 allows modern riflemen to tap into that historical mystique. Long live the .303 British.
