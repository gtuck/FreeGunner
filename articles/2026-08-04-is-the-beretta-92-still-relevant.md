Is the Beretta 92 Still Relevant?

The Combat Handgun That Refuses to Fade Away

 The Beretta Model 92 has been around since 1976 and continues to evolve as users’ needs change. (Robert Sadowski photo)

August 04, 2026

By Robert Sadowski

The Beretta Model 92 offers legendary reliability and exceptional shootability. It is a classic as much as it is iconic, and if you read between the lines that’s code for old and outdated. It is true that the 92 has endurance and longevity, but the reality is we live in a polymer-frame, striker-fire, 9mm world, and it begs the question: Is the Beretta 92 still relevant?
Evergreen Design
The Model 92 was introduced in 1976 and was quickly adopted by military organizations and law enforcement departments around the globe. Since then, the 92 has evolved into a long line of models with a letter suffix following the model number: 92S, 92SB, 92F, 92FS, 92D, 92G—there’s a family tree of model 92s. Beretta calls the 92 line of handguns, 90 Series of pistols.
To write off the 92 as an old design does not do the platform justice. You really need to know the Model 92’s history and how it outlasted legions of other handgun designs. The fact the pistol is still in production today demonstrates that the 92 is an evergreen design, just like the Colt 1911, CZ-75, SIG P226, Glock G17, and Browning Hi-Power. Common to all of these evergreen pistols is the fact they were designed as a full-size combat pistol, they were then chopped down for conceal carry and modified for competition.
Unique to the Beretta Model 92S and all series 90 Berettas is the open top slide. (Robert Sadowski photo)
Wonder Nine Ground Zero: Beretta 92
The early 1980s was the Wonder Nine era causing a seismic movement in combat handgun design. The 92 was at ground zero. Wonder Nines were a type of handgun equipped with a DA/SA trigger, double stack magazine, metal frame, and ran on a diet of 9mm ammo. These pistols offered maximum firepower in a full-size pistol and drew the attention not only of the shooting public, but law enforcement and the U.S. military.

The 92 is the textbook definition of a Wonder Nine. Typical semiauto handguns back then were single stacks using a single action trigger. Smith & Wesson, SIG, Walther, Ruger, and other handgun manufacturers saw the direction the world was heading. It was as if all the handgun companies in the world were drinking from the same Kool Aid pitcher. Then there were brands like Glock that jumped on the Wonder Nine band wagon and rewrote the definition of a combat handgun. Those designs, however, lacked the reputation that the Beretta 92 was building.
Hollywood Discovers the Beretta
Movies like “Die Hard” and “Lethal Weapon,” gave the Model 92 a foothold in pop culture. While Mel Gibson and Bruce Willis blasted bad guys with their 92s, LE and the military were rethinking their standard issue sidearm.
LE departments from Connecticut to California geared up with the Beretta 92 in the ‘80s, as did the U.S. Border Patrol. Uncle Sam joined the party and adopted the pistol in 1985, renaming it the M9. Model 92 earned a reputation as an accurate, extremely reliable, and is easily controlled pistol. I have a couple of former LE pals that absolutely loved the 92 and cringed when their agencies adopted Glocks and SIGs. But—there’s always a but.

On the streets, LE found the 9mm cartridge at that time wasn’t up to the job. Ammo technology lagged behind handgun development. The caliber was dropped faster than you can say .40 S&W Auto, and the Model 92 was sold as surplus. The Beretta Model 96, the .40 Auto version of the 92, never really caught on. Technology has since caught up with the 9mm caliber and the .40 Auto was dropped, making a full circle. U.S. military felt the 92/M9 was showing its age and in 2017 replaced the pistol for the SIG P320 a polymer frame striker-fire pistol chambered in 9mm.
That has led us to where we are now. Generations of shooters who cut their teeth on striker-fire, polymer frame 9mm pistols. Hand them a metal frame pistol with a DA/SA trigger and they will look at you same way when you hand them a rotary dial telephone.
The M9A4 slide has been reworked to easier perform a press check as well as mount an optic. (Robert Sadowski photo)
Old Technology or Adaptive Design?
Is the Model 92 quirky to run? Let’s just say it is different. Every platform has its own idiosyncrasies, and the 92 gets beat up over its open slide design and slide-mounted safety. These complaints are really from users who want to run the 92 like 1911, Glock, S&W or nearly any other semi auto pistol. Other complaints about the 92 are also common to many double stack handguns; a fat grip. Let’s start with the slide.
Unique to the Model 92 is the open top slide. Beretta invented the feature back in 1915. Think of the open top slide as a massive ejection port. It is nearly impossible to get a stove pipe jam or any other jam with the design, but if you do experience a jam, it is easily cleared with a swipe of your hand. While the open top design reduces weight, palm the slide during a reload after an extended training session and you can sear your hand faster than a gas station hot dog. Use the slide release during a reload. That’s the reason it is there.
Slide serrations on the 92 are another gripe. There are located at the rear of the slide and the open top design doesn’t offer a lot of real estate for serrations so grasping the slide to reload or do a press check is tricky. The scooped-out sides of the slide at the muzzle allow a typical style press check, but users get weirded out because their fingers are close to the muzzle. Beretta addressed the lack of serrations on 
newer versions of the 92/M9 called the M9A4 pistols
. New forward slide serrations allow a user to grasp the slide from under the pistol rather than over it to perform procedural manipulations.

Mounting an optic wasn’t a consideration when the 92/M9 was designed, but today a pistol optics are nearly mandatory. Beretta’s M9A4 and 92X/92XI pistols all come optic ready. It’s a good thing Beretta added the forward slide serrations because operating the slide from the rear is crowded with an optic mounted. Like I suggested, learn to run the slide release.
The last complaint users have with the slide is the location of safety lever. Rack the slide back during a reload and you can easily engage the safety lever without realizing it. The 92G fixed this by converting the safety into a decocker, so worst case scenario is the gun is accidentally placed in DA mode. On newer guns, Beretta changed the contour of the safety switch so that is less likely to happen. New 92X and 92XI pistols have a frame mounted safety similar in location as a 1911, which in my opinion changes the dynamic of running a Beretta 92 for the better. The irony is the original 92 design located the safety lever on the frame. The Italian military wanted it on the slide. Now let’s look at the trigger.
For generations trained on a striker fire trigger, a DA/SA is an oddity. The original intent was to design a semi auto pistol with a trigger similar to a double action revolver. When wheel guns were in all LE holsters, officers were trained to start pressing the trigger as soon as their hand was on the gun and stage the trigger. When it came time to shoot the remaining pull through was light, almost like shooting it in single-action pistol. Or if the threat goes away the trigger was easily released and returned to DA mode. DA/SA triggers are designed to be fired with full intent and not accidentally fired. The new Xtreme-S trigger found on many current 92 models is DA/SA and fully adjustable for take-up, break, and over travel, and allows the user to fine-tune it to their preference.
Now the grip. Girth is good on some things, but a pistol grip, not so much. Users with small hands find it uncomfortable and have a hard time firing pistols with fat grips. Beretta made a fix by creating the slim, straight back strap Vertec grip on many 92 models. Those with gorilla sized hands can use the wrap around grip module that goes over the Vertec style frame on newer 92 guns.
The features of the 92 require specific training. You can’t apply all that you have learned from a SIG, 1911, S&W and other closed top slide designs and apply it to the Beretta 92.
Popularity of the Beretta 92
I frequent a lot of gun stores and there is always one 92 in the case surrounded by SIGs, Caniks, Glocks, S&Ws, Rugers and all the other popular plastic guns. I often wondered why a gun that was in service with our armed forces for 32 years isn’t more popular? Next time you go to a gun range, notice if there are any Beretta 92s. Could it be the price point of the 92 is nearly double that of a popular striker-fire gun? Probably.
Beretta 92 Clones
When the popularity of the 92 was high, other countries licensed the design. Girsan in Turkey produces a 92 clone called the Regard MC series and adds finger grooves on the front strap. Taurus in Brazil still produces 92 knockoffs with the same model name, and with the safety lever located on the frame. Imitation, I’m told, is the sincerest form of flattery, and it is obviously profitable for Girsan and Taurus to produce Model 92 clones. These 92s are as genuine as a Chinatown Rolex, but they perform nearly as well as bonafide Berettas.
Langdon Tactical Beretta 92 Elite LTT is customized for today’s conceal carry users with a full redesign of the internal components and a low mount optic. (Robert Sadowski photo)
Langdon Tactical tuned the typical 92 into an extraordinary pistol with a buttery smooth trigger pull with help from his special trigger bar. Optics are mounted low making them more user friendly. To get behind a Langdon Tactical and you will experience everything good about the 92 with enhancements that create a remarkable soft shooting, performance driven gun. It’s a better Beretta.

Why The Beretta 92 Still Matters
The fixed barrel on the 
92 offers inherent accuracy and makes it a smooth cycling, soft shooter
. The magazine design is one of the best ever conceived. It takes to a suppressor well. The 92’s reputation was earned serving law enforcement and the military. The pistol is customizable and in a constant state of evolution, adapting to the new shooter needs. The Beretta 92 is and always will be relevant as long as there is a need for a proven, reliable, performance driven pistol.
