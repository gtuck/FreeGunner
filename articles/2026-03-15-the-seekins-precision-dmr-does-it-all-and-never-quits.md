The Seekins Precision DMR Does it All and Never Quits

Say goodbye to flyers with a rifle that's so consistent that accuracy comes down to the user alone.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

March 15, 2026

By Guns & Ammo Staff

Anyone who has spent time shooting groups with an AR-­15 has experienced unexplained flyers. Everything was done right and each shot felt good, but the walk downrange or glance through a spotting scope revealed otherwise. Oftentimes, the mystery is followed by the phrase, “Well, it’s a gas gun.”
The handguard attachment point to the upper receiver was designed for minimul influence on the barrel’s harmonics, reducing potential flyers. The flat bottom is good for support aids. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
An errant round could have devastating consequences, whether you’re a law enforcement professional, hunter or competitive shooter. Seekins Precision figured out that the most frequent cause of inconsistent accuracy in ARs is usually traced to the fitment of the upper receiver, barrel and handguard. What Seekins’ engineers found is that there is often an inconsistent force applied to the handguard when shooting prone or a supported position. 
With eight total brake ports, the ATC diverts gas left and right of the muzzle. Ports out the muzzle are a flow-through design that reduce flash. A self-timing nut is included. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
As with most AR-pattern firearms, the handguard is attached to the barrel nut, which also secures the barrel to the upper receiver. Truthfully, the barrel is not free floating, so the harmonics change when the rifle is fired. Recognizing what causes flyers, Seekins Precision set out to design an AR-­15-type rifle that delivers consistent accuracy. The result became 
its Dedicated Marksman Rifle (DMR).
 This use of the acronym “DMR” is a tribute to the Designated Marksman Rifles issued to the U.S. military, but the term has become one that more frequently refers to a class of firearms.
M-Lok slots offer ample room to accessorize the DMR while venting heat. The full-length top rail accepts clip-on night vision devices that pair in tandem with a receiver-mounted optic. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Spec’d Out
It is not just one specific part that enables accuracy. It is a combination of factors, such as quality components, design and assembly. Seekins Precision offers several configuration choices in the DMR line. There are three different cartridge choices to choose from, including .223 Wylde/5.56 NATO, .22 ARC and 6mm ARC. These are available in a 16-­ or 18-­inch barrel. G&A’s test rifle was chambered in .223 Wylde for an 18-­inch barrel.

The DMR receiver feature aesthetically pleasing flats and beveled edges with notches, reliefs and slots for controls such as the bolt release, magazine release, safety selector and trigger. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The muzzle of the DMR is fitted with a Seekins Precision ATC muzzlebrake. This muzzlebrake was primarily designed to reduce felt recoil on the shooter. The brake does not directly make a rifle accurate. However, the reduction in perceived recoil allows a shooter to focus more on executing the fundamentals properly. The barrel that it is attached to does have a direct influence on accuracy, however.
The barrel of G&A’s DMR sports a 1-in-8-­inch twist rate and is made from 5R 416 stainless steel. The barrel is also equipped with the Seekins low-profile adjustable gas block for shooting suppressed or tuning the action to run specific loads of ammunition reliably.
Surrounding the barrel is the SP3Rv3 handguard with numerous M-­Lok attachement slots and a rail on top. The trapezoidal shape of the handguard has a wide flat bottom that is advantageous for resting or supporting the rifle on a flat surface or bags. Whether on the hood of a vehicle, a tripod in a blind, or a bag on a barricade, the forend increases stability.

Seekins Precision Definitely Not Ambi (DNA) charging handle looks ambi, but only the left lever releases the handle from the receiver to rack the bolt carrier. The other wing offers leverage. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
To address errant flyers, Seekins’ engineers made the front of the upper thicker than the standard AR-15-pattern upper. The barrel and barrel nut are locked into the receiver, and the handguard attaches to the outside of the upper. The design reduced the chance of incidental contact against the barrel and doesn’t disrupt a barrel’s harmonics.
Excluding the magazine release button, the safety selector and bolt release lever controls are bilateral. The Magpul MOE+ grip features slick sides, grooved frontstrap and internal storage. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The controls for the DMR are bilateral — for the most part. There is no ambi mag release button, which isn’t that big of a deal for left-handed shooters. The Definitely Not Ambi (DNA) charging handle is also right-hand biased, though appearances would lead us to believe that it is. Setting this rifle up with a low-power variable optic (LPVO) or other magnified optic could prove challenging for a left-handed shooter, but does it affect accuracy? Not necessarily. Yes, manipulation of the DMR would be less efficient for southpaws.
The Magpul PRS Lite stock balances rigidity, weight reduction and adjustability for prone shooting or using the rifle on a benchrest. Slings can be attached using slots or QD swivels. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A Magpul PRS Lite stock is what brings up the rear of the rifle. It requires a tool to adjust length of pull (LOP), and it is equipped with an adjustable cheekriser. This type of stock may be limited in LOP adjustments, but it eliminates the wobble commonly associated with fast collapsible stocks.
At The Range
The DMR was tested with two different optics. Accuracy testing was done with a 
Zero Compromise
 ZC420 4-­20x50mm. A Trijicon VCOG 1-­8x28mm ($3,136, trijicon.com) was also utilized. The best group was achieved with the 
Hornady
’s Varmint Express featuring a 55-­grain V-­Max bullet. The .25-inch group from 100 yards resulted with an average velocity of 2,808 feet per second (fps). As far as consistent accuracy, Black Hills’ 77-­grain Open Tip Match (OTM) and Winchester 69-grain match loads were most consistent.
There are no surprises in the bolt carrier group, just quality metal for durability. The bolt head is enlarged for the 6 ARC. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
During accuracy testing, the DMR’s mild recoil stood out. For each round sent downrange, the recoil impulse felt soft, more like a push than a punch. This soft recoil made it easier to get back on target for another shot. The muzzlebrake was also not overly concussive, unlike other models that require doubling up on ear-pro.

The trigger was crisp with zero creep. The trigger pull was clean, and the reset was short. The trigger is a single stage, which we tested to an average 3 pounds, 7 1/2 ounces.
The front of the magazine well can be used as a solid barricade stop or as a place to grip the DMR. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Knowing the DMR is capable of repeatable and consistent accuracy, the rifle was evaluated for versatility. The purpose of any DMR is to bridge the gap between combat rifle and sniper rifle. So, the Seekins was tested in shooting drills that would be considered close-­quarter distances with the 1-8X VCOG attached. One drill that showed the versatility of the DMR was SGM (Ret.) Kyle Lamb’s VTAC Half-and-Half drill. The drill was slightly modified from its original format: The first string of fire was shot 10 rounds in 10 seconds at 20 yards. The next string of 10 shots was fired at the 10 yard line in 5 seconds. The last string of 10 shots was completed at the 5-­yard line in 21/2 seconds. The acceptable zone of accuracy was the A-­zone on the Skeletor side of a 
VTAC double-sided target
. There was no issue with making the time hacks using Seekins’ DMR. Just two shooter induced rounds landed just slightly to the right of the A-­zone. This occurred at the 10-­yard line.
The lower receiver is constructed from 7075 series billet aluminum. It houses a single-stage Seekins trigger group. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
How the DMR felt while shooting this drill was a soft recoil impulse that kept the sight movement to a minimum. The trigger, being light and crisp with a short reset, made the rifle feel as though it wanted to go fast. However, this can cause some shooters to outrun their abilities, so practice is recommended.
Two issues that arose when shooting the DMR at close distances came from the stock and handguard. The fixed-length stock can present a problem with some smaller-­statured shooters. It could easily be replaced with another collapsible AR-pattern stock though. Some might find the shape of the handguard to be difficult to use, also especially when shooting standing and unsupported.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 
Trijicon VCOG 1-­8X LPVO
 performed well, but most drills were shot at 1X and at full illumination. The glass was clear and bright, and the reticle was intuitive to use at 1X. The reticle was not red-­dot bright, but illuminated or not, it’s ideal for closer targets.
Parting Shot
Seekins Precision’s DMR delivered on accuracy and reliability. Consistency was achieved by the stiff upper receiver and attaching the handguard in a way that created a truer free-­float barrel. Flyers may never be blamed on these rifles. Consistency now falls on us, the shooter, and our choice of ammunition. In a marksman’s hands, the DMR exceeds expectations.
