Pulsar Thermion 3 LRF XT60 Riflescope: Reviewed

The successor to the Thermion 2 is among the best thermal riflescopes on the market. Meet the all-new Thermion 3. 

 A Seekins Havoc Element in 6.5 PRC was used for testing.

November 11, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

The thermal optic market is growing rapidly, but one brand stands as the leading manufacturer of thermals—Pulsar. Each year, the team of engineers at Pulsar continues to push the boundaries of thermal optic technology, and one of their recent flagship products has been the Thermion 2. Though it looked similar to traditional riflescopes, the Thermion 2 was brimming with high-tech features that made it one of the versatile rifle optics ever offered on the commercial market. Since its release in the spring of 2021, the Thermion 2 has won just about every award that gun writers could bestow upon it.
When word trickled down that Pulsar was working on 
the Thermion 3
, I couldn’t imagine what they would change. The Thermion 2 comes with a built-in laser rangefinder, an outstanding processor that provides clear images, and long battery life. But I’m not privy to the latest advancements in thermal technology, and I’m not as savvy as the engineers at Pulsar. They had a vision for the third-gen Thermion, and they made that vision a reality.
The battery is stored just under the turrets, and is rated for 8 hours of use in the high-resolution versions of the Thermion 3. Lower resolution models have 14 hours of power on a full battery.
Thermion 3: Seeing Thermal in a New Light
The world of thermals can be confusing for new buyers who may not understand which features make the difference between a $700 and $7,000 thermal. Sensor resolution is a sensible place to begin with, and the Thermion 3 is equipped with a 1280x1024 HD thermal sensor. To put it plainly, that means that the Thermion 3 XT60 has four times the thermal pixels as a standard 640x512 sensor, and the end result is crisp, clear images that show amazing detail even at higher magnifications. It’s the same 1280/1024 12-micron sensor used in other Pulsar products like the Telos laser range finding XT50 monocular, and if you’ve ever used that model or have seen videos or photos captured by the Telos then you understand the class-leading resolution. With the release of the Thermion 3, that same level of clarity is available in a Pulsar thermal riflescope.
1280x1024 12-micron sensors are class-leading, so why did it take Pulsar so long to release an optic with that sensor to the consumer market? I posed that question to Pulsar’s Kevin Reese. A Marine veteran, outdoor writer and avid hunter and shooter, Reese has had the opportunity to work with some of the world’s best thermal optics, and he says that Pulsar’s commitment to quality is what precluded a rush to market with the Thermion 3.

“Pulsar’s slogan is ‘Image. Quality.’, and they take that seriously,” Reese told me. “The engineers weren’t going to bring the Thermion 3 to market until the image quality was exceptional and everything worked flawlessly.”
Controls sit on a turret just above the main eye lens of the scope.
For those unfamiliar with the finer aspects of thermal optic performance, the 1280x1024 12-micron sensor in the Thermion 3 is best-in-class. You won’t find a higher resolution thermal scope that’s commercially available on the market at the time of writing. The sensor is made by Lynred in Isere, France, a leading supplier of premium sensors to the military and consumer market the world over. Having a Lynred sensor in a thermal optic is the equivalent of precision-ground Austrian glass. And it’s not just the sensors that are sourced from Europe. Pulsar has manufacturing facilities in Latvia and Lithuania, so all the major components of these optics are manufactured and assembled in state-of-the-art European facilities.

NETD, or Noise Equivalent temperature Difference, is a measure of the smallest temperature difference a thermal sensor can detect, and it’s expressed in millikelvins. The lower the NETD, the better the sensitivity of a thermal riflescope for detecting variations in heat. The problem, though, is that there is no standardized method of testing NETD across thermal platforms. Some cheaper thermal optics have used this to exaggerate their actual NETD figures as observed through the shooter’s eye, Kevin Reese told me. Lynred, however, measures the actual NETD figures as the shooter observes them, so when Pulsar products promise class-leading <20 mK sensitivity owners can be assured that those figures are accurate.
To zoom in with the thermal, use the diopter just ahead of the main control island, much like increasing the magnification on an analog scope.
If microns and millikelvins sound like novel units of measurement, understand this: Pulsar’s Thermion 3 offers images that are high-definition with superb sensitivity. The difference between thermal riflescopes comes down to fine details. With standard 640 sensors you’ll identify a feral hog at 50 yards. With 1024 resolution you’ll see finer detail. With the Thermion 3’s 1280x1024 12micron sensor with <20 mK NETD, you’ll see the wild hog in high-definition clarity.

Many thermals will probably allow you to put some sausage in the freezer if you’re shooting hogs at fifty paces. Shooting targets at 300, 400, 500 yards or more in total darkness is what separates premium optics like the Thermion 3 from competitors. Pulsar promises a detection range of over 3,000 yards from the Thermion 3 XT60. I couldn’t reach quite that far during testing due to terrain limitations, but I could identify objects across a sweeping valley at a range of over 2,000 yards in total darkness. If you’re interested in long-range precision shooting at targets, hogs, or coyotes at distances stretching to a half mile or more, then the Thermion 3’s resolution and sensitivity aren’t just a luxury, they’re necessary. I could not only see animals from 2,000 yards, but I could also make out the necessary detail to determine exactly what I was looking at.
The LRF XT60 variant of the Pulsar Thermion 3 boasts a 1280x1024 resolution, shown above. Alternate thermal profiles include black and white, inverted black and white, and multi spectrum.
There’s also a 1,300-yard laser rangefinder built into the Thermion 3, so with the touch of a button you can range deer-sized objects from roughly three-quarters of a mile away (or further—I reached 1,245 yards with limited target options, but early reports from Pulsar staffers have indicated that it’s possible to range objects over 1,300 yards consistently). The laser rangefinder isn’t the only tool in the Thermion 3’s ballistic toolbox. This thermal riflescope offers Bluetooth connectivity that allows shooters to connect their Thermion 3 to the Stream Vision 2 app on their device so that they can input ballistic data for their favorite loads. Doing so simply requires downloading the app, building your ballistic profile (either from the supplied list of rifle ammunition or by inserting parameters manually), and then connecting your device to the scope. When the Thermion 3 begins communicating wirelessly with the Stream Vision 2 app on your phone, you can activate the chosen ballistic profile and send that data directly to the Thermion 3.
Once the ballistic profile is activated, you simply need to range the object and the Thermion 3 provides a recommended aiming point (displayed as a red X on your Thermion 3 screen) and correction values. You can also activate weather data to correct for environmental conditions.

Advanced Technology That’s Immediately Accessible
There’s no question that the Thermion 3 XT60 is one of the most advanced thermal riflescopes ever released, but none of that built-in brilliance is of any real value unless you can quickly access the Thermion 3’s various menus and features efficiently in total darkness. To my mind, the Thermion 3’s ease of use is what makes it stand out from the crowd. The world’s most advanced thermal riflescope also happens to be one of the easiest thermals to operate in the field.
The top knob above the scope is used to change focus, rather than elevation.
I won’t delve too deeply into the manual of operation, but for practical purposes I’ll explain how to access the features on this scope you’re likely to need during an average night of predator or pig hunting. The primary menu controls are positioned on an island near the lens of the scope. On this island you have three primary buttons—a menu button, a back button, and a record button. The outer ring of the island is a dial that rotates to easily access the menus. The power button is neatly positioned on the side of the island so that you can instantly power the unit up or down without the aid of a light.
Once the unit is powered on, the control layout is simple to use. The record button allows the shooter to immediately record video of stills, and a long press of the button toggles between the two modes. There’s also recoil-activated video feature that automatically records video when the unit senses recoil.
The main menu button allows users to toggle between the main and quick menu settings with a long press. The quick menu feature allows for rapid adjustments to brightness, contrast, brightness, amplification, and distance, all of which can be adjusted using the wheel. The return button allows you to adjust picture-in-picture and palettes, using a long hold to toggle between these features as well. Display options include white hot, black hot, green, red hot, red monochrome, ultramarine, violet, rainbow, and sepia, so there are a multitude of display option choices. Picture-in-picture modes allow the activation or deactivation of the PIP setting. Main menu settings allow you to fine-tune the optic by adjusting ballistic profiles, activating between WiFi and Bluetooth, selecting among 10 different reticle design and 10 reticle colors, and much more.  
The profile of the Thermion 3 series is very similar to a normal daytime riflescope, meaning mounting and using the device is quite intuitive.
Focusing is simple and straightforward. To bring the reticle and display info into focus, rotate the diopter on the eyecup. To focus on the object being viewed, simply rotate the top knob (where the elevation turret would be located on a traditional day scope) until clear focus is achieved.
Battery life is an important consideration when selecting an optic. The Thermion 3 ships with a pair of APS5 batteries, a battery charger, and cable. Since the optic’s battery can be charged via a USB type-C power cable, it’s possible to charge the optic and the spare battery at once with two cables, and since most vehicles offer USB charging ports that means you can make certain that your primary and spare batteries are at full charge, even when you’re in the field. With a maximum battery life of 8.5 hours, that means with a spare battery in the pocket the Thermion 3 will run for over 17 hours. The battery compartment can be accessed by rotating the right knob on the optic, and there’s a USC charging port on the left knob.
Mounting and Zeroing
Like most hunters, I have spent most of my life hunting with traditional daylight scopes, and as such I was never a fan of boxy thermal optics that look like miniature toaster ovens planted on top of the rifle. The Thermion 3 looks and feels very much like a traditional scope with a 30mm main tube and a belled 60mm objective lens. The diopter adjusts just like a day scope, and even the knobs mimic the placement of traditional rifle turrets.
Because it comes with a 30mm main tube, mounting the Thermion 3 is similar to existing day scopes. With a pair of Picatinny-style 30mm rings you can quickly and securely mount the optic to your rifle, though the test optic came with a quick-release cantilever-style mount from Pulsar. Simply position the scope, lock the levers and the scope is mounted. It’s also easy to swap between rifles using the cantilever mounting system. Ring screws should never be tightened more than 22.1 inch-pounds to prevent damage to the optic.

While mounting the Thermion 3 is similar to a normal scope, zeroing it is different. To begin, choose a zero distance, align the crosshairs, and fire. Using the menu, you can then align the auxiliary cross (a red X accessed in the menu) with the point of impact. You’ll then adjust zeroing coordinates and save them. For subsequent shots, point of aim should coincide with point of impact. To simplify zeroing, Pulsar includes a thermal zeroing target with every Thermion 3 optic.
Field Use
The tech that Pulsar has packed into the Thermion 3 is remarkable, and I appreciate how easy this optic is to zero and how accurate it is in the field. I mounted the optic on my 
Seekins Havak Element rifle in 6.5 PRC
, and within minutes I had zeroed the Pulsar. The quick start guide is an invaluable tool as it walks you through each process in plain English and includes diagrams. If you can use a smart phone or use the basic features on your home DVR system, you’ll have no issues operating the Thermion 3.
The full suite of thermal sensors is jam packed into a surprisingly compact package, given the profile of the Thermion 3.
One of the best features of the Thermion 3 is the magnification ring which operates similar to a day scope. With other thermal riflescopes, adjusting magnification requires pressing buttons, and the .5 adjustments are anything but smooth. The Thermion 3 has a base magnification of 2x and an 8x digital zoom and it boasts .1 magnification adjustments, so zooming in or out feels very much as it does with a day scope. It’s more familiar and less jarring than the magnification adjustments on other thermal riflescopes.
The image quality is superb. Having a 1280 sensor with 12-micron pixel pitch creates clear, vivid images even at extended ranges, and with so many different displays and reticles from which to choose you can truly customize this optic as desired. The Stream Vision 2 app works seamlessly with the optic, and I found that loading my .308’s ballistics was not difficult. What’s more, I could add ballistics for multiple other guns and switch ballistic profiles as needed.
The Thermion 3 is a class-leading thermal optic that’s ideal for shooting and hunting in any light conditions, and as you’d expect it carries a premium price. The MSRP for the optic rests around $6,500, but let’s put that price point into perspective. If this level of technology existed on the consumer market two decades ago it would cost much more, likely what a new car does today. And the Thermion 2 XL60 carries an MSRP of $7,200 with a 1024 sensor. The Thermion 3 is indeed a premium product, but the price of thermal technology has come down while the capabilities of new products has increased. That’s rare in any market.

In addition to a thermal target, the Thermion 3 comes with a wireless remote control as well as removable throw lever for the magnification ring, which is especially valuable when shooting in low light. It also includes both daytime and nighttime eye cups that and interchangeable.
The Thermion 3 isn’t for every shooter or hunter, but for those who want the best, this is the top choice in thermal rifle optics. A far cry from the cheap optics that come from some factories, the Thermion 3 is built in state-of-the-art European labs, and Pulsar’s warranty and customer service are in line with their premium place in the market. We’ve waited a while for the Thermion 3, but it is allowing us to view thermal riflescopes in an entirely new light.
