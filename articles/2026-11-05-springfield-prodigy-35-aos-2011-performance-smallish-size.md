Springfield Prodigy 3.5 AOS: 2011 Performance, Smallish Size

A double-stack 9mm that shrinks the duty-ready 2011 platform into an everyday carry powerhouse

 Springfield boosts the capacity of its shortest Prodigy double-stack, giving you the best of both worlds. (Yamil Sued photo)

November 05, 2026

By Richard Nance

Anyone with even a cursory knowledge of handguns knows that John Browning’s 1911 was chambered in .45 ACP, a cartridge he developed in conjunction with the pistol. The 1911’s ergonomic design, functionality and second-to-none, sliding trigger have cemented its place in firearms history.
But the 1911’s seven- to eight-round capacity seems anemic by today’s standards, and in recent years double-stack 9mm-chambered 1911s have become increasingly popular because they offer the best of both worlds: 1911 shootability and performance, with a capacity rivaling that of a modern, striker-fired pistol.
A case in point is the Springfield 1911 DS Prodigy AOS. Introduced in 2022 with 4.25- or five-inch barrels, it shipped with a 17-round flush-fit and a 20-round extended magazine. In early 2025, the 1911 DS Prodigy line grew to include two different Compact models, one with a 3.5-inch barrel and one with a 4.25-inch barrel. Both came with 15-round flush-fit magazines.
The longer grip accommodates a higher capacity magazine, providing the defensive shooter with 18 rounds out of a relatively compact gun. (Yamil Sued photo)
Double-Stack
Now there’s a new version of the 3.5-inch Prodigy. This one features a full-size grip—upping the capacity from 15 to 18 rounds and dropping the “Compact” nomenclature.

“The new 1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS 9mm pistol offers the best of both worlds: the short and handy slide of a compact but the full 18+1 capacity of a full-size grip module,” explained Springfield’s Mike Humphries.
Three extra rounds might come in handy, but perhaps of more consequence—especially for those with larger hands—is the fact that the full-size grip gives you more to hang onto. This aids in recoil control for faster, and likely more accurate, follow-up shots. That’s worth noting because even those who live in restrictive states can benefit from this new Prodigy configuration, as the pistol is offered in a version with 10-round magazines. But you still get that added control provided by the longer grip.
Features
The 
1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS
 is seven inches long and 1.5 inches wide across the ambidextrous thumb safety. The longer grip boosts height to 5.5 inches compared to the 3.5 Compact, and at 1.4 inches the grip is just a hair wider than a traditional single-stack 1911. Weight is 29.3 ounces, three to four ounces lighter than the full-size Prodigy models.

For those unfamiliar with the platform, the 1911 DS Prodigy has many variants including compensated models, optic-equipped models and gear-bundled models—even a Firstline program with discounted offerings on select models for specified first responders. Despite the array of offerings, all 1911 DS Prodigy pistols share a slew of notable features.
The Prodigy’s frame is constructed of billet 7075-T6 hardcoat anodized aluminum, with a Cerakote finish and polymer grips. Springfield’s Adaptive Grip Texture, which consists of staggered dual-height pyramids, locks the pistol to your hands for optimal control without abrading.
The three-hole trigger is excellent, and the trigger guard is not only undercut for a better hold, but also squared off and serrated for those who wrap a finger there. (Yamil Sued photo)
The Adaptive Grip Texture wraps around the grip 360 degrees. There’s even a bit of texturing on the front of the trigger guard for those who prefer to wrap the index finder of their non-shooting hand around it to minimize muzzle flip.
The top of the frontstrap is smooth, providing a natural index for your middle finger. The oversize, rectangular trigger guard will easily accommodate a gloved finger.
The undercut trigger guard facilitates a high hold on the Prodigy for optimal recoil control. Scalloping on the bottom of the trigger guard helps lock the middle finger of your shooting hand and the index finger of your support hand in place.

Prodigy pistols feature a Picatinny rail on the dust cover for mounting a light, laser or combo light/laser unit. While optics have largely replaced lasers on pistols, being able to attach a light to a defensive pistol is an important consideration.
All Prodigy pistols sport a match-grade bull barrel with an 11-degree crown. The crown helps protect the barrel’s rifling and adds a distinctly modern look. Unlike the traditional 1911 barrel design, the bull barrel doesn’t require a bushing to keep it properly aligned.
The bull barrel’s extra forward weight translates to less muzzle flip. Prodigy barrels are constructed of forged stainless steel, with a black DLC finish. The forged carbon steel slide features a Cerakote finish and front and rear serrations for easy racking.
The blacked-out, serrated rear sight is integral with the cover plate and the AOS adapter plates. It’s tall enough to co-witness with most optics. (Yamil Sued photo)
Sights and Optics
Prodigy slides feature the Agency Optic System (AOS), developed in collaboration with Agency Arms. The AOS offers plates that can accommodate an array of optics, including those with Trijicon RMR, Shield RMSc, Leupold DeltaPoint Pro, Aimpoint Acro and Docter/Noblex footprints.
The latter accommodates Springfield’s Hex Dragonfly optic as well as the Burris FastFire, Vortex Venom and others. And it is this adapter plate that’s included with the Prodigy 3.5 AOS. If that’s not the plate you want, the pistol also comes with a coupon to purchase one of the other plates for $50, which is a big discount from the $129 Springfield normally charges for a plate.
The billet steel AOS plates each have an integral rear sight. This, along with the pistol’s front sight, will co-witness with most optics. Despite the reliability of today’s pistol optics, having a redundant sighting system on your pistol is never a bad idea.
Prodigy sights also make a great standalone option. The black serrated U-notch rear sight allows you to pick up the front sight quickly. The front sight has a tritium insert surrounded by a white circle. It’s visible regardless of lighting conditions.
Controls include an ambidextrous thumb safety, and the grip safety’s beavertail is shortened for better concealment—with the hammer recontoured to match. (Yamil Sued photo)
Safeties and Controls
As you’d expect, the Prodigy has a grip safety, and this features a prominent memory bump that enables you to deactivate the safety more easily.

Advertisement

Browning’s original 1911 design had a thumb safety on the left side. The Prodigy, like many modern 1911s, has an ambidextrous thumb safety. Left-handed shooters will no doubt appreciate having a thumb safety on the right side of the Prodigy, but it doesn’t benefit only them. Injury may require a right-handed shooter to operate the Prodigy with their non-dominant hand.
The safety is large enough to manipulate under stress, even when wearing gloves. The safety clicks positively on and off, providing tactile confirmation of its status.
The recessed slide stop on the left side of the frame is easily accessible without being in the way. Serrations on the slide stop provide a non-slip surface for your thumb. Vertical serrations on the round magazine button on the left side of the pistol serve the same purpose.
Trigger and Beaver Tail
Despite being introduced 115 years ago, the 1911 trigger still reigns supreme. It’s a single-action trigger, meaning that when you press it, the hammer is released. Unlike most triggers that are hinged, the 1911’s trigger slides straight back, which translates to better on-target accuracy. The Prodigy’s curved, skeletonized trigger is adjustable for overtravel.
The 15-round Compacts and the new 3.5-inch 18-round model share two subtle differences that set them apart from their full-size brethren. The beavertails are shortened for better concealment, and the hammers are correspondingly recontoured to match.

Performance
Having shot several Prodigy variants, including the Compact 3.5, I knew what to expect from the new 3.5. The only difference between that pistol and the one I held in my hands was that the latter had a grip about a half inch longer to accommodate the 18-round magazines.
Even though the magazines are considered flush fit, they extend slightly below the magazine well. Scalloped areas on the magazine floorplates give you something to grab hold of when removing one from the pistol. When the gun is functioning normally, this is convenient. When clearing a double feed, where the magazine is held under the pressure of the recoil spring and must be ripped out of the pistol, this could be a lifesaver.
The 1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS looks and feels more like a custom 1911 than a stock one. Fit and finish is superb, with nary a blemish or sharp edge to be found. Not only is a smooth pistol more comfortable to shoot, but it’s less likely to snag on your cover garment when drawing from concealment.
I mounted a Trijicon RMR on the gun for testing. As you can see in the accompanying chart, the pistol seemed to prefer the heavier loads, but since bench rest accuracy tells only part of the story, I was interested to see how it performed in defensive-type shooting.
Shorter barreled handguns tend to generate a snappy recoil impulse because there is less weight up front. The 1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS was easy to control, largely because of its bull barrel, which adds weight toward the muzzle.

Running the 1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS at seven to 10 yards, I was able to deliver accurate hits in rapid succession. The pistol cycled smoothly, without a single hiccup. It felt well balanced with the longer grip and 3.5-inch slide. The dot on my RMR recovered predictably, shot after shot.
The 1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS has intuitive controls. It was easy to load thanks to the beveled magazine well. After inserting a magazine, I was able to reliably hit the slide stop lever with my thumb to send the slide home.
The 3.5-inch match grade bull barrel sports an 11-degree crown, and it’s a bull style that puts more weight out front for improved control. (Yamil Sued photo)
The pronounced memory bump on the grip safety enables you to fire the pistol even with an imperfect grip. The ambidextrous thumb safety works as well as any I’ve used. The magazine release button was easy to press without altering my grip.
But the star of the show is the trigger. It’s curved to mate well with the contour of your finger, and the face is serrated for better purchase. The trigger on my test gun was smooth and clean breaking, with minimal take-up and no discernible overtravel—hence no need for overtravel adjustment.
The trigger pull weight measured 3.5 pounds according to my Custom Gunsmithing tool. Trigger reset was short and distinct.

Final Thoughts
You might assume John Browing would hate the idea of a 9mm-chambered 1911, but I’m not so sure. He was, after all, at the forefront of innovation. One thing I can say with certainty is that I’m a fan and agree with Humphries when he says that the new 3.5-inch gun builds on the Prodigy platform’s strengths but adds what he calls a “unique and appealing configuration.”
Gone are the days of having to choose between a 1911 and a high-capacity pistol. Fans of the 1911, looking for a double-stack, feature-rich pistol that’s compact enough to carry yet big enough to shoot well are sure to appreciate the 1911 DS Prodigy 3.5 AOS from Springfield Armory. I know I did.
