The Benelli M2-­G1 is a New Shotgun Heavyweight

Dominate close-quarters with the Benelli M2-G1 semiautomatic shotgun.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

August 17, 2026

By Rich Nance

In close quarters, few firearms are as effective as a 12-­gauge, semiautomatic shotgun. Since the early 1900s, this platform has served the U.S. military, law enforcement and citizens and it continues to do so despite the popularity of AR-­15s, personal defense weapons (PDWs), and pistol caliber carbines (PCCs). As a young cop in the early 2000s, I remember our SWAT team had the option of a Benelli M4, which many preferred to the Vietnam-era Colt M16s we were issued.
At the core of the M2-G1 is the R.P.O., featuring dual short-stroke pistons under the barrel, near the chamber. These help the shotgun accommodate different loads and reduce fouling. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The M4 was easier and faster to shoot than the Remington Model 870 pump-­action carried in our patrol vehicles, but semiauto shotguns were considered finicky, especially when compared to the ultra-­reliable pump-­action. But the Benelli M4 was different.
The M4 used the Auto-­Regulating Gas-­Operated (A.R.G.O.) short-stroke system with dual self-­cleaning pistons for enhanced reliability and extended service life. In 1999, a version was adopted as the M1014 Joint Service Combat Shotgun, made famous by the U.S. Marines and is still in use by the military.
The pistol grip has a recoil-absorbing rubber overmold. It is textured and contoured to optimize control and hand position. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
On the commercial side, Benelli’s M4 Tactical Series includes the standard model, the M1014 Tactical (civilian version), and the EXT Tactical. While the standard model and the M1014 each have a five-plus-one capacity, the EXT has an extended magazine tube upping that to seven plus one. The EXT also has a skeletonized, telescoping stock.

Introduced in 2026, Benelli’s newest tactical shotgun is not an M4. It’s called the “M2-­G1.” Benelli fans may find this odd because the M2 Series has, until now, consisted of a Speed 3-­Gun model and 13 field models, with nary a tactical version in the lineup. 
Even a passing glance suggests that the M2-­G1 is a tactical shotgun. It has an extended magazine tube, prominent ghost-­ring sight set-up, pistol grip, and five-­position collapsible stock. (A version without a pistol grip will be available for those living in restrictive states.)
Ghost-ring sights, enlarged controls, and the MultiCam Black target operators. Even single-loading the large ejection port is ergonomic. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
What does “R.P.O.” stand for?
As a member of the M2 Series, you might assume the M2-­G1 is inertia driven, but it’s not. Nor does the M2-­G1 use the A.R.G.O. system like the M4 Series. Instead, the M2-­G1 features Benelli’s new Rear Piston Operated (R.P.O.) system. It has a lot in common with the A.R.G.O. concept, but it is entirely different than the inertia drive in every other M2.

Familiar to certain M4 models and the U.S. Marines’ M1014 shotgun, the M2-G1 features a five-position adjustable stock and grip. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Developed by Benelli in 1967, the inertia driven system proved simple, reliable and clean running. Rather than gas, it relies on the shotgun’s natural recoil energy to cycle the bolt. While the inertia system is great for sporting and field shotguns, it wasn’t perfect for tactical applications. Unlike most gas-­operated shotguns that use pistons to slow the action, inertia-driven shotguns tend to produce more felt recoil, too. In a duty or defensive scenario, recoil can slow the sight recovery between shots. During a long training session, extra recoil will also wear you out.
Push-button-release sling swivels are provided for a two-point configuration. When not in use, M-Lok slots can vent barrel heat. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
From a tactical standpoint, the biggest drawback to an inertia gun is that its operation is dependent on a proper shoulder mount. If you can’t mount the shotgun solidly, the inertia spring might not compress enough to cycle the bolt. This could induce a malfunction, comparable to limp-­wristing a pistol, which occurs when your grip doesn’t provide enough resistance for the slide to reciprocate.

Like the A.R.G.O. system in the M4, the R.P.O. system in the M2-G1 collects gas near the chamber. Both systems isolate the recoil-spring assembly, thereby protecting it from the gas system. The main differences between the two are that the A.R.G.O. system is auto regulated and uses self-­cleaning pistons. Hence, the R.P.O. system provides many of the benefits of the of A.R.G.O. at a reduced cost.
The Picatinny rail section on the receiver supports optic mounting, but it sits below the standard sights and can be removed. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The R.P.O system delivers cleaner operation, reduces fouling, and lowers maintenance requirements than traditional, forward-mounted gas systems. Like the A.R.G.O. system, the R.P.O system is backed by a 10-­year warranty.

The M2-­G1 has a 3-­inch chamber, so the magazine tube holds seven 23/4-inch shotshells for a capacity of eight with one chambered. The beveled loading port makes the M2-­G1 quick and easy to load.

The anodized aluminum receiver is sandwiched between the synthetic stock and forend. Its oversized controls make the M2-­G1 easier to operate under duress, when even simple tasks can seem overwhelming.
An often overlooked control on many tactical shotguns is a pistol grip, which can improve a shotgun’s handling and afford the shooter more control than a traditionally configured stock. Pistol grips and crossbolt safeties aren’t always a good pairing, though, because to manipulate the safety you often must compromise your grip.
The front sight is a post that aligns within the adjustable ghost-ring rear, flanked by a pair of white dots. Both are protected by wings. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
On the M2-­G1, I had to rotate my wrist to the right and the shotgun to the left to engage the safety, I was able to disengage it while maintaining my grip. The M2-­G1 has a rubberized, textured pistol grip that enhances control and dampens some felt recoil. 
Since warfighters and cops often wear gloves, a shotgun designed for military and law enforcement use should have an oversized triggerguard like that on the M2-­G1. 
The triggerguard houses a curved trigger that measured 6 to 6½ pounds according to my NRA universal trigger weight system. That’s a little stiff but it may lighten and smooth out during training use.
The loading gate is grooved and slotted, while the front of the triggerguard is aggressively scalloped for feeding shells by feel. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The M2-­G1 will be offered in grey, Flat Dark Earth (FDE), and a licensed MultiCam Black finish. Guns & Ammo received a sample in MultiCam Black for evaluation. The otherwise black shotgun is accented by a subtle MultiCam Black pattern on the receiver, barrel, and magazine tube. It gives the shotgun a high quality, tactical vibe without any eye-­catching glare and flair that could compromise the user’s position.
At 8 pounds, the M2-­G1 is 39½-inches long with an 18½-inch barrel. It’s solid but balanced. The five-­position, collapsible stock helps police or military especially where a shotgun may be shared by multiple users wearing layers of uniform and armor. Not only does a collapsible stock better fit users of different builds, it also enables them to adjust the length of pull according to the thickness of soft or hard armor. The M2-­G1’s stock is adjusted with the push of a square shaped button on the left side that confidently secures it in place.
M-Lok slots are not only available on the handguard, a slot is on both sides of a barrel/magazine clamp, an ideal location for a light. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The M2-­G1 forend differs from the M4 I’m familiar with, as well as other M2 offerings. It’s longer, with sides that are more heavily contoured. The forend’s shape and texture provide a comfortable, non-­slip gripping surface for enhanced comfort and control. Three M-­Lok slots are atop the forend for accessories, and three on either side including one to the rear. They provide ample space for mounting a light, laser, or other accessory without leaving wasted rail space to bulk up the handguard or add unnecessary weight. Even the barrel clamp has an M-­Lok slot on both sides.

Feeding the M2-G1
One of the main benefits of a shotgun is that it can fire an array of ammunition in 23/4- and 3-inch lengths. Benelli recommends that all Benelli semiautomatic 12-guage shotguns be used with field loads of 11/8 ounces or heaver and have a minimum muzzle velocity of 1,200 feet per second. A break-in period using 50 rounds of high-brass field loads is further recommended for optimal performance. 
A tactical shotgun does not often require a change of chokes, but a premium choke wrench is provided. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The most common load for a tactical shotgun is 00 buck. Using the removable Modified choke, I patterned the M2-G1 with Federal’s BallistiClean Law Enforcement Frangible 00 Buck at distances ranging from 5 yards to 25 yards. At 5 yards, all nine pellets were within 2½ inches. At 10 yards, the spread was 5 inches. At 15 yards, 8 inches. At 20 yards, 9 inches. And at 25 yards, 10 inches.
These groups were tighter than I expected, which is a good thing. However, these results proved that, despite popular opinion to the contrary, you must aim a shotgun. Just pointing in the general vicinity of the bad guy isn’t going to cut it. Depending on the situation, you may need more precision than 00 buck can provide. That’s where rifled slugs and optics and help with accuracy and distance. A rifled slug is a single projectile with impressive ballistics.
Oversized controls such as the charging handle and bolt-release button are intuitive to find and confidence-inspiring to operate. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Shooting offhand at 25 yards, I fired several Federal Power-­Shok 1-ounce rifled slugs. All impacted within a 8-inch head-size area on the target. The M2-­G1’s ghost-­ring sight system worked just fine but topping the rail with a red-dot sight simplifies aiming and enhances accuracy for many shooters. 
As expected, the Benelli M2-­G1 performed flawlessly. It’s smooth cycling and recoil is easily managed. Parkerized operating components provide corrosion resistance, while increasing lubricity for decreased wear and tear. The M2-­G1 is ready for action out of the box, but you may want to accessorize it.

The fire control assembly is in a polymer housing that contains the crossbolt safety, trigger, hammer, sear, bolt catch and loading gate. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Parting Shots
Any long gun intended for professional or defensive use needs a light. A light is critical on a tactical shotgun because you must be able to discern between a “shoot” or “no-­shoot” target, regardless of lighting conditions. A sling is also necessary for law enforcement and military use because it enables the user to safeguard the shotgun while keeping both hands free to perform other tasks. For home defense, a sling is optional. While not as critical as a light (or a sling for tactical operations), an optic provides a prominent, precise aiming point, helping to make aiming instinctual. Given the choice, I’d always opt for a red-dot sight over mechanical sights.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
Whether for professional use, defense or training, the M2-­G1 is a solid choice. It isn’t just tactical, it’s territorial. The intuitive, user-­friendly functionality and capacity will help you own the space your in.
