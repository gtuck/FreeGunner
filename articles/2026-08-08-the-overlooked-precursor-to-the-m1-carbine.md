The Overlooked Precursor to the M1 Carbine

The 1905 Winchester paved the way to the M1 .30 Carbine.

 Winchester Model 1905, .32 SL, est. 60% condition. (Photo by Dave Emary)

August 08, 2026

By Dave Emary

Mention the 1905 Winchester and most shooters will say, “The what?” It was a rather unsung rifle that could probably be credited with being one of the first semiautomatic, detachable-­magazine centerfire rifles. It was chambered in rather anemic cartridges and is limited in interest to hardcore Winchester collectors. The lack of power in the two cartridges for which the Model 1905 was chambered led to the development of the more popular and powerful Model 1907 and Model 1910 Winchester, both scaled-­up versions of the 1905 designed for more powerful cartridges. However, the 1905 Winchester and one of its cartridges played a large role during World War II through the 
M1 Carbine
 and .30 Carbine cartridge.
Nineteen O’Five
At the turn of the 20th century, interest in automatic firearms was increasing. The designs were generally all new and novel, and a little slow to catch on because most early semiautomatics could not match the accuracy of the newly introduced bolt-­action rifles or the long-­established lever-­actions. New semiautomatic firearms were usually expensive and initially not chambered in powerful or useful cartridges, other than the Remington Model 8.
Regardless, the evolution of automatic small arms would eventually play a major role and provide an advantage for U.S. troops in World War II. The 1905 Winchester was one of the first centerfire semiautomatic firearms. It was a blowback-­operated rifle developed by Thomas C. Johnson at Winchester and was available in either .32 Winchester Self-­Loading or .35 Self-­Loading cartridges. The rifle was an evolutionary development of Johnson’s earlier Model 1903 .22 rimfire. The Model 1903 features received a patent on August 27, 1901. The 1903 was a successful design that was initially chambered in the proprietary .22 Winchester Automatic rimfire cartridge and was later chambered in .22 Long Rifle (LR) and renamed the Model 63 in the 1930s.
The 1905 was one of the first rifles to feature a detachable box magazine, available in either five-­ or 10-­round capacities. The magazine release consists of a small tab that protrudes from the lower right side of the receiver that is pushed towards the magazine and then pulled out of the receiver. It is not exactly a quick release as the magazine fits tight. The rifle’s standard barrel length was 22 inches. The safety is a crossbolt push-­button at the front of the triggerguard, nearly identical to the original M1 Carbine safety. The buttstock is attached by a long screw that passes through the buttstock into the receiver. The forend houses a counterweight that is one piece with the bolt and the charging rod that protrudes from the front of the steel forend cap. The charging rod is spring loaded to the bolt and counterweight yet it does not recoil with the bolt and counterweight. The rifle is charged from the magazine by pushing the charging rod to the rear and releasing it. It can be fully pushed to the rear and turned a quarter turn to lock the bolt open. The bolt is a large, heavy piece and is contiguous with the counterweight under the forend. The rifle came standard with a ramp adjustable tangent leaf rear sight and a blade front sight. The tang was drilled for a folding Lyman or Marbles aperture tang sight, though receiver sights were also available. The 1905 was available as either a plain finish with a straight or pistol grip buttstock for $28 or a fancy finish with upgraded wood, pistol grip stock and checkering for $43. It was expensive when new, and probably a little bit of a hard sell for most buyers who were used to buying lever-­action rifles in that period for about $15 to $20. My 1905 was made in 1908 and weighs 7 pounds, 10 ounces. It has a plain finish stock with pistol grip and chambers the .32 SL.

The 1905 was considered a takedown rifle. There is a knurled screw at the rear of the receiver with a spring-­loaded retaining bar in the tang. When the retaining bar is pressed down, the knurled screw can be unscrewed and the trigger group, rear receiver bulkhead and stock slide straight back out of the main receiver to separate the gun into two pieces. As like most other firearms of that period, my 1905 has very good fit and finish, retaining the appearance and feel of a solid gun.
When compared side by side, the M1 Carbine trigger group (above, bottom), bears a striking resemblance to the 1905 trigger group (above, top). (Photo by Dave Emary)
Production ran from 1905 to 1920, with a total of 29,113 rifles manufactured. The 1905 garnered an early reputation as a reliable firearm, but quickly received negative press for its underpowered chamberings.
Model 1905 Cartridges
.32 SL he .32 SL fired a .321-­inch, 165-­grain bullet at approximately 1,400 feet per second (fps) from a 22-­inch barrel. The 1929 Winchester catalog listed a 165-­grain soft point at 1,390 fps, while the 1923 Remington catalog listed 165-­grain soft-­point and metal-­case bullets at 1,440 fps. The .32 SL was similar in performance to the period .32-­20 High Velocity (HV) loading. The cartridge operated at approximately 25,000 psi and had a cartridge overall length (COL) of 1.880 inches. It is hard to imagine that at a muzzle velocity of 1,400 fps that a soft-­point bullet would offer much expansion. The .32 SL was probably not effective on anything much bigger than a coyote at modest ranges.

.35 SL he .35 SL was loaded with .351-­inch, 180-­grain bullets at approximately 1,400 fps. The 1929 Winchester catalog listed 180-­grain soft-­point and full-­patch bullets at 1,395 fps, while Remington’s 1923 catalog listed 180-­grain soft-­point and metal-­case bullets at 1,450 fps. The overall cartridge length of the .35 SL is 1.650 inches, and maximum pressure was approximately 25,000 psi. The .35 SL was in the same league as a standard performance .38-­40 Winchester cartridge for power and trajectory. The muzzle velocity of 1,400 fps would have again made much expansion of the soft-­point bullet unlikely. Due to the larger diameter bullet, the .35 SL may have been effective on deer with well-­placed shots for 50 to 75 yards.
Both calibers of ammunition are a collector thing at this point. If you can find any, prices reflect that. Shooting the 1905 in either caliber is a senior-­level reloading project. You need access to a lathe of some sort to make the cases. Modified .32-­20 Winchester and .357 Magnum cases can be used for both chamberings, with rims turned to correct diameter and thickness. The correct extractor groove needs to be cut ahead of the rim as well as the case trimmed to length. A .321-­inch expander in a .32-­20 size die will make .32 SL cases out of the .32-­20 case. A .351 WSL full-­length size die is available 
from Redding
  that works fine for the shorter .35 SL case. A .32 H&R/.327 Federal shell holder works for the .32 SL and a 
Lee R19 shell holder
 is made for the .35 SL. In a pinch, a .32-­20 shell holder can be used for the .32 SL. Any .321-­inch .32 Special bullet works in the .32 SL. For the .35 SL, an inexpensive .351-­inch 180-­grain TMJ bullet is available from 
Graf & Sons
 and 
Buffalo Arms
. Soft-point hunting bullets are available for both the .32 and .35 SL from 
Hawk Bullets.
For shooting my 1905 in .32 SL, I made cases from .32-­20 cases by reducing the rim diameter and thinning the rim to specification and cutting a new extractor groove with my mini lathe. I simply ran a .32 Special .321-­inch expander into the .32-­20 neck, trimmed to length and loaded 
Hornady
 .321-­inch 170-­grain round-­nose flat-­point bullets. I used 11.8 grains of WC296, which produced 1,379 fps at the muzzle from my rifle. I used a Hornady #36 shell holder for the .32 H&R/.327 Federal. My ammunition fed and functioned flawlessly from the 1905 Winchester.
From left to right: .30 Carbine, .32 SL and .35 SL. The .32 and .35 SL were introduced in the Winchester 1905. Thirty-­five years later, the .32 SL was reduced to .30 caliber for the M1 Carbine. (Photo by Dave Emary)
Military Legacy
It is fairly common knowledge that the M1 Carbine cartridge was derived from the 1905 Winchester .32 SL cartridge. In fact, in the 1940 U.S. Army circular requesting a .30-­caliber light rifle, it was suggested that the cartridge should be similar to the .32 SL cartridge. Initially, Winchester did not submit a gun for the light-­rifle competition. Instead, it accepted a contract to develop the ammunition for the new light rifle, the result being the .30 Carbine cartridge. Winchester scaled down the .32 SL to .30 caliber, increased the operating pressure to 40,000 psi and used a lower sectional density .30-­caliber 110-­grain bullet. It resulted in a muzzle velocity nearly 600 fps faster than the .32 SL, a 30-­percent gain in muzzle energy and a flatter shooting cartridge. The .30 Carbine cartridge has effective ballistics to about 200 yards, which far eclipsed the .32 SL.
The other interesting contribution of the 1905 Winchester to the M1 Carbine is not so well known. The trigger group of the 1905 was essentially copied by Winchester for the M1 Carbine. When the two trigger groups are put side-­by-­side, the similarity is obvious. The trigger group parts design, triggerguard shape and safety are almost identical. The only part that is substantially different is the hammer, which was simplified in shape to aid production of the M1 carbine.

Today, T. C. Johnson’s Winchester Model 1905 and its .32 and .35 SL cartridges have been relegated to the dustbin of firearms history. I think it should be elevated to a bit higher status than that. It was an innovative, trend-­setting firearm in that it was one of the first centerfire semiautomatic rifles, and one that fed from a detachable magazine. Its main downfall — the underpowered cartridges — turned out to be a significant influence for one of the most produced and circulated military firearms and cartridges of World War II. Several design features were incorporated into the M1 Carbine. These forgotten guns come available from time to time and are not exorbitantly expensive. Ammunition is the big problem with shooting them, but for the person looking for an advanced reloading project it is possible to make good ammunition for both 1905 Winchester chamberings.
