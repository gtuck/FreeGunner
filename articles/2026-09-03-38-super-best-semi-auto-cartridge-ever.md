## Article Text (extracted)

.38 Super: Best Semi-Auto Cartridge Ever

The Elusive Trifecta of Speed, Power, and Accuracy

 The .38 Super offers speed, power, and accuracy, making it one of the best semiautomatic pistol cartridges ever. (Brad Miller PhD photo)

September 03, 2026

By Brad Miller PhD

The .38 Super is under-appreciated, ignored, and unknown to some shooters. That’s sad because the .38 Super is one of the best semiautomatic pistol cartridges ever designed. It offers speed, power, and accuracy. Let’s learn more about it.
In the Beginning
The .38 Super began life in Colt’s Government Model pistol in the late 1920s. However, at that time, the name “Super” applied to the pistol, not the cartridge. Colt introduced a new pistol called the “Super .38” Automatic Pistol, which was just the common .45 ACP Government Model 5.0-inch-barreled pistol rechambered in .38 Automatic. The gun was introduced in late 1928 and started shipping in January 1929. The .38 Super cartridge did not exist at that time. The gun was chambered for the .38 Automatic cartridge, which did exist.
The .38 Automatic cartridge, formally the .38 Automatic Colt Pistol (.38 ACP, also called the .38 Rimless, Smokeless) was created in 1900 by John Browning for his then-new Model 1900 semiautomatic pistol. It wasn’t exactly rimless, however, as the case has a small semi-rim that was used to headspace the round on the back of the barrel.
The .38 Automatic Super +P (right) was introduced in 1929, and it evolved from the earlier and lower-pressure .38 Automatic (left). Both rounds shown here have 130-grain bullets. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
Browning’s cartridge and gun were tested by the War Department in 1900 as candidates for a new service pistol. The .38 Automatic cartridge consisted of a 105-grain metal jacketed bullet running at 1,259 fps from a 6.0-inch barrel. Some sources reported the velocity was over 1,300 fps. The ammunition was loaded by Union Metallic Cartridge. It was not adopted by the War Department.

The bullet weight was increased, and the 1904 Thompson-LaGarde Report listed two loads for the .38 Automatic used in their tests with a Colt Military Model 1902 (6.0-inch barrel)—a 130-grain jacketed bullet at 1,107 fps and a 120-grain softpoint at 1,048 fps. The Thompson-LaGarde Report decided a larger diameter bullet was better and was the force behind the development of the .45 ACP.
During its early years, the .38 Automatic’s bullet weight and velocity were all over the place, with bullet weights from 105 to 130 grains. Velocities varied from 1,000 fps to 1,259 fps. As time went on, the bullet weight was more or less standardized to 130 grains, but velocities varied from one manufacturer to another.
When the Super .38 pistol was introduced, Colt’s December 1928 ad showed the .38 Automatic with a 130-grain FMJ or softpoint bullet and a speed of 1,190 fps. These .38 Automatic loads already existed. For example, the 1926 Peters catalog shows the .38 Automatic with a 130-grain bullet at 1,190 fps from a 6.0-inch barrel. Did Colt ignore the barrel length when they posted the speed in their ads? There’s no mention that 1,190 fps was a new loading. A speed of 1,190 fps is pretty specific and raises the question that Colt copied the speed for their ads and ignored the barrel length. Hard to say without more information.

Remington changed things. They started with the name. In Remington’s 1930 ammunition catalog, they listed the cartridge as the .38 Automatic Colt Pistol, just like their 1923 catalog. But in their January 1, 1930, Jobbers’ Wholesale Price catalog, they called it the .38 Super Automatic Colt Pistol. There aren’t any ballistics in these publications, so we don’t know if there was a change in performance that went along with the change in name.
In 
Colt’s Super .38: The Production History from 1929 through 1971
, author Douglas Sheldon writes that the velocity was increased to 1,300 fps by Remington sometime in late 1932. It showed up in Remington’s 1933 catalog, and it was available with a metal case or a hollowpoint bullet.
This is the high water mark for the .38 Super, and at 1,300 fps it produces 488 ft-lbs of muzzle energy. The Super’s impressive energy was, however, quickly eclipsed by the introduction of the .357 Magnum in 1935. The .357 Magnum fires a 158-grain bullet at 1,235 fps from a 4.0-inch barrel. That produces 535 ft-lbs of muzzle energy, which clearly beats the .38 Super.
A diagram of the 38 super +P.
That Was Then, This Is Now
Today, we recognize two 
.38 Automatic rounds
: the .38 Automatic with a SAAMI pressure limit of 26,500 psi and the .38 Super Automatic +P with a pressure limit of 36,500 psi. In 1974 SAAMI added “+P” to the name to help distinguish it from the lower-pressure .38 Automatic. There was no increase in pressure in 1974. It was just a name change. Colloquially, we just call it the .38 Super.
The .38 Super is one rare example whose performance has been downgraded over time. Remington reduced the speed of the 130-grain bullet from 1,300 fps to 1,275 fps in 1953. And they did it again in 1983, when they dropped the speed to 1,215 fps, where it stands today. Winchester did the same thing. Their 1954 catalog shows a 130-grain bullet speed of 1,300 fps. They dropped that to 1,275 to 1,280 fps by 1960, then down to 1,245 fps by 1976. Their current speed is also 1,215 fps. The 85-fps change reduced the muzzle energy from 488 ft-lbs to 426 ft-lbs.

In spite of the reduced loads from the big-name manufacturers, boutique manufacturers load the .38 Super to its full potential. Ammomakers like Buffalo Bore, CorBon, Doubletap, Underwood, and Wilson Combat push 115-grain bullets to close to 1,500 fps, 124/125-grain bullets to 1,350 fps, and 147-grain bullets to 1,225 fps from a 5.0-inch barrel. A 124-grain bullet at 1,350 fps produces 502 ft-lbs of muzzle energy. The power is back!
IPSC/USPSA competition breathed new life into the .38 Super because it performed better than the .45 ACP in compensated pistols. The .38 Super shoots lighter bullets with large charges of slow-burning powder, so it reduces muzzle rise better and allows faster follow-up shots. Plus, a .38 Super magazine holds a couple more rounds than a .45.
Even revolvers like this Pietta King Trio were chambered in .38 Super. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
The .38 Super’s semi-rim headspacing flaw became apparent over time. Headspacing off the semi-rim proved to be a problem with accuracy because the chambers were cut to a generous depth, and excess headspace was linked to poor accuracy. When chambers were corrected to headspace on the case mouth, accuracy was greatly improved.
A second issue with the semi-rim showed up later. As IPSC/USPSA shooting became a magazine-capacity race, double-stack high-capacity magazines became desirable because they reduced the number of reloads a shooter needed to make during a stage. The semi-rim cases didn’t feed smoothly from double-stack magazines, especially with the reduced-power recoil springs required to keep compensated pistols operating. The fix was to remove the semi-rim, thus was born the rimless .38 Super. Rimless versions of the .38 Super are named .38 Super Comp, .38TJ (Todd Jarrett), and .38 Super rimless. The 0.406-inch SAAMI spec diameter of the .38 Super rim was reduced to around 0.386 inch.
The .38 Super is still popular in compensated pistols (Open Division) in IPSC/USPSA practical shooting competition, though 9mm Luger has come to dominate this division with heavily loaded ammunition called 9 Major (9mm Luger loaded to meet Major power factor ballistics requirements) because 9mm brass is less expensive—often free as range pickups. A few select ammunition manufacturers load .38 Super exclusively for these competitions. They have to be loaded heavy to make Major power factor (165 in USPSA, 160 in IPSC, in Open Division). Power factor is calculated as bullet weight times velocity divided by 1,000. A 130-grain bullet must travel at least 1,270 fps to qualify for a 165 Major power factor. (130 X 1,270 / 1,000 = 165.100.) The “old” .38 Super ammo at 1,300 fps would qualify (pf = 169), but the current ammo at 1,215 fps would not (pf = 158).
Handguns that have been chambered for the .38 Super include Model 1911s like this customized Colt. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
Atlanta Arms, Eley, and Geco load ammo for these competitions. They use 124-grain bullets. Atlanta Arms claims a speed of 1,380 fps from a 5.0-inch barrel (pf = 171). Eley says their ammo will achieve a power factor of 165 to 170 in a 5.0-inch barrel. Geco claims 1,410 fps from a 6.0-inch barrel (pf = 174). Eley and Atlanta Arms use rimless Super Comp cases, while the Geco ammo has the semi-rim.
In the early days of IPSC/USPSA the power factor for making Major was 175. This means a 115-grain bullet would have to go at least 1,522 fps, and a 124-grain bullet would have to go at least 1,412 fps to qualify (most loads exceed this for a margin of error). Most .38 Super loads had to exceed its normal pressure limit to get these speeds with light bullets. These excess pressure loads combined with unsupported chambers and the strength of the brass made at the time, sometimes resulted in blown cases, and thus was born the condition called “Super Face” to describe when case ruptures blasted hot gas and particles in the shooter’s face. I’ve experienced Super Face events when cases with high-pressure loads failed (.38 Super and 9 Major—“Major Face”). I don’t recommend it. (Always wear eye protection.) The shooter’s hand also takes a whack since most of the gas pressure is directed downward. Grips can be shattered, and magazines are often damaged or destroyed. Stronger brass helps, and Starline’s .38 Super +P and .38 Super Comp are more robust than Winchester and Remington cases, in my experience.
Guns that chamber the .38 Super
 have to be large enough to handle its length. The Government Model was perfect for this because it chambers the .45 ACP, which has a maximum length of 1.275 inches, and the .38 Super’s maximum length is 1.280 inches (call them the same). A gun designed around a shorter round, such as the 9mm Luger, won’t handle the .38 Super because of the 9mm’s shorter maximum length of 1.169 inches.

Guns other than a 1911 design also have been chambered in the Super. These include the SIG SAUER Model P220 and Taurus Model PT38, though these have been discontinued. Tanfoglio of Italy chambers their large-frame pistol (T95), sold as the EAA Witness, in .38 Super. I have the Witness Steel model. It is SA/DA with a 17-round magazine capacity. It’s heavy, and that makes it pleasant to shoot, and mine has been quite accurate with a range of ammo, though I have not fired it from a Ransom Rest to test its capabilities.
Glock has never made a pistol in .38 Super, however, such a thing exists. Lone Wolf Arms made a .38 Super conversion barrel for the Glock 10mm Model 20 in the past. It was 6.0 inches long, and I put one in my Model 20 and cut it down to 4.6 inches. The magazines have to be modified to work with this cartridge, and that’s where it failed. A magazine modified by Lone Wolf and one I modified did not give reliable feeding, so it has not evolved beyond a curious novelty. They don’t offer this barrel today, and given reliability issues with magazines, that’s probably wise.
Even revolvers have been chambered in the .38 Super. Smith & Wesson made a Model 627-4 with a 5.5-inch barrel and an eight-shot cylinder for competitors, but it has been discontinued. Pietta recently introduced a Single Action Army clone they call the King Trio, which has three cylinders—one for .38 Special/.357 Magnum, one for 9mm Luger, and one for .38 Super.
My accuracy results with the Pietta King Trio firing .38 Super ammo have not been that good with most ammo, shooting a 7.0- to 8.0-inch 15-shot group. But it does like the Wilson Combat 125-grain HAP load, putting 15 shots in 2.99 inches at 25 yards. It shoots hollowpoint .357 Magnum ammunition very well, too, so it seems that loads with hollowpoint bullets or handloads might be required to find what it likes loaded in .38 Super cases.
This EAA Witness was chambered in .38 Super. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
Top Loads
I’ve not fired much .38 Super factory ammo other than to test accuracy and velocity in a variety of guns. I started reloading it from the outset, so the vast majority of what I shoot has been handcrafted.

Advertisement

I would be hard-pressed to pick a favorite factory load, for a variety of reasons. I do like that the Remington 130-grain FMJ bullet is a roundnose, like the original load, but that’s for aesthetic and historical reasons. I would love it if it had the full 1,300 fps ballistics.
Traditional .38 Super bullets are 0.356 inch, and that’s what SAAMI specs indicate. That said, the barrel groove diameter spec is 0.355 inch, just like the 9mm and the .38 Special/.357 Magnum. Thus, 9mm pistol bullets and .38/.357 revolver bullets work fine. In fact, 
Hornady
’s .38 Super load data includes 0.355, 0.356, and 0.357 jacketed bullets.
Accuracy and velocity chart for factory ammo.
The Super’s versatility is wide, and it will handle anything from the lightest bullets (88 to 90 grains) up to 180 grains. And with handloading you can pick whatever performance level you want, from mild to wild. The Super can be downloaded to .38 Automatic ballistics for low recoil. How low you can go depends on what will reliably cycle your pistol.
On the upper end, the right selection of powder can produce impressive speeds that can exceed factory-loaded ammo and still stay within the SAAMI pressure limit. A 124/125-grain bullet can reach 1,450 fps, which is the same as the higher-pressure 9x23 Winchester cartridge and what the .357 Magnum will do in a 4.0-inch barrel.
The best powder I’ve used in the .38 Super is VihtaVuori N105. In my experience, it has been the most consistent powder for producing small groups with a range of bullets, and it can produce top velocities. Unfortunately, VihtaVuori appears to have dropped this powder from production.

Advertisement

But don’t be discouraged. There are many excellent powders for the .38 Super. I dug through my results from testing accuracy with my gun in a Ransom Rest, and the powders that performed the best with several different loads/bullets are VihtaVuori 3N38, Accurate No. 7, Ramshot Silhouette, Hodgdon Longshot, and Winchester 572.
Other powders that did well in my handloads include Alliant BE-86, Alliant Bullseye, Alliant Unique, Alliant Power Pistol, VihtaVuori N320, and Winchester AutoComp. That’s a lot of powders that have proved to work well in the .38 Super. I’ve listed some loads I used in the past in my compensated pistol. The charge weights might be above published limits, and they should only be used in guns with fully supported barrels. The loads with 3N38 use large charge weights of powder, and this provides a lot of gas for a port or compensator to reduce recoil and muzzle rise.
Some of the best accuracy I’ve had has been with Hornady, Zero, and Sierra hollowpoint bullets. Hornady has two JHP bullets—XTP and HAP (Hornady Action Pistol). I’ve found about the same accuracy when comparing them side by side, but the HAP bullets are generally less costly. Zero makes two different 9mm JHP designs—a roundnose HP and a conical HP. Most of my results favor the conical design, but the roundnose HPs have produced some very small group sizes as well and shouldn’t be ruled out. Of course, these are results from my gun (with a Kart barrel), so you should try several to see what your gun likes best.
The .38 Super is powerful and accurate. Performance-wise, boutique ammomakers bring back the original high performance, and handloading allows you to do that yourself. It’s named Super. That’s how it performs. At full power, it has less recoil and more muzzle energy than the .45 ACP, and you can get more .38 rounds in the gun than .45s. That’s Super.
