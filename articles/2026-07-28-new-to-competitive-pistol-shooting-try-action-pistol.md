# New to Competitive Pistol Shooting? Try Action Pistol.

New to Competitive Pistol Shooting? Try Action Pistol.

Bianchi's Short Course is a perfect introduction to competition shooting, without the need for fancy equipment.

 G&A’s James Tarr and Julie Golob go to the ground for maximum stability to engage 50-yard targets while shooting the CMP Bianchi Cup course of fire for Guns & Ammo TV. (Photo by Patrick Sweeney)

July 28, 2026

By James Tarr

I’ve been aware of Action Pistol competition for decades but never competed because none of my local clubs are set up for it. Operated by the Civilian Marksmanship Program (CMP) since 2023, the Bianchi Cup tournament is held in May and features a strictly regimented course of fire that requires barricades, plate racks, a moving target, and shooting out to 50 yards. I shot the Action Pistol course of fire while filming segments for Guns & Ammo TV with 
Springfield Armory’s Julie Golob
. Golob has won the Bianchi Cup 11 times competing in various divisions. I am in love with this competition. It is a wonderful balance of speed and accuracy, and a perfect way for people who haven’t shot competitively to experience it. Best of all — and the reason for me writing this column — I learned about the official Action Pistol Short Course. You can practice at your local club.
You don’t need a lot of equipment to participate in Action Pistol. There are different divisions for various types of pistols, including ones specifically suited to the kind of pistols we would actually carry for self-­defense. I shot the match at the home of the CMP Bianchi Cup: Green Valley Rifle and Pistol Club in Hallsville, Missouri. I used my personal, full-­size Springfield Armory Echelon 4.5, which is a stock factory gun with iron sights. Other than eye and ear protection and at least four magazines, the only other items I needed to bring were a holster and ammunition. I used a Safariland Gravity outside-­the-­waistband holster I was evaluating for Guns & Ammo’s “Carry Rig” column.
An upcoming segment of Guns & Ammo TV features Patrick Sweeney competing against pro-shooter Julie Golob during the Action Pistol Barricade stage. (Photo by Patrick Sweeney)
The CMP took over administration of the Bianchi Cup from the NRA in 2022, as well as other sanctioned Action Pistol matches. All the details of the official four-­event course of fire is on the CMP website (thecmp.org), but let me offer a brief summary.
Action Pistol and The Bianchi Cup were created in 1979. There are just four stages. In each you fire 48 rounds for a total of 192 rounds and a possible 1,920 points. You never fire more than six shots in a string, so it’s revolver-­neutral. AP-­1 targets are the official type, which are tombstone in shape. There is a black 4-­inch circle in the center and an X-­ring. The number of “X” hits is used as a tie-­breaker. The 10-­ring measures 8 inches in diameter, and the 8-­ring is 12 inches in diameter. If you hit the target outside the scoring rings, you receive 5 points.

In three out of the four events you’re firing on a line beside other competitors so focus is required. In those same events you’re shooting at paper targets that appear, and the start signal is visual — when the targets appear, that’s your signal to go!
For the Falling Plate event, you’re shooting at a steel plate rack with six 8-­inch plates. For this event, start with your hands above your shoulders, get an audible start signal, draw and shoot the plates twice at 10, 15, 20 and 25 yards. This is a par-­time course of fire, with 6, 7, 8 and 9 seconds at the various distances. You are not allowed to fire extra shots on this or any of the stages, so between that and the time constraint you really must focus. 
For Guns & Ammo TV, I shot this alongside former G&A Handgun Editor Patrick Sweeney who used a Springfield Armory Echelon 4.0 equipped with an Aimpoint COA optic. That dot reticle helped his accuracy at 25 yards where my front sight appeared nearly as wide as the plate.

For the Barricade event, start with your hands flat on a 2-­foot-­wide barricade in front of you. There are two targets, and from either side of the barricade fire six shots on each. This is done at 10, 15, 25 and 35 yards, and you get 5, 6, 7 and 8 seconds per string at those respective distances. Smart competitors learn how to brace themselves against the barricade for the long shots.
For the Practical event, you are engaging two tombstone targets at 10, 15, 25 and 50 yards. There are several strings at each distance, and you’re firing one, two or three shots at each target with various time constraints that are not too fast — as long as you don’t mess up the draw. At 15 yards and beyond, you can go prone. Prone gets you steadier, but getting down on the ground takes time. I went prone at 50 yards and found the extra time to get into position was more than made up for by the improved steadiness. I shot this alongside Julie Golob, and while I generally hate aiming I have to say it was a lot of fun. At 50 yards, my front sight was just about the same width as the target.
The Moving Target event is the only one performed solo. It’s shot at 10, 15, 20 and 25 yards at a tombstone target that moves from right to left, then left to right, at each of those distances. Fire six or three rounds at the target on each pass, depending on distance. The target travels 60 feet in 6 seconds, so it is really moving. I learned that I had to actually lead it. How much lead? That depends on the speed of the ammo. You have to wait to draw until you see it, so this is the most speed-­intensive of all the stages. This stage is the main reason why I never became interested in Action Pistol. Why? Nobody in my area has a mover like this. Then Julie Golob introduced me to the Short Course, which changed how one can practice for movers.
There is a short course listed in the current CMP rule book, but the one I shot with Golob is the NRA Action Pistol Short Course. NRA ran the Bianchi Cup from 1984 to 2022. You can find the Short Course on Golob’s website, which she created for 
all things Action Pistol related
. You’ll need the appropriate AP-­1 targets and an electronic timer in order to set a par time, but it’s that straightforward.
For the Short Course Practical event, set up two targets 3-­feet apart. At 10 yards, starting with hands above shoulders, fire one round on each in 3 seconds, two on each in 4 seconds, then three shots on each with your strong hand only in 8 seconds. Move back to 15 yards, and fire one round on each in 4 seconds, then two on each in 5 seconds, and three on each in 6 seconds.

For the Short Course Barricade stage, if you don’t have an actual barricade just use something that works. During filming we used polymer range barrels stacked two high. Use two targets, 6-­feet apart, and start with your hands flat on the barricade. From 10 yards, fire six rounds around the right side, then fire six around the left side; you have 5 seconds for each string. Move back to 15 yards and repeat. You’re afforded 6 seconds on each side at the longer distance.
To practice the Plate event, don’t stress if your club doesn’t have a plate rack. Golob set up six targets and pushed them as close together as possible, then stapled paper plates in the center of the targets to simulate the white steel plates. Run it twice at 10 yards, with a 6-­second time limit, then run it twice at 15 yards in 7 seconds.
For the short course Mover stage, when you don’t have access to an actual moving target, spread those six targets out so they are 3 feet edge-­to-­edge. At 10 yards you’ve got 6 seconds to hit all six targets, one shot on each — first running right to left, then left to right. Move back to 15 yards and do it again.
Whether you’re talking about the four official events or the Short Course, you must compete under pressure and time constraint, draw, use a barricade (cover), engage multiple targets, and engage a moving target. These are all skills you should be practicing, and I believe competition is the best way to learn and practice the skills that you’ll want and need in case of a critical defensive situation.
Don’t be intimidated by the sight of fancy equipment or running and gunning at USPSA and IDPA matches. Bianchi Cup and Action Pistol are less intimidating, offering a balance of speed and accuracy. It doesn’t require any movement while you’re shooting. In fact, people have competed at the Bianchi Cup from a wheelchair. All you need to practice the Short Course is a firearm, ammo, holster, eyes and ears, targets and a timer. Once you can get all your shots off in the time allotted, work on increasing your score.

James Tarr
