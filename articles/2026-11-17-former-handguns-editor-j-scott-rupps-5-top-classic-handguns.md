Former Handguns Editor J. Scott Rupp's 5 Top Classic Handguns

Veteran firearms journalist J. Scott Rupp picks his favorite classic handguns of all time. 

 Former Handguns editor shares his top 5 classic handguns. 

November 17, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

Applying the term “classic” to a firearm is a subjective exercise. Some people go strictly by how old a design is, while others reserve the description for guns that set a certain standard. You can also use aesthetics as your yardstick; a gun’s lines instantly register as “classic” to your eyes. And then there’s always the old parent standby: “Because I said so.” In coming up with this list of classics, I used all of the above. They’re presented in no particular order.
Smith & Wesson Model 29
“Did I fire six shots or only five. Well, to tell you the truth, in all this excitement I kind of lost track myself. But being as this is a .44 Magnum, the most powerful handgun in the world and would blow your head clean off, you’ve got to ask yourself one question: Do I feel lucky?” When Clint Eastwood’s eponymous “Dirty Harry” character uttered those words in 1971, with a Smith & Wesson Model 29 pointed at a suspect, the shooting world went nuts for the gun and its cartridge.
The .44 Magnum was the brainchild of Elmer Keith, who was experimenting with hot .44 Special loads and convinced Smith & Wesson along with Remington Arms to develop, respectively, a big, strong revolver and a big, strong cartridge. Smith & Wesson’s N-frame revolver was announced to the public in early 1956. Initially called the NT-430 (N frame, Target, .430 bore), it became the Model 29 when the company began numbering its models in 1957.
The Model 29 was a square-butt revolver with oversize walnut target stocks, and in addition to the 6.5-inch length (later changed to six inches for the standard model) it was also offered with four- and 8-3/8-inch barrels, as well as some rare 5- and 5.5-inchers. Sights included a fully adjustable rear and a red-ramp front.

While the basic model was discontinued in 1999, Smith & Wesson has kept variants in the catalog through the years, and today the company offers the Model 29 as part of its Classics line with four- or 6.5-inch barrels plus a machine-engraved version.
There’s also the well-respected Model 629, a stainless steel version of the 29. I picked up a 629 with a five-inch barrel a couple decades ago, and it’s a fine revolver, but it lacks the panache of the Model 29. To partially remedy that, I recently purchased a Model 57—a .41 Magnum N-frame with the same gorgeous lines of the 29. It’s fast become a favorite, and I do in fact feel lucky.

Ruger Blackhawk
When Hollywood was cranking out Western TV shows and movies in the 1950s, Bill Ruger smelled opportunity. Colt’s Single Action Army—the Peacemaker—was front and center on TV sets and the silver screen, but there were no new SAAs for sale because Colt had ceased production in 1941. Ruger filled the gap with his Blackhawk, introduced in 1955.

It looked and felt like a Peacemaker—and its early success prompted Colt to resume SAA production—but the Blackhawk had an edge: Out of the gate it was built to handle magnum cartridges, making the revolver popular with both recreational shooters and hunters. Early chamberings included .357 Magnum, .41 Magnum, .44 Magnum, .45 Colt, .45 ACP and .30 Carbine. The initial barrel length was 4-5/8 inches; 6.5-, 7.5- and 10-inch lengths came soon after. The Blackhawk’s .44 Magnum’s recoil proved too much for most shooters, so Ruger followed with the bigger, heavier Super Blackhawk in .44 Magnum in 1959.
The New Model Blackhawks and Super Blackhawks arrived in 1973, introducing Bill Ruger’s transfer-bar safety that allowed the gun to be safely carried with all chambers loaded. While calibers for the New Model Blackhawk have remained mostly faithful to the original lineup, the Super Blackhawk has expanded to include such big boomers as the .454 Casull and .480 Ruger.
My Blackhawk addiction started with an Old Model (often referred to as “three-screw” due to the number of frame screws) .41 Magnum, and I’ve since added two New Model Blackhawks and a Super Blackhawk. They are hands-down my favorite revolvers. I love the sound of the hammer cocking, the easy-pointing heft, and the way the plow-handle grip rocks back in your hand under recoil. It turns out I should’ve collected more because Ruger recently announced the suspension of centerfire revolver production, including the Blackhawk.

The 1911 has been a popular handgun in the U.S. for more than a century. The Government model in .45 ACP (bottom, a 1945-built gun in the process of being restored) was the original and remains popular, but newer version like the Commander size in 9mm (top, a Springfield Armory Ronin 4.25) have a lot of fans. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
1911
John Browning developed what would become the 1911 for U.S. military trials to replace the anemic .38 caliber sidearm, and its service to our Armed Forces spans generations—from World War I through Afghanistan. Over its lifespan it has also been embraced by civilian shooters from sea to shining sea.

The 1911’s profile is instantly recognizable, and what sets it apart for shooters is the 18-degree grip angle and a trigger that is the envy of all semiautomatics. The trigger slides straight to the rear, promoting a fundamentally correct trigger press and greatly improving shooter accuracy potential.
The original 1911 size is the so-called Government model with a five-inch barrel. Specialized military needs later led to shorter Commander and Officer’s sizes. While the .45 ACP has reigned supreme for a long time, other cartridges have been chambered over the years. In recent decades, gun designers figured out how to make the 9mm run reliably, and today it gives the .45 a run for its money.
The 1911’s enduring popularity means that today you can not only buy about any kind of 1911 you want—from economical models to high-end guns costing thousands of dollars—but you can also customize an existing pistol thanks to a smorgasbord of aftermarket parts.
My love affair with the 1911 started when I was a kid and my dad introduced me to Mickey Spillane’s Mike Hammer character, a hard-boiled detective who carried a Government .45 under his suit jacket. So, my first 1911s were full-size .45s. But my favorite—and most practical—1911 is a Springfield Ronin 4.25, a 9mm Commander that’s a joy to shoot and to carry.

Thompson/Center’s revolutionary single-shot design began with the Contender and culminated in the Encore shown here. Driving out a single hinge-pin allowed quick barrel replacement, and hunters and target shooters could choose from a vast array of cartridges from .22 LR to centerfires capable of taking down the world’s largest game. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Thompson/Center Contender
The Contender was a Transformer long before Transformers were a thing. Invented by Warren Center in 1967, his break-action single-shot had the ability to convert from a .22 Long Rifle to .45-70 Government—or practically anything in between—with a quick barrel swap.
It quickly became a favorite for both handgun hunters and silhouette competitors, and in addition to common calibers it spurred some excellent niche cartridges from Steve Herrett, J.D. Jones, Wes Ugalde (the TCU rounds that were developed in cooperation with Contender manufacturer Thompson/Center Arms), and Ken Waters. These specialty centerfires ran the gamut from 6mm to .416 and could handle game from varmints to elephants as well as pinging long-range steel targets.
The Contender employed a novel hammer that could be changed in various ways (depending on variant) to accommodate rimfire and centerfire cartridges, and changing barrels requires only removing the fore-end and driving out the large pivot pin on which the action hinged. Sights, including scopes, remain attached to the barrel, so there’s no change in zero when switching barrels.

Handgun barrels ranged in length from 6 to 14 inches, and the Contender could also be converted into a rifle by changing fore-ends and adding a buttstock and rifle-length barrel. It was truly a gun for all seasons.
The Contender was eventually succeeded by the stronger-framed Encore, which is what I have. It’s a true transformer, and I can change mine from a 6.5 Creedmoor handgun to a .338 Federal rifle to a .50 caliber muzzleloader in just minutes.
Thompson/Center went dark for a while after parent Smith & Wesson shuttered it, but the company is back under new ownership. The current firm’s focus is more on Encore long guns, but it still sells the components for Encore handguns, and there are aftermarket outfits making handgun components for both Contenders and Encores.
Review: Thompson/Center Encore
The Desert Eagle has been in countless TV shows, movies and video games. Its unique semiautomatic design allowed it to accommodate magnum revolver cartridges as well as modern semiauto rounds designed specifically for the platform. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Desert Eagle
It’s not the biggest, baddest semiauto ever to walk the planet—that title belongs to the .475 Wildey—but the Desert Eagle is chambered to such heavy hitters as .44 Magnum and .50 Action Express. It’s been in so many movies and TV shows, as well as video games, it deserves its own star on Hollywood’s Walk of Fame.

Advertisement

The secret sauce is a unique gas-driven design that Magnum Research’s Bernard White developed in 1983. Instead of common short-recoil or blowback systems, the Desert Eagle employs a gas piston and a rotating bolt that locks and unlocks similar to that of an AR-15.
White’s design allowed the Desert Eagle to handle magnum cartridges, and the initial Mark I model was chambered to .357 Magnum and .44 Magnum with 6-, 10- or 14-inch barrels. The Mark VII came next, and it introduced an adjustable trigger, later, the powerful .50 Action Express cartridge.
Today’s version is the Mark XIX, and it’s chambered to .357 Magnum, .44 Magnum, .50 Action Express and the .429 Desert Eagle cartridge—a necked down .50 AE. These are big cartridges, but the Desert Eagle is a big gun. My own .44 Magnum, which I got back when Magnum Research offered a “design your own Desert Eagle” website, weighs a whopping 66 ounces with its 6-inch barrel.
My barrel has a muzzle brake, which helps tame recoil but also makes it quite blasty. I’ve shot the .50 AE and .429 DE out of Desert Eagles as well, and while certainly shootable they’re quite a handful. But, really, isn’t that the point? The allure of the Desert Eagle is straight-up power. In fact, I’ve been thinking of picking up a conversion kit that will turn my .44 Magnum into a .50 AE with just a barrel and magazine swap. More power!
