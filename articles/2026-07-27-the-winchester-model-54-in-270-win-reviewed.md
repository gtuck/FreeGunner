The Winchester Model 54 in .270 Win.: Reviewed

Revisiting the Model 54 using vintage ammunition and loads available a century ago.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

July 27, 2026

By Dave Emary

If you are like me, your interest in firearms includes the role of small arms in history. The year 1925 was important to firearms history. It was the year that Winchester introduced its Model 54. It wasn’t the first bolt-­action rifle produced, but it was the first commercially successful bolt-­action rifle. It was also the predecessor to the vaunted Model 70 and the .270 Winchester. The .270 became the standard bearer for high-­performance hunting cartridges. Smokeless powder was still in its infancy, too, and high-­velocity cartridges were very new. Lever-­action rifles still dominated the sportsmen market, but bolt-­action designs were beginning to challenge them. Though reloading and optically sighted rifles were a novelty of the era, the bolt-­action rifle and .270 helped advance the development for each subject.
Guns & Ammo’s Model 54 sample was manufactured in 1929. It features a Standard Grade walnut stock with hand-cut checkering on the forend and around the grip. The stock also features a Schnabel-style forend and aftermarket Pachmayr recoil pad. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
As with previous rifles reviewed in the series, “Of Wood & Steel,” Guns & Ammo revisited the Model 54 and tested it using vintage ammunition and handloads made to replicate loads available a century ago.
The Model 54 bolt borrowed inspiration from the Mauser design, which was a popular action at the time of its development. The bolt featured a winged safety at the rear and a 90-degree lift. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Not To Be Overshadowed
Firearms transformed 100 years ago, both in design concepts and performance. Lever-­action rifles had been the firearm of choice for 60 years, between the end of the Civil War and 1925. The diminished dominance of the lever-­action began with the development of the bolt-­action rifle and magazine solutions to make repeaters. This paralleled the development of high-­performance cartridges and the introduction of stronger steels and innovative manufacturing processes. Bolt-­action rifles eventually exceeded the accuracy potential and range offered by lever-­action designs.
From underneath the Model 54 bolt assembly, the notch for the ejector is visible, as well as the Mauser-type extractor trapped in place within the action’s grooves as the bolt rotates during cycling. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Bolt-action rifles weren’t new in 1925. The Mauser brothers revealed their first bolt-­action military rifle in 1871, perfecting it with the Model 1898. Most countries around the world had adopted some type of bolt-­action rifle for military issue by the mid-­1890s. In the U.S., both Remington and Winchester produced Model 1917 Enfield bolt-­action rifles for the U.S. Army during World War I, but the lever-­action was preferred among American riflemen until the 1920s.

The Standard Grade walnut stock on a 1929-manufactured Model 54 would have been equipped with a steel buttplate. Sling swivel attachment points were upgrades. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Introduced in 1925, the Winchester Model 54 bolt-­action was designed by Thomas Johnson, a Winchester engineer. Based on the Mauser action, it featured a turned-­down bolt, claw extractor, and an elegant, slim, walnut stock. An excerpt from the sales flyer that year reads, “The Winchester Model 54 rifle is a sporting arm intended, with one exception, for big game shooting particularly at long ranges and under conditions where the utmost strength, durability and reliability are required. It is furnished for a selection of cartridges suitable for any except the thickest skinned game of the world. A graceful, simple, well balanced weapon of attractive lines, it has been designed to give exceptional accuracy combined with the most perfect adaptability to modern sporting marksmanship methods. This Winchester marks the supreme development in the high intensity sporting rifle.”
The forend was fastened by a screw to a barrel band that supported a leaf-type rear sight mounted to the barrel. Handcut checkering is centered on the band within a triangular border. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
In addition to the new-­for-­1925 .270 Winchester, the Model 54 was initially offered in .22 Hornet, .250-­3000 Savage, 7mm Mauser, 7.65mm Belgian Mauser, .30-’06 Government and 9mm Mauser. The 7.65 Belgian Mauser and 9mm Mauser were interesting offerings from the standpoint that they both seem obscure today. Later chamberings added the .220 Swift, .257 Roberts, .30-­30 Winchester and 8mm Mauser. Special-­order chamberings included .23-­35 Winchester, .32 Special and .35 Whelen.
The non-adjustable two-stage trigger is curved and serrated. It would have required a gunsmith’s services to lighten the trigger to the 11/2-pound average pull observed during Guns & Ammo’s evaluation. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 54 had a one-­piece stamped triggerguard and floorplate, a two-­stage trigger with a corrugated finger surface, a two-­piece firing pin like on the Model 1903 Springfield, a claw extractor as on the Mauser, and a Mauser-­style wing safety atop the bolt shroud. The 54 also had three action screws to ensure stable bedding in the stock. The receiver was pre-­drilled for a Lyman receiver sight, as well. In 1933, Winchester introduced the “Speed Lock,” which improved lock time from just more than 5 milliseconds to about 3 milliseconds. It was rather amazing for that era, especially when we consider the lock time compares favorably with today’s rifles.

The Model 54 sports a Mauser-style, three-position safety located on the bolt shroud that blocked the firing pin. It rotated over the top, making the safety less than ideal when a scope was mounted. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 54 was proclaimed by some to be the highest form of the bolt-­action rifle, but there were detractors due to certain features. Many did not like the cheap aesthetic of the stamped one-­piece triggerguard and floorplate. Handling G&A’s sample Model 54 for the first time, I admit that I was rather put off by the looks of the stamped triggerguard and floorplate on an otherwise nice rifle.
Five rounds could be loaded from the top of the action. Note the military-style slot at the rear above the cartridge rim. This feature of the action would accept cartridges fed from an appropriate clip. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Critics also complained about the two-­stage trigger that functions as the bolt stop. When it was introduced, the two-­stage trigger was considered fine for military rifles but on the 54 it was regarded as “inappropriate” for target shooting and hunting. The two-­piece firing pin was also frowned on by many, as was the Mauser wing-­type safety on top of the bolt shroud. The safety rotated to the right instead of the left, just as it did on Mausers.
The round action was grooved to guide the bolt and initiate extraction and ejection. Cartridges fed reliably due to the center position above the internal magazine and the wide feed ramp. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 54 was available with a Standard Grade stock and a 24-­inch barrel with a walnut pistol-­grip configuration that weighed 7¾ pounds. A Standard Grade carbine with 20-­inch barrel weighed 7¼ pounds. The carbine was not available in .22 Hornet, though. The Model 54 was offered in a Super Grade that featured a select walnut stock having a rubber pistol grip cap, checkering and cheek piece. A slip-­on front-­sight cover and sling swivels for a 1-­inch sling of the “highest quality leather” were also peddled.
The .270 Win. was developed by necking down a .30-’03 case to accept a .277-caliber projectile. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 1925 sales flyer also touted the accuracy of Winchester’s Model 54. It showed groups shot by none-­other-­than Lt. Col. Townsend Whelen in .270 Winchester and .30-­’06, who would later contribute to Guns & Ammo until his death in 1961. Those groups from a Model 54 measured 1½ inches and were fired at 100 yards. Col. Whelen’s results, “Mr. Rifleman,” matched well with the results from our test rifle.
The Model 54 sold well until the stock market crash of 1929. It was replaced in 1936 by an improved version, which was designed to address the aforementioned complaints. It was named the “Model 70.” However, during its 10-­year production run, more than 50,000 Model 54 rifles were sold. More than half of its sales occurred during the Great Depression.

At The Range
For the series “Of Wood & Steel,” Guns & Ammo sourced a 1929-­production year Model 54 chambered in .270 Winchester. The gun has a 24-­inch barrel with a bright, shiny bore and a Western receiver sight. At some point in its life, the original steel buttplate was replaced with an aftermarket rubber Pachmayr buttpad. Still, the gun weighed 7 pounds, 14 ounces, and had a two-­stage trigger the averaged a crisp 1 pound, 8 ounce, pull weight on our Lyman Digital Trigger Gauge.
At some point, the factory front sight was replaced by a serrated post with a steel bead. The groove highlight the option for a protective hood. The barrel-mounted rear sight was also eliminated. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
To test the rifle, I developed loads to simulate what was offered in 1925. The 1925 Model 54 sales flyer and Winchester catalog listed the .270 Winchester with a 130-­grain soft-­point bullet at 3,160 feet per second (fps) from a 24-­inch barrel. IMR 1147 propellant, which eventually became IMR 4320, was new in 1923, and this is what was most likely what Winchester loaded in its .270 cartridges. I was unable to find any IMR 4320 for this evaluation, so I used IMR 4064. The 
Hodgdon Reloading Annual Manual 
 shows virtually no difference in the performance of the .270 Winchester with IMR 4320 and the IMR 4064 of today. For testing the Model 54 in .270 I loaded a 
Hornady
 130-­grain Spire Point on top of the Hodgdon guide’s maximum of 47.5 grains of IMR 4064 with a CCI 200 primer. Table one shows our test results when fired at 100 yards. G&A Editor-­in-­Chief Eric Poole did all the shooting for record to ensure consistency for comparison. For this series testing 100-­year-­old hunting rifles, we fired three, five-­shot groups at 100 yards and one group at 200 yards due to ammunition constraints, barrel cooling and maintenance. We had a limited amount of production ammunition for three rifles, so the test protocol was modified to accommodate fewer groups.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
To explore the effective ranges that these guns with period sights would have offered, we also fired a few groups beyond 200 yards. With a 130-­grain bullet, the .270 Winchester demonstrated that it offered effective and superior ballistics between 400 and 500 yards. With iron sights used as a baseline for comparing all test rifles, the actual effective range for hunting would have been limited by the eyesight and skill of the shooter more than the accuracy potential of the rifle and ammunition. To demonstrate this, we also shot a steel target with a 10-­inch circle to represent the kill zone of a deer at long distance. The Model 54 easily held this target’s kill zone at 200 yards.
