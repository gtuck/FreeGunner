POF Minuteman P15 Custom Review: Performance Beyond Looks

How Customizations Evolved the Minuteman into the Perfect Rifle

 KJ’s custom leopard-print POF Minuteman reflects the personality behind the build while maintaining the equipment and configuration developed through years of rifle training. (Powers Photography)

September 24, 2026

By Kim Jenkins

I remember exactly why I bought this rifle.
At the time, I was preparing for my first intensive rifle course, a multi-day training in South Carolina with Gladiator Gunz Training Group, the company I now contract with. I had trained with the group before and was usually the only woman in class. I was hesitant to attend something more advanced because I was not sure I was there yet from an ability standpoint.
The team assured me I would be fine. It was training. The point was to learn, make mistakes, and improve—not arrive already knowing everything.
At the time, I was running the first rifle I had ever purchased: a roughly $500, 16-inch ArmaLite with iron sights and absolutely no frills. I had trained with it on and off for a year or two, and it had given me a solid foundation.

But as I prepared for a more demanding course, I started thinking more seriously about equipment.
You do not need a “Gucci gun” or the most expensive gear available to train effectively. You do need dependable equipment suited to what you are asking it to do. By then, I had tried other people's rifles and started identifying features that made shooting more comfortable and efficient for me.
I walked into a local gun shop and told them I needed a rifle for training. They handed me a POF Minuteman with a 10.5-inch barrel. At the time, I did not fully understand the tradeoffs that came with choosing that barrel length. I only knew it felt noticeably more manageable for my frame than the 16-inch rifle I had been using.

The shorter configuration fit the job. I needed something maneuverable for dynamic drills, movement, positional work, and the close-to-intermediate distances involved in the training I was doing. It would eventually become my home-defense rifle as well, another role where the compact configuration made sense to me.
Barrel length is always a trade-off. Shorter barrels offer maneuverability but generally give up some velocity and produce more muzzle blast. A 16-inch rifle remains a versatile middle ground, while longer barrels can better support applications where velocity and distance are priorities. There is no single barrel length for every job.
For mine, 10.5 inches made sense.
The current POF Minuteman configuration includes a Rugged Razor 556 suppressor, EOTech holographic sight and magnifier, Unity riser, PERST-4 laser, weapon-mounted light, and custom leopard-print finish by Red Status Gunworks. (Kim Jenkins photo)
My initial setup was fairly simple: an optic, a few changes to the controls, and a Radian ambidextrous charging handle.
About three months later, I took it to South Carolina. She ran flawlessly. The rifle felt like it had been made for me. It was easy to maneuver, comfortable through long training days, and allowed me to focus on learning instead of fighting my equipment.

From that point forward, she became my main squeeze.
The Rifle Evolved as I Did
The rifle did not stay simple. As my training progressed, I was exposed to suppressors, different optics, low-light work, night vision, and new shooting environments. With that exposure came a better understanding of what actually improved the rifle for me.
Its appearance evolved too. I love prints. They are one way I add some femininity and personality to a craft society still tends to view as male-dominated.
The leopard print came while I was teaching a course through my women’s program at a local gun shop in La Vernia, Texas, called Red Status Gunworks. The veteran-owned shop performs custom builds, Cerakote, firearm customization, and firearm sales.
The Friday-night series covered fundamentals, concealed carry, legal considerations, and practical firearm education, followed by mocktails and what I affectionately called “girl dinner.”
The shop had been following my work for some time and wanted to do something special for me. They told me to bring the rifle in. They already knew what I liked. When I got it back, the Minuteman had become the leopard-print beauty it is today.
The finish may be the first thing people notice, but the rifle had earned my trust long before it became pretty.
Years of training with the Minuteman have included movement, positional shooting, target transitions, and drills at distances ranging from approximately five to 100 yards. (Powers Photography)
The Current Setup
Today, the rifle wears a 
Rugged Razor556 suppressor
, EOTech holographic sight and magnifier, Unity riser, PERST-4 laser, Modlite weapon light, SBA4 brace, Radian charging handle, flat-faced trigger, and sling. None of these additions happened overnight. Each supports something I actually do with the rifle.

Advertisement

The Rugged Suppressors Razor556 was my second suppressor purchase, both facilitated through Silencer Shop, and it was definitely the more informed purchase.
First, I break things. My equipment gets used, so durability matters. I also appreciate the Razor's straightforward mounting system and Go/No-Go indicator. It is easy to install, easy to remove, and easy to visually confirm.
More importantly, it changes the experience of shooting a 10.5-inch 5.56 rifle.
Once I became “Team Suppressor”, it became very difficult to go back. Shooting a short 5.56 unsuppressed now feels almost uncivilized.
The suppressor does not make 5.56 silent, and hearing protection is still necessary, but reducing some of that blast and concussion makes the environment better for me and for everyone else on the firing line. These days suppressed is essentially this rifle's normal configuration.

Advertisement

There is some extra housekeeping. Suppressed rifles get dirty. More carbon and fouling work their way into the system, which means more attention to cleaning and lubrication. Cleaning guns is not exactly my favorite pastime, but it comes with the territory.
My EOTech and magnifier give me flexibility across the distances I normally train. At closer ranges, I run the holographic sight alone. When the distance stretches out, I can flip the magnifier into place for a clearer view without changing optics.
The Unity riser is equally important to me. I want to bring the rifle to my eye, not bring my face down searching for the optic. The raised position gives me a more natural, upright head position and a consistent sight picture. I also prefer the quick-detach feature because equipment can fail, and I like having options.
Then there is the PERST-4. I have PVS-31s and have used them during training, nighttime range sessions, and hog hunting in Texas. The PERST-4 gives me infrared-laser capability for that environment. Yes, the night-vision setup is also a little bit of a flex. But you cannot hate from outside the club.
The 
Modlite PLHv2
, however, is nonnegotiable. This rifle also serves a home-defense role, and I cannot responsibly make a decision about something I cannot identify. Its placement works specifically with my smaller hands and allows me to activate the light without substantially changing my support-hand grip.

That is a recurring theme with this entire build: it is set up for me, not for what someone else's rifle looks like online.
Weight, Balance and Training
This is in no way the lightest rifle I own. Suppressor, magnifier, laser, light, mounts—it all adds up.
The configuration evolved gradually, and I adapted with it. If anything, the additional weight has reinforced the importance of strength training. If I expect to shoulder, carry, and maneuver this rifle through a six- or eight-hour training day, I do not want fatigue to become the reason my technique deteriorates.
That does not mean adding everything possible to a rifle and trying to exercise your way around a bad setup. Weight placement matters. And this gun is balanced.
It may be heavier than some of my other builds, but it does not feel awkward or excessively front-heavy. The Razor adds weight at the muzzle, but on this rifle it helps the gun settle, and the overall configuration remains manageable during movement, transitions, and unsupported shooting.

Advertisement

The goal was never to build the lightest rifle possible. It was to build the rifle I wanted to train with.
More Than 5,000 Rounds Later
The ammunition I most often run through it is Freedom Munitions .223 Remington 55-grain FMJ. I buy it in bulk, train with it regularly, and it is also what is currently loaded in the rifle at home.
I know for a fact that I have put at least 5,000 rounds through this rifle. Those rounds have not all been fired standing on a square range. Training has included six- to eight-hour classes, two- to three-hour private cadre sessions, daylight work, low-light training, weapon-mounted-light work, and shooting under night vision. Depending on the drill, distances have ranged from roughly five yards to 100 yards.
At closer distances, the magnifier stays out of the way. When the drill stretches farther, I bring it into position. In darkness, the light and night-vision equipment get an opportunity to actually perform the jobs for which they were added.
Across all of that use, I have not experienced a rifle-related reliability issue that caused me to question my trust in the platform. For me, reliability is not a 200-round test conducted for an article. It is thousands of rounds, years of training, and repeatedly reaching for the same rifle knowing what I am going to get when I press the trigger.

KJ runs the 10.5-inch POF Minuteman during live-fire training. Although the finished rifle carries more equipment than the original configuration, its balance and familiarity make the added weight manageable during extended training sessions. (Powers Photography)
More Than a Pretty Rifle
This rifle sits beside my bed. That says more about my confidence in it than any spec sheet could.
I own other rifles. Some are lighter. I have shot other brands and configurations that I genuinely enjoy. But this one is mine.
Would I choose it again? Yes. There is nothing about the current configuration I would change today. Now I can’t tell you the exact all-in cost because it was never assembled from one shopping list. The rifle evolved as my skills and training changed.
I know how it moves. I know where it balances. I know the sight picture, the trigger, the light placement, and how the rifle feels after hours of training.
Personalization and capability are not opposites. A serious rifle does not have to be black, sterile, or identical to every other rifle on the firing line.

Advertisement

The problem is not making your rifle pretty. The problem would be prioritizing pretty over performance. This rifle earned my trust before it ever received the leopard-print finish. The finish simply made it look more like me. The training underneath it is what makes it mine.
Years ago, I bought it because I was worried I might not be ready for the next level of training. Today, it sits beside my bed because it is the rifle I trust most.
Train Smart. Carry Confident.
