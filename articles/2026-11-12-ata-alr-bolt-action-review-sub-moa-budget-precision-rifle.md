ATA ALR Bolt-Action Review: Sub-MOA Budget Precision Rifle?

Precision rifle features without the precision rifle price!

 The ATA ALR bolt-action rifle from Century Arms is designed for accuracy, control, and confidence. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

November 12, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

In case you aren’t familiar with Century Arms, the company is an importer, distributor, and manufacturer of rifles, handguns, and shotguns. The company started out in St. Albans, Vermont, in 1961 as Century International Arms, but now it’s known as Century Arms. From what I’ve learned, it began as a typewriter repair shop and then shifted to firearms in the early 1960s. Today, the company is headquartered in Delray Beach, Florida, and it also operates state-of-the-art factories in Fairfax, Vermont, and Georgia, Vermont. For much of their time in business, they imported military-surplus rifles; today, they import Russian WSR-10 rifles and the 
Turkish-made Canik pistols
, among other guns. And they also produce AKs domestically. The company also imports and distributes the Turkish-made ATA ALR bolt-action rifle, and 
Shooting Times
 received one right off the production line for this report.
According to the operator’s manual that came with the ALR rifle, “ATA Arms traces its roots to founder Celal Yollu’s early mechanical skills developed in Anatolia, beginning with the first shotgun he built in 1955. From those beginnings, the company has evolved from a small workshop into a leading Turkish firearms manufacturer, internationally recognized for its commitment to quality, safety, and R&D-driven production. Drawing on decades of experience in shotgun manufacturing, ATA Arms has expanded into rifle development and is proud to offer its ALR and ASR products to shooters worldwide.”
With that introduction, let’s get into the details of the ALR.
The buttstock folds, and it is adjustable for length of pull, cheekpiece height, and buttpad position. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
ALR Details
The ALR is a bolt-action repeating rifle with an aluminum chassis, a 24-inch barrel complete with an eight-slot (four on each side) muzzle brake, and a detachable box magazine. Let’s dig in a bit deeper, starting with the chassis and stock.

The stock is totally adjustable. Length of pull can be adjusted from 13.5 to 15 inches. Loosen the base-tightening nut on the left-hand side of the buttstock and push it. Slide the stock base forward or backward while pressing the adjustment nut. Release the nut when the desired length of pull is attained. Then the nut can be tightened. I did all the firing for this report with a length of pull of 13.5 inches.
The cheekpiece also is adjustable. Loosen the adjustment nut on the left-hand side, move the cheekpiece vertically to the desired height, and then tighten the nut.
The buttpad position can be raised, lowered, or rotated after rotating the thumbwheel in front of the buttpad counterclockwise. Once the desired position is reached, tighten the thumbwheel clockwise.

And the buttstock folds. Hold the grip with one hand and pull the stock back until you overcome the spring force. Continue pulling the stock to the rear while folding it to the right or to the left. Clearly, the ALR chassis offers a lot of adjustability. By the way, the fore-end has M-LOK slots on both sides and underneath.
The trigger also is adjustable, and you don’t need to remove the action from the chassis to do so. Simply use the included hex key to turn the easily accessible adjustment screw located inside the trigger guard just ahead of the trigger. Turning it clockwise increases the pull weight, and rotating it counterclockwise decreases the pull weight. It’s adjustable from 2.4 pounds to 3.75 pounds. I discovered that right out of the box, the trigger pull averaged 3 pounds, 2.8 ounces (3.18 pounds), according to my RCBS trigger pull gauge, so I did not adjust it for my shooting session. And by the way, the trigger is a two-stage type, and once the first stage was reached, the trigger broke cleanly and consistently.
The rifle’s fluted bolt has three locking lugs, a large knob, and a 60-degree lift. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The fluted bolt has three locking lugs; a 60-degree lift; and a large, knurled knob. The bolt release is located on the left-hand side of the receiver.
The sliding safety is positioned on the right-hand side of the receiver, and it is a three-position type that allows the safety to be engaged and the bolt to be locked in the full rearward position. In this position, a white dot is visible. In the middle position, the safety is engaged, but the bolt is not locked and can be operated. The white dot is visible in this position as well. The “Fire” position is with the safety all the way forward. In this position a red dot is visible.
The rifle comes without sights, but it does have a Picatinny top rail that makes installing a scope quite easy. I used my trusty Nightforce NX8 4-32X 50mm F2 scope for the shootout.

As I said earlier, the ALR features a detachable box magazine. Century’s website says the rifle comes with one magazine, but we received an extra one for our report. Both of them are AW-pattern double stack, double feed; made of metal; and hold 10 rounds each. The magazine release is a paddle type located in front of the trigger guard, and it’s ambidextrous. By the way, our rifle is chambered for 6.5 Creedmoor, but it’s also available from Century Arms chambered in .308 Winchester. The MSRP is $1,099.
The two-stage trigger is user adjustable from 2.4 pounds of pull to 3.75 pounds of pull. Our sample averaged 3.18 pounds right out of the box. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The rifle weighs 10.67 pounds and is 46.5 inches long overall. The barrel is 1.02 inches in diameter at the receiver, tapering to 0.79 inch at the point where the muzzle brake is attached. I have to admit, that muzzle brake makes shooting the rifle indoors really loud. I did all the shooting (for velocity and accuracy) from inside my shooting shed, and I wished I would have doubled up with ear plugs and earmuffs. My muffs alone made shooting a bit unpleasant due to the noise level, but recoil-wise, the rifle was very pleasant to shoot. I chalk that up to the substantial weight of the rifle.
Shooting Results
Speaking of shooting the ALR, I achieved some really good accuracy results. The details are listed in the accompanying chart, so I will just touch on some of the high points here.
Shooting Times
 protocol calls for five-shot groups at 100 yards, so I fired three, five-shot groups each with seven factory loads. Four of the loads carried 140-grain match bullets; one load had a 140-grain polymer-tipped, rapid-expanding hunting-type bullet; one load had a 130-grain match bullet; and the other load had a 120-grain “solid expansion” bullet. The best five-shot group accuracy average was 0.50 inch, and I got it with Federal’s American Eagle 140-grain Open Tip Match (OTM) ammo. Not far behind were Federal’s Gold Medal 130-grain Hybrid OTM load and Hornady’s 140-grain ELD Match load, both of which averaged 0.60 inch for their five-shot groups. All loads produced five-shot group averages of 0.90 inch or less, and the overall average for all five-shot groups was 0.71.
Left to Right: Magazine capacity for the AW-pattern detachable box magazine is 10 rounds. The rifle comes with one magazine, but Shooting Times received an extra one. The ALR comes with a large, eight-slot muzzle brake installed. Joel says it made the rifle’s report really loud. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Just for fun I kept track of the three-shot groups too. Two loads tied for first place, averaging 0.38 inch. They were the American Eagle 140-grain OTM and the Winchester Match 140-grain BTHP. The Federal Gold Medal 130-grain Hybrid OTM was really close at 0.40 inch. All loads had three-shot group averages measuring 0.75 inch or less, and the overall three-shot group average was 0.53. Clearly, the ALR is capable of very good accuracy.
The ALR is built for serious shooters. It delivers reliable, repeatable performance. And it’s durable yet refined.
