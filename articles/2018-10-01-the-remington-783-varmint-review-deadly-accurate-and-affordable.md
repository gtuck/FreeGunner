The Remington 783 Varmint Review: Deadly Accurate and Affordable

Heavy Barrel Precision That Outperforms Its Budget Price Tag

 Big Green’s accurate new 783 Varmint is the next big thing in ‘critter gitters.’ (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)

October 01, 2018

By Brad Fitzpatrick

Budget bolt-action rifles are big sellers, and in 2013 Remington announced its newest affordable entry-level turnbolt, the Model 783. As spiritual successor to the brand’s previous budget models, including the Model 788, the 783 had big shoes to fill—and it entered a field of affordable rifles that was more crowded than at any time in history—but the 783 was a success for Big Green because it proved accurate and reliable despite its low cost.
This year Remington has launched the 783 Varmint, a heavy target version of the sporter model that’s designed explicitly for shooting predators and small game. Like other previous 783 models, this newest version features a push-feed, dual-lug bolt with a sliding plate extractor and a plunger-style ejector in a tubular action, and the rifle comes equipped with Remington’s CrossFire adjustable bladed trigger. Other features that carry over from the previous model include a detachable metal box magazine, a two-position rocker-type safety and a barrel nut design that allows for precise headspacing. (

The standard 783 sporter is among the most affordable of all budget guns, with a starting suggested retail price of just $354 ($399 for a package model with a mounted scope). Those cost savings came, in part, thanks to the addition of a basic black injection-molded stock.
Features
The Varmint version, however, is priced at $625 and comes equipped with an upgraded nutmeg laminate stock with a beavertail fore-end and dual sling studs that’s well finished and fits the 783 barreled action nicely. There are no signs of cost-cutting on the stock, no obtrusive gaps or rough cuts around the magazine well or a gaping barrel channel. In fact, the stock looks similar to the laminate model used on the Model 700 VLS model—but it's not the same.

The beavertail-style fore-end sports two sling swivels—one for a sling, one for a bipod—and with mild-recoiling cartridges the gun stays on target thanks to its weight. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
At its widest point, the beavertail on this stock measures about 2.25 inches wide, so the rifle settles nicely into position for sustained shooting. The stock on the Model 783 Varmint is built to last and is a major upgrade over the polymer stock on the entry-level gun.
The heavy-contour, free-floated 26-inch barrel on the Model 783 Varmint measures 0.83 inch at the muzzle, and it features a recessed crown to protect the rifling. As you might expect, this does not make the 783 a particularly light rifle. Unscoped and unloaded, this gun weighs nine pounds, eight ounces. With a scope in place, the weight jumps to just over 11 pounds, but that’s not out of line with rifles in this class. Savage’s 110 Varmint, probably the 783’s closest competitor in terms of cost and dimensions, weighs 9.9 pounds sans scope.
The bulk of the 783’s weight is in the forward portion of the rifle, and the gun has a slightly nose-heavy feel with a balance point at the front of magazine well. When chambered for common varmint/predator cartridges like the .223 and .22-250, recoil is not a factor. Still, Remington added a dense, soft recoil pad to this gun for added comfort.

The 783 Varmint comes with a matte blued finish on the metalwork that’s durable and doesn’t threaten to alert wary ’yotes by reflecting light as high-gloss blued finishes can. The cylindrical action comes with a minimized ejection port, a popular feature on affordable bolt guns because reduced ejection ports increase rigidity in the action, which, in turn, improves accuracy.
Sporting versions of the 783 come with flattened bolt knobs that bear the Remington “R” logo, a styling cue that never particularly appealed to me. The Varmint version of the rifle ditches the flattened bolt knob for a rounded, oversize teardrop-shaped version that is, in my opinion, easier to cycle.
With its good-looking laminate stock, bladed CrossFire trigger and installed optics rail, the 783 Varmint offers a lot of features and excellent accuracy for just over $600. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
The Bolt
There’s minimal play when cycling the action, the bolt running smoothly along the raceway. The dual-lug design does require a 90-degree bolt lift as opposed to three-lug actions that permit bolt lifts closer to 60 degrees. The increased bolt lift didn’t interfere with cycling, didn’t result in bashed knuckles and didn’t significantly slow cycle time, though.
The rear of the bolt shroud is enclosed. There’s a small cocking indicator that protrudes from the rear of the shroud and offers both a tactile and a visual reference regarding the condition of the rifle. A bolt release tab is located on the left rear portion of the action opposite the two-position safety. The top rail mount offers plenty of space for mounting optics, and the four-round, detachable box magazine’s release is located at the front of the magazine.
Performance
On a blustery December morning with overcast skies, I headed to the shooting range to test the Model 783 Varmint’s accuracy potential. The test rifle was chambered in .22-250, and I mounted a Trijicon AccuPoint 2.5-12.5x42mm scope on the rifle using Trijicon’s heavy-duty 30mm rings, an ideal optic for a predator rifle since the scope’s tritium lamp offers battery-free illumination for hunting in low-light conditions.

The resulting rig produced very mild recoil. The weak little shove and barely-there muzzle rise made it easy to stay on target and call shots, which is an important feature on varmint and predator guns if you’re hunting alone and having to make quick holdover and windage adjustments.
The pistol grip of the 783 is comfortably angled and allows the shooter to easily reach the trigger, and the oversize trigger guard is designed to accommodate gloved fingers when hunting during the worst winter weather.
The CrossFire trigger, like Savage’s AccuTrigger and Mossberg’s LBA, features a bladed design that prevents discharges when the gun is dropped since the blade must be retracted before the gun can fire. My test rifle’s trigger was factory set for an average pull weight of 4.5 pounds, measured on an RCBS trigger gauge, but the trigger design is adjustable for weight. I do wish the blade on the trigger was a bit longer, but the system worked okay, and for a factory budget trigger, it’s fine.
I suppose you can quibble with various elements of the 783 Varmint’s design, but you simply cannot argue against the accuracy potential of this rifle—especially at this price. I tested three different loads from Remington, Browning and 
Hornady
, and each was capable of sub-m.o.a. accuracy—with several groups edging much closer to the half-inch range—and every single one of the loads produced at least a single group that measured less than 0.8 inch.
The best group of the day came via Hornady’s Varmint Express ammo with V-Max bullets: It went 0.46 inch. That’s pretty impressive accuracy for a rifle built on a budget action and using factory ammunition.
The detachable metal box magazine was initially difficult to the remove, but the problem eased. Fitzpatrick noted the magazine was finicky about cartridge position. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Evaluation
The 783 Varmint is a very accurate gun that’s fun to shoot because there’s no obtrusive muzzle blast or excessive recoil. The laminate stock looks good. Nothing about it screams budget, and it will hold up in the field through years of hard use. Having a top-rail pre-mounted is becoming more common on today’s rifles because it’s a convenient and robust design, and the dual sling studs are a nice addition since most of the hunters and shooters who buy this gun will probably be mounting a bipod.
I compiled a short list of negatives for the rifle, but none of them are particularly heinous. I prefer a raised comb on a rifle that will invariably be scoped, but that’s more of a taste issue than a detraction from the rifle’s design since there are plenty of shooters who will enjoy the Remington’s straight-comb design.
The trigger’s not bad and functions fine, as evidenced by the stellar accuracy, but I’m not a fan of the bladed design and would probably upgrade the gun with an aftermarket trigger.

Lastly, the box magazine was at times frustrating to use. The release on the front of the magazine is tight, and it requires the shooter to pry the mag free a bit when it’s new, but this happened less frequently as I became more familiar with the design. Also, the metal lips on the magazine are somewhat finicky about how the cartridges are seated.
Minor detractions don’t take away from just how well this gun shoots. The list of manufacturers making dedicated heavy-barrel bolt-action varmint rifles is rather small—though if you include “tactical” and “long-range” bolt models that are, essentially, heavy-barreled varmint-style rifles the list lengthens substantially.
Closing Thoughts
Nevertheless, at $625 the Model 783 Varmint is quite a bargain, and anyone who’s in the market for a new coyote or prairie dog rifle should give the 783 a hard look. It’s a fun gun to shoot, and anyone—young, old, novices, experienced shooters—can make this gun perform well, and you’ll never suffer from recoil fatigue. Or sticker shock.
