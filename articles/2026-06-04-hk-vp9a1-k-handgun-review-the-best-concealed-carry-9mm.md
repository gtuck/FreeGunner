HK VP9A1 K Handgun Review: The Best Concealed Carry 9mm?

One of the best duty handguns is now available in a compact model. How does it fare as a home defense or concealed carry pistol?

June 04, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

While “VP9A1 K” doesn’t exactly roll off the tongue, HK’s latest addition to the vaunted VP9 series of guns speaks for itself. Breaking it down, “VP” stands for 
Volkspistole
 or “people’s pistol,” and the A1 indicates it’s the latest generation of the VP9, which comes with a few key upgrades.
The “K” signals that it’s the compact version, or “Kompakt” 
auf Deutsch
, and it answers the call for a smaller VP9A1 more suitable for concealed carry. While it sports the same 4.09-inch barrel as the original for an overall length of 7.32 inches, height on the K version is just over five inches—0.3 inch shorter than its full-size brother. Nominal weight is 22.6 ounces with an unloaded mag. Those dimensions put it in the same ballpark as popular options like the Smith & Wesson M&P9 2.0 Compact, Glock 19 and others.
The pistol has a phenomenal trigger, with a light, clean break. The takedown lever makes an excellent indexing spot for the thumb. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Features
The VP9 platform dates back about a decade, and the recent A1 version brought several upgrades. I’ll start with the grip because I think this is one of the gun’s biggest selling points. The VP series offers not just interchangeable backstraps but also interchangeable side panels, and the newer A1 increases the potential combinations of panels and backstraps to create a truly custom fit.
The gun ships with the medium backstrap and medium panels installed. Small and large side panels plus small and large backstraps are included. Further, there’s a second medium backstrap with an extension at the top that increases reach to the trigger.

The A1 frame cashiers the 
original VP9
’s finger grooves for a straight, textured frontstrap. I think most people will find this to be a good move, because while finger grooves can be comfortable to shoot and can improve control, that’s only true if the groove layout matches your hand.
The A1 also sports a flared magazine well. It’s subtle but effective, not affecting concealment much at all while being a big help in speeding reloads. The gun’s 15-round metal magazines have base plates incorporating a serrated recess on each side to aid in stripping a stubborn magazine. The mags feature bright orange followers, something I always appreciate, and numbered witness holes at the 4, 6, 8, 10, 13 and 15-round marks.
When HK was making changes to the VP9, it also paid attention to the trigger. I’ve long been a champion of Walther triggers, calling them the best striker-fired triggers in the biz. I think the VP9A1 K’s is even better, thanks in part to a nickel-Teflon coated trigger bar. It has about a quarter-inch of take-up followed by a four-pound, three-ounce break that’s so clean you’d almost think you were shooting a 1911. Reset is short and distinct.

The pistol’s controls include a slide-lock lever that’s big enough to be used as a slide release but not so large it will catch on clothing during the draw.
The takedown lever is particularly well designed. It provides enough of a shelf to make a great landing spot for your thumb, and it’s perfect not only for indexing but also for enabling you to place downward pressure on it.
I’ve found both traits helpful to get a red dot on target more quickly. Because my thumb is always positioned the same way, the pistol points more consistently, and pressing down on the lever helps keep the muzzle flat—reducing the chances the dot will be too high in the window to see.
The HK employs an ambidextrous paddle magazine release. It’s rather short, but it’s easy to operate and drops magazines quickly. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Magazine Release
One control American shooters might find off-putting on this German-made gun is the ambidextrous paddle release at the back of the trigger guard. I actually like a paddle because I don’t have to shift the gun in my grip like I have to do with a lot of button releases. With the paddle, just press down with your index finger.
However, I did find the HK paddle has a somewhat sharp corner at the rear that hits the middle finger of my firing hand when I go to grip the gun. It’s irritating, and if the gun were mine I’d carefully round the corner edge with fine sandpaper.

Safeties
There is no manual thumb safety, but there’s a safety lever in the trigger along with a firing pin safety. There’s also a “disassembly safety.” You can’t rotate the takedown lever unless the magazine is out of the gun and the slide is locked back. Once in this condition, when you do flip down the lever, it automatically decocks the striker, so you don’t have to pull the trigger to field-strip the gun.
The VP9A1 K’s external extractor also serves as a loaded-chamber indicator. It projects slightly—very slightly—when there’s a round in the chamber, showing a sliver of a red mark. It’s not all that obvious, and I think you’re better off conducting a press check.
There’s also a red firing pin indicator in the rear of the slide. It doesn’t protrude, but when the gun is cocked you can see the red mark inside the rear of the slide. When it’s not cocked, you can’t.
The A1 slide features more aggressive front and rear slide serrations than the original VP9, although the serrated “charging supports” on the back of the slide—protrusions under the rear sight—remain unchanged. If you’re shooting the iron sights or if you use the pinch method to cycle the slide, you’ll really appreciate these projections.
The optics-equipped version came with a direct-mount Holosun, and the irons co-witnessed. The charging supports at the rear are a big aid in racking the slide. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Sights and Holosun Red Dot
The VP9A1 K is available with an optics-ready slide that uses adapters for mounting various red dots or as an optic-equipped model, the latter a $400 upgrade. That’s the version I tested. The sight is the Holosun VP9-GR, a solar-charging green-dot optic specifically designed for VP9 pistols. [
Ed. note: As we were getting this issue ready for press, HK announced it is partnering with Vortex for optics-equipped VP9s going forward.
] The sight mounts directly to the slide and therefore sits closer to the bore. That helps shooters pick up the dot more quickly.
The iron sights include a drift-adjustable serrated black rear and plain black front blade. The sights are tall enough to co-witness with the Holosun sight.
I know some gunmakers are dispensing with the rear sight on optics-ready guns—or not making their sight systems tall enough to co-witness—but I’m firmly in the redundancy camp: If an electro-optical sight should go down, I want backup irons that can keep me in the game without having to remove the optic.
The gun ships with small, medium and large grip panels that slide into the grip. There are also small and large backstraps, plus two mediums—one with an extension and one without. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Before taking the VP9A1 K to the range, I configured the grip the way I thought I’d shoot it best. As shipped—medium backstrap (no extension) and medium panels—the grip measured 2.2 inches front to back, with a 5.75-inch circumference and a 2.92-inch reach to the trigger. Side to side it was 1.32 inches at its thickest point.

Using a 3/32 roll-pin punch, I drove out the pin at the base of the grip and removed the backstrap and both panels. The panels are marked left and right in addition to size, and installation is simply a matter of matching up the female rail in the insert with the male counterpart in the frame and sliding in the panels. The manual notes that improperly installed grip panels can cause malfunctions, so take a second to make sure there are no gaps between the panels and the frame. The backstrap slides on easily.
It’s worth noting here that if you really want to experiment, you could, for example, run a small panel on one side and a medium or large on the other to create a sort of palm swell. I went with the small size all around, including the backstrap. Set up thusly, the grip measured 2.11 inches front to back, with a 5.56-inch circumference and a 1.29-inch width. Reach to trigger was 2.75 inches.
HK’s manual notes that the VP9A1 K is designed to fire full-power loads, and I chose test ammo accordingly. I should point out here that the pistol has a polygonal barrel. This design, which is used by other companies like Walther and Glock, promises higher velocity and potentially better accuracy because it provides a better gas seal and a stronger grip on the bullet. On the downside, it’s prone to lead fouling, meaning you’re better off sticking to jacketed bullets—which most of us do anyway.
The gun carried and drew nicely from a C&G Covert/Alpha IWB holster, and it proved accurate with a wide variety of loads—including the Black Hills 100-grain HoneyBadger. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Range Time
Bench accuracy was excellent, thanks in no small part to the terrific trigger. As you can see in the accompanying chart, all the 25-yard averages were under two inches. When I read HK’s note regarding ammo, I wondered how it would do with the Black Hills HoneyBadger—which, while a +P load, is light for caliber. Not only did it run 100 percent, it was also the most accurate of the loads I tested.
On the next range trip I ran a series of 5x5 drills with the gun, drawing from concealment with a C&G Covert/Alpha inside-the-waistband holster under a heavy winter coat on a day when the temps struggled to break freezing. Despite those handicaps, my times weren’t all that far off what I can do with a gun drawn from an outside-the-waistband holster and not concealed.

I conducted the repetitions with the Holosun’s green two m.o.a. dot and 2/32 m.o.a. circle/dot reticles; with the sight off, viewing the irons through the optic’s window; and with the optic off the gun, shooting the iron sights straight up.
The exercise reinforced that I’m almost always faster with irons. However, the first shots on target from the holster with the Holosun reticles were faster than what I usually achieve with an optic, which I attribute to how well the pistol points and the fact that takedown lever served so well as a landing spot for my thumb—as well as how close to the bore the optic sits.
Recently I had the chance to chat with a rep for XS Sights, and she noted that the company is seeing a demand for irons that co-witness in the lower fifth of the sight window, instead of the lower third many shooters had sought. HK’s sights sit in that lower fifth with the Holosun, and after shooting the irons through the optic’s window, I’m a total fan. The sight picture isn’t crowded, and you get a sufficient view of the front and rear sights to make alignment easy and fast.
HK’s irons are excellent all by themselves, producing a sight picture that’s fast to acquire and align. Had I not kicked a couple rounds outside the “down zero” zone on the IDPA target because I was pushing it, the plain iron sights run would’ve been one of my fastest 5x5 times ever.
Of course, you would expect excellent control from a gun weighing a pound and a half with sight. But the gun’s features and the ability to make the grip just right for your hand are huge contributors as well.

Reloads were a mixed bag. While I do like a paddle release, the HK’s paddle is pretty short, terminating a half-inch behind the trigger. It took a bit to get used to crooking my finger to hit the release instead of being able to press almost straight down, as I’d done with other paddle releases in the past. But it was fine once I got used to it. The metal mags dropped free and easy, and the mag well does an outstanding job of funneling fresh magazines into the pistol.
There were zero malfunctions during testing, with one exception: The slide would not consistently lock back on an empty magazine when using the Fiocchi 115-grain full-metal-jacket ammunition I used for drills. While it is full-power ammo, it apparently doesn’t have quite enough oomph to lock the slide; no other ammo displayed the problem. But even so, the Fiocchi never failed to eject or chamber.
I carried the VP9A1 K in the C&G holster for a couple of days. It’s just over the line for me in terms of size, but that’s because I’m short and stocky, which is usually not conducive to concealing guns of this size. It’s not that I found it uncomfortable or too heavy—it’s neither—but the grip sits high on my body and tends to print easily unless the cover garment is on the thicker side.
While I never carry appendix, I did try it out just to see, and the gun did conceal well in this position. I think “normal” people who are used to carrying guns larger than micros should have no problem concealing the VP9A1 K, either appendix or behind the hip.
I don’t usually comment on what kind of case comes with the gun, because most of them are so basic. Not the HK’s. Its black soft case features loops for five spare mags, which is especially nice if you shoot competition. Straps with hook-and-loop fasteners keep the pistol from shifting around in the case, and the twin zippers have holes for inserting a small lock.

Final Thoughts
HK pistols enjoy an excellent reputation for good reason. They’re well built, accurate and dependable guns that have served tons of military and law enforcement personnel, along with everyday citizens, for years. The VP9A1 K would fill the dual roles of home defense and concealed carry perfectly, and while it’s on the expensive side, it merits the attention of anyone looking for a good all-around defensive firearm.
