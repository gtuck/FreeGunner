.22 Trail Gun! Uberti's Single-Action Virginian

Classic Western Style Meets Practical Rimfire Performance

July 25, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

As much as we love to pontificate about what sidearm is best for defense against bears—or for that matter against two-legged predators—most of us actually use a nice rimfire trail gun far more than we ever will use a personal-protection gun. I’ve packed and used trail guns since I was 13 years old and have a soft spot for these useful tools.
My current personal go-to is the .22 LR single-action revolver shown here. Made by Uberti in Italy and imported under the Interarms umbrella, the model was named the Virginian, and it came in an assortment of sizes and calibers. Interarms went out of business in 1999; the Virginian was discontinued well before that.
Used versions of the Virginian are occasionally available on Gunbroker and sometimes can be found at gun shows. They go for $300 to $500 in very good condition. I am unable to determine how many were manufactured. Judging by its serial number, mine was likely made in the 1980s.
Fundamentally the Virginian is a scaled-down clone of 
Colt’s Single Action Army
, or rather, a Colt New Frontier. Like the New Frontier, the Virginian has a flat-top frame, an adjustable rear sight, and a ramped front sight. Plus, there’s a transfer bar incorporated in the hammer/trigger system that prevents accidental firing if the hammer takes a blow while lowered on a live cartridge.

The sleek little revolver features a correct .22 rimfire-size frame, elegant color-casehardened finish on the frame and polished blue on the barrel and grip frame, and walnut grips. Most Virginians were made with 5.5-inch barrels and were sold as convertible revolvers with two cylinders—one in .22 LR and one in .22 Magnum.
Mechanicals
If you are familiar with operating a classic Single Action Army type revolver, you’ll be familiar with the Virginian. Ear the hammer back to halfcock and open the side gate to load. Thumb fresh cartridges into the cylinder chambers, then close the loading gate. Don’t lower the hammer directly to the rest position, as that’ll scratch the cylinder; bring it back to fullcock, then carefully lower it to the rest position. Thanks to the transfer bar inside the hammer channel, it’s safe to carry with all six chambers loaded and the hammer down.
To fire, cock the hammer fully back, aim, and squeeze the trigger. Repeat. Each time the hammer is drawn back, the mechanism rotates the cylinder so that a fresh cartridge is presented and locks it in place as the hammer drops.

To remove fired cartridge casings—or unload live rounds—place the hammer on halfcock, open the loading gate, and use the ejector rod to thrust the cases out of the cylinder chambers.
To switch cylinders, halfcock the hammer, press the cylinder pin detent rod, and pull the cylinder pin forward until it releases the cylinder. Open the loading gate and lift the cylinder out of the frame. Place the other cylinder into the frame, finagle the cylinder pin through the center of the cylinder, and make sure the detent rod clicks into place.
As an aside, you can press the cylinder pin retaining rod and push the cylinder pin deep into the frame to lock up the mechanism for storage. The inside end of the cylinder pin will protrude into the hammer channel and prevent it from contacting the firing pin.
Provenance
I know next to nothing about the history of this particular Virginian .22 revolver. By the lack of wear and the fact that it’s still paired with the .22 Magnum cylinder (which tend to get lost), I can deduce it was stored a lot more than it was carried and fired. Aside from that, all I can tell is that it’s about 40 years old and in perfect working order.
Rangetime
Before testing the Virginian, I was curious about how well it would shoot. Many convertible .22 revolvers have their barrels bored to 0.224-inch diameter to fit .22 Magnum projectiles perfectly, rather than being bored to 0.222 inch to fit the .22 LR properly. As a result, accuracy with .22 LR ammo is often rather ho-hum.

I didn’t take the time to push a soft lead slug through the barrel and measure its diameter, figuring that groups on target would be indication enough.
The .22 functioned and fired like a champ. Trigger pull weight is a tad gritty, but it’s nice and light at just 2 pounds, 11 ounces. Feel and balance is just right, and pointability is so good the revolver feels almost alive.
I first tested Remington .22 LR Thunderbolt 40-grain RN ammo, as it often proves to be surprisingly accurate for a bulk load. Not this time; with an average of 2.13 over three consecutive five-shot groups, accuracy was usable but not inspiring. At this point in testing, I figured the Virginian had a bore diameter optimized for the .22 Magnum.
Then I tested American Eagle .22 LR ammo loaded with 38-grain HP copper-washed bullets. Holy moly! Groups clustered less than one inch at 25 yards. Average of all three five-shot groups was just 0.86 inch. That’s darned good for iron sights directed by middle-age eyes. When in need of a trail gun, I would gladly pack the Virginian stoked with that American Eagle ammo for the rest of my days.
After swapping out the .22 LR cylinder for the .22 Magnum version, I attempted to shoot groups using 
Hornady
 Critical Defense 45-grain FTX ammo. I write “attempted” because the bullets patterned like shotgun pellets. Even worse, at least one out of every five tumbled and impacted the target sideways.
A quick calculation with a bullet stability formula indicated that the bullets should be stable in the 1:16 twist, so I was left perplexed about what caused the bullets to tumble. Clearly, further testing with different .22 Magnum ammo is called for, but I didn’t have any on hand. Therefore, I am reporting only the .22 LR results in the accompanying chart.
Not to worry, though. I’m perfectly happy using the .22 LR cylinder and shooting the much cheaper .22 LR ammo through it. You can bet it’ll be prowling about the farm and on hikes with me.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
