Nosler M48 Rifle Full Review: A Prime Choice for Long-Range Hunting 

Like it or not, long-range hunting is a massive trend. The Nosler M48 with a Shilen barrel proves to be the best in its price category.

 The Nosler M48 is designed to be precise at long range. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)

February 01, 2018

By Joseph von Benedikt

Whether you agree with it or not, long-range hunting is a massive trend. Hunting ammunition, optics and rifles are being engineered to offer greater reach than ever before. Nosler’s new M48 Long Range rifle is one of the best (and probably 
is
 the best in its price category) of the crop.
For starters, even though it’s accurate and comes with a sub-m.o.a. accuracy guarantee, it’s designed to fall within classic deer-rifle weight parameters. It tips the scale right at 7.5 pounds bare.
Shilen Barrel
The heart of a good rifle is its action, but its soul lives in the barrel. Nosler’s rifles have match-grade stainless steel Shilen barrels that produce outstanding accuracy. The M48 Long Range is currently available in six different cartridges: 6.5 Creedmoor (1:8 twist), .26 Nosler (1:8), .28 Nosler (1:9), .300 Win. Mag. (1:10), .30 Nosler (1:10) and .33 Nosler (1:10).
A sleek, removable muzzle brake graces the end of the matte-black Cerakoted barrel, and each rifle comes with a thread protector for hunters who prefer to endure recoil rather than put up with the added blast of the brake. Hunters who prefer to shoot with a suppressor can easily spin off the brake and replace it with their preferred can. Barrel diameter at the thread shoulder is 0.630 inch, which is just adequate to properly square a suppressor. It’s worth using an alignment rod to confirm correct bore alignment with your suppressor before shooting to prevent a potential baffle strike.

Interestingly, the barrel is not fluted. Many if not most upper-crust precision hunting rifles feature fluting, which is advertised to minimize weight and maximize surface area for quicker cooling. I’m a fan of fluting if it’s masterfully done, but, candidly, much fluting isn’t. Nosler did not go the fluting route with the M48, and that’s okay. It’s the subject of some controversy, but there’s always the slightest chance that fluting can create issues with accuracy and cause barrels to heat unevenly and point of impact to wander as a result.
Predictably, the barrel is free-floated, in this case in a Manners carbon-fiber stock I’ll get to shortly. A just-right gap of .050 inch provides adequate barrel clearance on each side without being unsightly. The action and barrel are bedded perfectly straight in the stock using aluminum pillars and glass bedding compound.
The M48 Long Range’s action employs a fluted, Cerakoted, push-feed bolt and a two-position rocker safety. The trigger is an adjustable Timney. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Action
Nosler makes outstanding hunting rifle actions bordering on custom-action perfection. I’ve long considered them to offer adequate rigidity and tight-enough tolerances to support superb precision, without the reliability issues sometimes encountered in the tight tolerances of true custom actions.

Featuring dual, opposing-lug lockup, M48 bolts display sleek design and good machining. They feature shallow, straight fluting that holds lubricating oil, minimizes friction and provides a place for crud to accumulate without affecting reliable function. The bolt is matte black Cerakoted like the rest of the metal parts.
The nicely contoured bolt handle is machined integral to the bolt body, and it features a checkering-type knurled ring around the bolt knob that provides a good non-slip grasp. On every M48 rifle I’ve had the pleasure to examine, the wear on both locking lugs has shown equal wear on each side—which is what you want to see.
Of push-feed design, the recessed bolt face picks up fresh cartridges and slides them into the chamber as slick as the proverbial whistle. A robust 0.23-inch-wide M16-type extractor positioned at two o’clock, just above the right-hand locking lug, reliably hauls fired cases from the chamber, and a plunger-type ejector at 11 o’clock in the bolt face heaves them out of the action. To remove the bolt, depress the tidy little bolt catch located at the left rear of the action and draw out the bolt.
Trigger
I’m a trigger snob of the worst kind. Nosler has an astute understanding of go-switch addicts like me and others in the precision-shooting community. The M48 is fit with a beautifully tuned Timney. It’s adjustable, but there’s no need to bother. The sample I tested breaks crisply and displays no creep and indiscernible overtravel. The trigger shoe is a serrated, curved affair about 0.22 inch in width. A two-position rocker-type safety is built into the trigger, and it protrudes at the expected point just right of the bolt shroud.
Other Feautures
Bottom metal is one piece, made of aluminum and superbly contoured. The metal surfaces are perfectly finished before the matte black Cerakote coating is applied. Like the action, the bottom metal is glass bedded into the Manners carbon-fiber stock. Fit and finish are simply outstanding.

The floorplate is hinged and may be opened by pressing the small metal catch located where the front of the trigger bow transitions into the mag well. Both action bolts feature hex heads that are less susceptible to buggering and much easier to torque to correct spec than traditional cross-slot heads.
The Manners carbon-fiber stock has the colors embedded in the epoxy finish, so they won’t rub off. A groove in the high comb allows the bolt to come all the way back. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
On the subject of the stock, the Manners MCS-T Elite Tac features 100-percent carbon-fiber construction with a molded-in camo color that is impossible to scratch off. Nosler chose Manners’ Elite Swamp camo pattern, which features a mix of clear epoxy that allows the carbon-fiber weave to show through, a tan and an OD green—all tastefully applied into the hand-laid shell of the stock as it’s built. The grip offers a discreet palm swell, and both it and the fore-end feature molded-in texture.
A fairly high comb assists in achieving a consistent cheek weld, and the semi-flat-bottomed fore-end plays nice with bipods (two swivel studs are present, one for a bipod and the other for your carrying strap) and rides well on sandbags. That said, it’s thankfully not one of the wide beavertail-type fore-ends so uncomfortable to carry in the field. A deep Pachmayr Decelerator buttpad transfers recoil kindly.
Range Time
Currently, five major ammo manufacturers have long-range-specific lines of big game hunting ammunition. After mounting Leupold’s new VX-5HD on the rifle (see sidebar) and digging up a box of each in 
.300 Win. Mag
., I headed to the range.
Being relatively light, the rifle recoils with a bit of zest despite the muzzle brake. To me, it feels like it kicks about like a lightweight .270 without a brake, which is plenty manageable.
Nosler’s manual recommends a barrel break-in procedure, and it’s a good one. The guidelines are to clean after every shot for the first 10 rounds, every three for the next 15, then shoot five shots, clean, fire a fouler and then shoot a three-round group for accuracy. However, I was on a tight deadline, and since the cornerstone of this break-in process requires allowing the barrel to cool to ambient temperature, it takes more time than I had to devote to the project.
The barrel is a 26-inch Shilen stainless that’s treated to a matte black Cerakote finish. It comes with a nicely fitted muzzle brake and a thread protector. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Besides, the rifle shot beautifully from the first 100-yard group I recorded, turning in an average of slightly over three-quarter m.o.a. with 
Hornady
’s Precision Hunter ammo loaded with 200-grain ELD-X bullets. In fact, had I not introduced some human error and shot a not-great group as the last of my three consecutive three-shot groups for record, it would have averaged closer to one-half m.o.a. That’s fantastic accuracy for a semi-production rifle with factory ammunition.
Barnes’s new VOR-TX LR ammunition loaded with a 190-grain LRX bullet also posted a sub-m.o.a. average, and two of the other test loads averaged less than 1.25 m.o.a., as you can see in the accompanying chart.

Because it’s a hunting rifle, I fed all ammunition from the magazine, loading all three cartridges for each group and cycling them briskly rather than single-feeding and babying them as I might for a match rifle. Not only did the M48 action function with stellar reliability, it fed so smoothly that several times I withdrew the bolt slightly in doubt, thinking it hadn’t actually picked up and chambered a cartridge. In every case, it had.
Unlike many of today’s so-called precision hunting rifles that sport vertical pistol grips, adjustable combs and fat fore-ends, the M48 Long Range carries well in the hand and balances nicely in the offhand and other field positions. It doesn’t point like a vintage side-by-side, mind you, but it’s positively shootable. By the end of my range session, it was beginning to feel like an old friend, and I spent a double handful of ammo shooting distant targets from improvised positions.
Of the several cartridges the M48 Long Range is currently chambered for, the 6.5 Creedmoor will certainly be the easiest to shoot with extreme precision and is arguably the best choice for a hunter wanting easy-to-shoot ergonomics, long barrel life, and terminal performance adequate for all deer-size game and perhaps the occasional elk. However, for the wide-open West and the vastly different game I hunt, I’d pick either the .28 Nosler or the .300 Win. Mag. version.
The first is the most interesting to me and offers truly spectacular ballistics coupled with enough bullet weight and diameter to comfortably handle the biggest elk and to adequately step up to moose. It’s a handloader’s dream for big game at extended ranges. If you don’t handload, though, the .300 Win. Mag. offers outstanding versatility and a plethora of purpose-built hunting ammunition. It offers plenty of authority for any game in the Lower 48 and even for big bears, assuming there is an appropriate projectile choice.
Final Thoughts
Whether you yearn to be ethically lethal on big game standing 600 yards distant or merely appreciate accuracy and want to be able to place your inside-300 shots on whitetails with surgical precision, Nosler’s 48 Long Range rifle is worth a close look. It is unfailingly reliable, looks great, feels great and shoots like custom rifles that cost double its price.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
