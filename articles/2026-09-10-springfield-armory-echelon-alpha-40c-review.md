Springfield Armory Echelon Alpha 4.0C Review

A Budget-Friendly Echelon

 (Yamil Sued photo)

September 10, 2026

By Rich Nance

If you’ve been to the grocery store or the gas pump lately, you know the cost of everyday goods and services is on the rise. In the current economy, people have less disposable income. Fortunately, thanks to Springfield Armory, you might still be able to afford a variant of its popular Echelon pistol. While 
the standard Echelon 4.0C
 has a suggested retail price of $710, the Alpha 4.0C’s suggested retail is just $599.
The budget-friendly Alpha 4.0C has fewer frills than the standard Echelon, but it functions the same. As with all Echelon pistols, the Alpha 4.0C’s serialized component is the Central Operating Group (COG)—a stainless steel chassis that can be swapped between grip modules. Springfield and a couple of aftermarket makers offer these modules in various configurations.
The Alpha 4.0C also features the Variable Interface System (VIS), which facilitates the direct mounting of a variety of popular optic footprints via a pin system that exerts inward pressure on the optic base, preventing lateral movement. While the standard Echelon’s VIS accommodates more than 30 different sights, the Alpha 4.0C VIS comes with pins only for the Trijicon RMR footprint—although you can still order other pin sets, along with adapter plates for the Docter and Aimpoint Acro footprints, separately.
The Alpha has deeper slide serrations than the standard Echelon but lacks the regular model’s forward trench. (Yamil Sued photo)
When you compare the Alpha 4.0C to the standard Echelon, you’ll notice a few other differences. For starters, the Alpha 4.0C’s slide has been recontoured. It lacks the Echelon’s distinctive forward trench and rear flare. Instead, the Alpha 4.0C’s slide is more squared, with deeper front and rear serrations. In addition, the Alpha’s slide cover plate is polymer, rather than metal as with standard Echelon models.

The Alpha 4.0C’s front sight uses a plain white dot instead of a tritium night sight like the standard 4.0C. While this is clearly a cost-saving effort, some may prefer it because in the dark a glowing front sight could distract you from the dot of your optic.
The Alpha 4.0C is dimensionally identical to the Echelon 4.0C. It’s 7.25 inches long and 5.12 inches tall, with a 1.2-inch grip width. Both come with the small grip module, making them easy to conceal, while supporting a full three-fingered firing grip.
The Alpha 4.0C uses interchangeable backstraps, but unlike the standard Echelon, it comes only with the medium size. But, as with the VIS pins, you can order small and large backstraps from Springfield’s web store.

There’s no rear slide flare on the Alpha, and the optic plate is polymer instead of metal, but you still get the Tactical Rack U-notch rear sight. (Yamil Sued photo)
The Alpha 4.0C comes with a single, 15-round stainless steel magazine instead of the 15- and 18-round coated magazines you get with the standard Echelon. However, the Alpha 4.0C will accept magazines designed for other Echelon models. There’s also a 10-round Alpha for restricted states, as well as a California-approved 10-round Alpha with a loaded-chamber indicator that retails for $50 more.
For testing, I ordered a plate to mount an Aimpoint Acro P-2 to the Alpha 4.0C. It worked fine, although a direct mount is always better. By eliminating a mounting plate, you remove a potential failure point. Also, without a plate, the optic will sit lower—maybe low enough to use the standard-height sights as backup iron sights.
Since I prefer the small grip module and the medium backstrap, the Alpha 4.0C felt familiar right out of the box. It handled like the many Echelon variants I’ve fired over the years, and it was easy to forget that the Alpha is the economy model. In fact, if it weren’t for the different magazine, white dot front sight and redesigned slide, I wouldn’t be able to tell the Alpha 4.0C from the standard 4.0C. The Alpha 4.0C even fit perfectly in my 4.0C holsters.
The Alpha 4.0C retains the Echelon control layout and the grip geometry of the standard
The Alpha 4.0C’s grip ergonomics, trigger pull and ambidextrous controls are all standard Echelon. The trigger pull on my test gun broke at five pounds of pressure. It was clean and smooth breaking, especially for a striker-fired pistol. Reset was positive.
 The Alpha 4.0C functioned flawlessly during testing, which consisted of benchrest accuracy at 25 yards and practical, offhand shooting at closer ranges. Accuracy wise, the Alpha 4.0C delivered. Average five-shot group sizes ranged from 2 to 2.9 inches.

Having run an Echelon during a recent five-day Gunsite 250 Defensive Pistol course, it’s a platform I trust. Like all Echelons, the Alpha 4.0C is a well-balanced fighting pistol. As expected, it more than held its own during rapid-fire drills. The Alpha 4.0C is easy to shoot, while being compact and slim enough to conceal well.
The Alpha 4.0C is an Echelon in all ways when it comes to quality and functionality. If you’ve been wanting an Echelon and can do without extra backstraps and VIS pins, a spare magazine and a tritium front sight, the Alpha will save you $111 compared to the standard 4.0C. You can put that toward your groceries, gas, or better yet, ammo.
