Pistols in America Across 250 Years

Free men own guns, and there's perhaps no better symbol than a handgun to distinguish an American from another person living elsewhere in the world.

 (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)

July 02, 2026

By James Tarr

Pistols were intended to be a worst-­case-­scenario defensive weapon. For many of us, that description hasn’t changed. While there is and has always been recreational plinking, target shooting, competition, and hunting, self-­defense has been the principal reason law-­abiding American citizens buy handguns.
Since the founding of our nation, Americans have witnessed the transition from single-­shot blackpowder muzzleloading technology to five- and six-­shot cartridge-­fed revolvers, to magazine-­fed autoloaders that offer double-­digit capacities and rounds-­between-­failure reliability measured in the tens of thousands.
Handguns used to be made exclusively by hand, some artisans shaping steel and wood into works of art. Since the 20th century, modern steel alloy chemistry, polymer technology, and computer-­aided machines have advanced precision and scalability, while allowing engineers to express their creativity through 3D modeling. The best part is, we can still find handmade examples that are works of art. Though mainstream media and politicians aim to criminalize and demonize the lawful manufacture of firearms for personal use, they are racing against a 3D-­printing hobby that enables us to better build firearm frames in the privacy of home. It would make the Founding Fathers proud.
In an era where hunters and hunting are declining, there have never been more handgun owners in America. Female ownership has significantly increased, as have the number of female-­focused firearms organizations: A Girl and a Gun (agirlandagun.org), 
Shoot Like a Girl
, Armed Women of America (armedwomen.org), are a few. Hollywood pretends a 130-­pound woman can beat most 220-­pound men, but the only true equalizer is a handgun.

Action pistol competitions are available every week in most parts of the U.S. Teaching skills and innovating techniques are an important part of that experience. Even as outdoor rifle ranges disappear to urban sprawl, indoor ranges are sprouting. For example, I live in the Detroit area. Forty years ago, there were three indoor ranges near me. Today, I can count at least 10, some privately owned while others are corporate chains. The same is true for most metropolitan areas in the U.S.
America is the largest consumer of firearms in the world, and it’s not even close. Some manufacturers sell more firearms in the U.S. than they do in the rest of the world combined! Why? Except for some exceptions, most adults in America are allowed to own a handgun — or 80! Serious gun collectors even exceed that number, which is not the situation for the rest of the world.
When considering 250 years of American history, this is a special place in terms of our relationship with firearms. The Second Amendment of the Bill of Rights wasn’t written to give us access to firearms to defend ourselves from the common street criminal. It was written to guarantee our continued access to weapons of war in case we had to fight off another tyrannical government. Recreation and defense against crime are how handguns are most often employed by fellow citizens. With almost no restrictions in most states, the Second Amendment makes our culture unique on the world stage.

For the first century of the American experience, there really weren’t laws regulating the type of firearms we could buy, or how we could carry handguns. In the wake of the Civil War, progressive solutions began restricting our rights.
I became a gun enthusiast during the 1980s, back when the practice of concealed carry was unusual. In fact, only one state — New Hampshire, the “Live Free or Die” state — had “Constitutional Carry” in its books. As of 2026, 28 states recognize Constitutional Carry. In the ’80s, Florida pioneered “Shall Issue” concealed carry permits, and now that is more often the rule than the exception. The number of states where citizens can’t easily acquire a conceal-­carry permit is in the single digits. A few states may always be subject to restrictive gun laws, but America has generally been moving steadily in the right direction, toward “… shall not be infringed.”
Free men own guns, and there’s perhaps no better symbol than a handgun to distinguish an American from another person living elsewhere in the world. Our handguns are, in every way, better than they’ve ever been. 

James Tarr

Handgun Editor

 James Tarr has worked as an armored car driver and a police officer, and then spent most of the next 20 years as a private investigator in and around Detroit.
