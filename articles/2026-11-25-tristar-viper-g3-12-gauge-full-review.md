Tristar Viper G3 12 Gauge: Full Review

The latest generation of the Viper shotgun from TriStar is here to prove its worth. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)

November 25, 2026

By Guns & Ammo Staff

More and More hunters and shooters are learning that the “MADE IN TURKEY” represents value and quality. Depending on who the importer is, customer service stands ready to impress also.
TriStar Arms has been one of the companies that has helped change opinions in favor of Turkish guns, and that came as a reminder when Guns & Ammo’s staff first received the TriStar Viper G2 in 2007. The gas-­operated semiauto convinced skeptics and sold them on its low price. Freedom Group purchased Remington around that time, and prices of the venerable Model 1100 were on the rise. Shooters gave the Viper G2 a look and quietly conceded that it was a robust, reliable shotgun.
Though straight grain and lacking figure, the Turkish walnut is warm, handsome and strong. The comb, thumb notch, and grip contour are comfortable and intuitive. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
TriStar’s tagline is “The Value Experts,” in part due to the Viper G2’s success. The Viper G2 has been in production since the Bush Administration, and much about manufacturing and the shooting sports has advanced in 20 years.
Enter the Viper G3 in August 2026
. In many ways, it’s similar to the G2 that it replaces, and much of the G3’s upgrades appear cosmetic. What hasn’t changed is the reliability and affordable market the Viper G3 aims for.

First, A little History...
You wouldn’t be reading about TriStar or the Viper G3 if not for Gus Bader. In 1983, Bader started American Arms, a firearm importation company. By 1996, the political climate prompted Bader to close the doors on American Arms, but he continued to work in the firearm industry.
Two of Bader’s former American Arms employees formed an alliance with another investor, creating TriStar. (Hence, the name. “Tri-” for “three.”) The fledgling company fell on hard times during the early 2000s. Bader’s former employees asked him to consider purchasing their share of TriStar, but Bader admitted to G&A that he had reservations about doing so. In an interview, Bader said that he believed TriStar had a 50-50 chance of avoiding insolvency, but he gambled and bought TriStar in 2005.
(Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Under Bader’s direction, Tristar recovered from its financial woes. Today, the company is flourishing. Gus Bader’s son, Ryan, is the vice president of Sales and Marketing for TriStar. A major factor in the company’s turnaround has been its commitment to working with its Turkish manufacturers to elevate the quality. Buyers who were willing to give TriStar a shot were rewarded with a fine firearm at a fair price. Word spread and the Bader family continued working with factories in Turkey to improve the quality of their product and help TriStar shotguns keep pace with more expensive shotguns manufactured in Italy and the United States. The Viper G2 became the model that elevated perception of Turkish manufacturing.

The Generation 3 TriStar Viper
Much of the Viper G2’s architecture was carried over to the G3. Both guns share similar operating systems that rely on gases to cycle the action. The G3 features two ports in the bottom of the barrel that channels gas into the operating system, and the force generated by these gases pushes a piston. The piston moves rearward on the action ring, which is attached to twin action bars and, finally, the bolt body. After firing, the gas pressure weakens and the return spring surrounding the magazine tube overcomes the energy and drives the bolt assembly and action bars forward, back into battery. It’s a relatively simple and robust system with no O-­rings to replace. 
Controls are larger than the bolt handles and bolt releases on shotguns offered 20 years ago. Oversized controls were incorporated from the competition scene and are now standard details. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Cleaning is simply a matter of clearing the gas ports — a small wire brush works — and wiping down the piston and cylinder. TriStar says the Viper G3 can fire 1,000 rounds between cleanings, but fieldstripping and cleaning take no more than 10 minutes. The Viper G3 box also includes a red “fast” or “heavy” piston for use when shooting magnum loads. TriStar classifies magnums as 12-­gauge loads weighing more than 1¼ ounces or traveling at 1,400 feet per second (fps). Swapping pistons requires nothing more than pulling the standard silver piston free and replacing it with the red piston. The color is quick to identify.
The forend cap is a conventional thread-on design. It is unscrewed to remove the walnut hanguard and provide access to the gas operating system for cleaning and maintenance. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
It’s generally accepted that gas guns reduce recoil, but that’s not exactly true. Gas systems prolong recoil as gas cycles through the action. Practically speaking, gas guns reduce perceived recoil — still a benefit. Extended range sessions shooting 12-gauge shells can leave you feeling ragged, but the TriStar Viper G3 wasn’t uncomfortable. In fact, recoil felt quite manageable. The Viper G3 would be a suitable option for new or recoil-­sensitive shooters.
G&A’s Viper G3 sample wore a Turkish walnut stock and black anodized and blued finish. It has a 3-­inch chamber and comes with a 28-­inch vent-rib barrel. Unloaded weight tested at 7 pounds, 6 ounces, which hints that the G3 was intended to be a versatile multi-­purpose shotgun. Heavy 12-­gauge guns weighing 8 pounds or more are great for target shooting because they absorb recoil effectively, but they’re a burden to carry in the field all day. Ultralight 12 gauges in the range of 6 pounds are what you want when trekking rolling terrain and hunting wild birds, but if you take a light 12-­gauge field gun for a day of clay target shooting, you’ll suffer from the recoil. Seven pounders such the Viper G3 are right in the middle. The Tristar is light enough to carry afield but heavy enough for range work.
Two pistons are included with the Viper G3. The red piston facilitates high-velocity shotshells loaded to 1 1/4 ounce or greater. The silver piston is for standard loads. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The Viper G3 has a ventilated top rib that is flat and serrated to cut glare for midday shooting. The rib measured 6.8mm at the receiver and at the muzzle, so it’s not tapered the way some target ribs are on more expensive shotguns. Choosing a flat rib or stepped rib is also a matter of taste than function. Most American shotguns are equipped with flat ribs, so this is not unusual. A flat rib such as the one on the G3 prompts shooters to keep at solid cheekweld on the gun. TriStar also provides a red fiber-optic front sight.

Viper G3 bolts feature an extractor mounted to the outboard side of the bolt. There’s a small, fixed ejector at the rear-inside portion of the receiver wall. The ejector was so tiny that it appears sheared off, but it worked fine during our testing. It is noticeably smaller than the ejector on a Beretta A300 or A400. 
The receiver is made from machined 7075 aluminum alloy and it’s anodized with a reflective black finish. The chrome-­lined barrel is made from 4140 steel, so it has a blue finish.
The Viper G3 is manufactured by Khan Industries, and the company deep drills each of the barrels. The process is more expensive than standard hammer forging, but deep-bore drilling offers two advantages. First, the steel isn’t stressed during the way that it when forged. Second, the barrels are straight and typically shoot to point of impact.
The front sight is a red fiber optic dot producing a bright bead that naturally adjusts with ambient light. It’s fastened to a vent rib that’s serrated to prevent glare. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
At the Range
The Viper G3 did just that. Impacts at 40 yards with Federal Field & Target 12-­gauge loads with 11/8-­ounces of lead No. 71/2 shot per load, and a velocity of 1,200 fps, was slightly low. In shotgun parlance, it would be considered a 45/55 pattern with 45 percent of the shot landing above the point and aim and approximately 55 percent falling below. Average pellet counts were higher in the left half of the target, so point of aim is slightly above and to the right of the center of the target. The Viper G3 patterned better than many other semiauto shotguns. If you’re searching for a 50/50 field gun — which is the case for most hunters and casual clay target shooters — the Viper G3 could be an affordable solution for you.
The Viper G3 is offered with a lovely Turkish walnut stock that shouldn’t be taken for granted. The walnut stock is given an aggressive laser checkering that’s reminiscent of fish scale wood checkering. 
It has a standard 14¼-inch length of pull. Two additional spacers are included, each measuring a quarter-inch, though the model we had was an early production gun that came with quarter-inch spacers that did not fit the gun. Whether they were drilled incorrectly or simply dropped into the wrong box, it’s hard to say, but TriStar customer service was eager to solve the situation.  The drop and cast shims worked fine. The shooter only needs a half-inch or 13mm socket wrench with an extension.
Gas-operated shotguns are reliable. With the TriStar G3 updates are subtle, but it was tested to more than 1,000 shots between cleanings. Note the silver gas piston for standard shells and dual action bars under the bolt. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The trigger on the Viper G3 is actually one of the gun’s best features. There’s a fair amount of take up prior to the shot, but no more than most competing semiautos. Its average trigger weight tested at 43/4 pounds for 10 pulls on a Wheeler gauge. That may not sound great to a rifle shooter, but considering that many factory shotguns have triggers that average more than 6 pounds, the Viper G3 possesses one of the better triggers in this sub-$1,000 price point. The safety is familiarly located at the rear of the triggerguard, so it’s an easy reach.
The grip has a turned-down style with fish scale-like laser texture borderd by classic points. The straight grain and circumference of the small of the stock suggests long-term durability. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Underneath, the beveled opening on the loading port is large enough that it won’t bite your fingers when loading the gun. The oversized shotgun controls on the right is a mixed blessing. The bolt release is easy to use, but it’s sometimes too easy. On more than one occasion it was unintentionally pressed to send the bolt into battery.

Final Thoughts
After spending some time at the range with the Viper G3, it’s clear that it’s a good gun. It’s performed well, shot to point of aim, and was comfortable to use. 
Accessories for the TriStar Viper G3 shotgun include length-of-pull and cast shims, and buttpad spacers. A heavy piston, five chokes in a convenient case, and choke wrench are nice to have. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
A trio of Beretta/Benelli Mobil-pattern choke tubes is provided, and the case contains other goodies such as a choke tube wrench, drop/cast shims, and the spare piston. While the price wasn’t official at the time of press, our contacts at TriStar let us know that the suggested retail price is $799; a bit more than the G2. Not bad for 20 years!
