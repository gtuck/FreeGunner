## Article Text (extracted)

Dan Wesson Specialist 1911 | Duty-Proven. Civilian Ready.

Match-Grade Accuracy Meets Elite Law Enforcement Reliability.

 Dan Wesson’s Specialist 1911s come in several configurations, and we got our hands on the full-size 10mm Auto variant. 

June 23, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

The last time I wrote about a Dan Wesson pistol for 
Shooting Times
 was 2023, and that report was focused on the DWX hybrid semiautomatic pistol that combined elements of the classic Model 1911 and the respected CZ75. The pistol I’m reviewing here is a straight-up, good old Model 1911. It’s called the Specialist, and the model was originally designed for law enforcement use. It is now offered for civilians and chambered in 9mm Luger, .45 ACP, and 10mm Auto. Some Specialists are offered with 4.25-inch barrels or 5.0-inch barrels. My pistol is a 5.0-inch-barreled 10mm Auto.
Features
In my opinion, the Specialist is really classy, and that shows in many of the pistol’s very nice touches. Its slide has a flattened area running all the way from the front of the ejection port to just in front of the front sight, and this area is finely grooved. Dan Wesson calls it an inverted rib. The sides of the slide have wide front and rear grasping grooves (five on each side up front and seven on each side at the rear), and the name “Specialist” is etched into the bottom of the left side just above where the slide stop resides in the frame.
The ejection port is lowered and contoured, and a case clearance relief at the rear portion of the port has been sort of dished out. The slide has a satiny black nitride finish, but the stainless-steel barrel bushing and recoil spring guide rod plug have a matte stainless finish. The end of the recoil spring plug is checkered.
The Specialist’s grip panels are textured G10, and they are held in place by hex-head screws. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The forged stainless-steel frame is the same satiny black color as the slide, and it has an integral accessory rail on the dustcover. The rail has a single cross-slot. The frame’s frontstrap is checkered 25 lines per inch (lpi). And yes, I counted it. Plus, the area where the top of the frontstrap meets the trigger guard is contoured to allow a high handhold. On the other side, the flat, checkered (25 lpi) mainspring housing has a magazine well funnel at the bottom to make inserting the magazine easier.

The ambidextrous grooved manual thumb safety is extended and a bit larger than usual, but it’s not so large that it is obtrusive. The beavertail grip safety has a memory bump at the bottom for consistent engagement. The slide stop is grooved, and the extended magazine release is checkered. The magazine release, slide stop, thumb safety, grip safety, and mainspring housing all wear a satin black finish.
Inside, the frame is machined to accept the ramped barrel. (I’ll get to more about the barrel in a moment.) And a long, solid aluminum trigger with vertical grooves on the face and an overtravel adjustment screw is installed. My pistol’s trigger pull averaged 4 pounds, 7 ounces, with a variation of 10 ounces over a series of 10 measurements with an RCBS trigger pull scale. It is very crisp and clean, with a slight amount of take-up but no detectable overtravel.
The skeletonized hammer has fine grooves on the rounded portion. It, too, is matte stainless finish.

It’s been said that the barrel is the heart of every Model 1911. The Specialist’s stainless-steel barrel is 5.0 inches long. The muzzle end is slightly bigger in diameter (outside diameter measures 0.58 inch at the muzzle, according to my digital calipers) than the rest of the tube, which helps ensure a good tight fit with the barrel bushing, and the muzzle itself has a full-diameter precision crown. As I said earlier, the barrel is ramped, meaning the chamber is fully supported, and Dan Wesson marks the chamber with the word “Match.”
Right out of the box, the fit of the slide to the frame and the barrel to the slide are very tight. There is no gap between the rails of the frame and the slide at the rear above the grip safety. I didn’t detect any wiggle between the slide and the frame, and the barrel locked up nice and tight, too.
The black and tan grips are made of G10, and they are textured in a distinctive pattern. The accompanying photographs illustrate it better than I can describe it in words, but I can say that it provides a secure gripping surface. Each grip panel is held to the frame with two black hex-head screws.
The Dan Wesson Battlehook ledge-type rear sight is dovetailed and held fast by two screws. Its face has fine horizontal striations and a U-shaped notch that is 0.14 inch wide at the top. It bears the Dan Wesson logo on the front flat portion. The whole unit has been recessed into the top of the slide’s optic cut cover plate. That’s right. This Model 1911 is optic ready, which is the trend for just about all other types of semiautomatic pistols these days. Model 1911s are coming on board with this trend; however, there are still a lot of standard 1911s out there that are not optic ready yet. This one is.
The Specialist’s front sight features a tritium dot surrounded by a white ring. The post is 0.127 inch thick and 0.194 inch tall (as best as I can measure the height). And the front sight is dovetailed into the slide.

Our 10mm Auto Specialist came with two stainless-steel magazines with rounded followers, and each one holds eight rounds of ammunition. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The Specialist comes with two steel magazines that hold eight rounds each. The magazines are marked “DW,” “10mm,” and “Made in USA,” and they have witness holes on both sides, rounded steel followers, and rubber basepads. The basepads are attached with two Phillips-head screws.
The pistol comes with another really nice touch. I’m referring to the elegant box that it comes in. Inside the sleeved, blue, flip-top box is a layer of foam that’s been cut out to fit the pistol, the magazines, and some small accessory items. That foam layer is removable, and underneath it is another foam layer that’s cut out for a good-size cloth pouch that contains the operator’s manual and a padlock-style gun lock.
The 10mm Specialist
 is 8.75 inches long, 1.45 inches thick, and 5.50 inches tall. The grip circumference is 5.38 inches (with the grip safety depressed); the sight radius is 6.5 inches; and the pistol weighs 42.8 ounces unloaded. At the time of this writing, the manufacturer’s suggested retail price is $3,199.
Performance
I’ve said it many times: The 10mm Auto cartridge is a round that has a cult following, although based on the number of new guns and factory loads introduced over the last several years, the cartridge is experiencing an upswing in popularity. Originally, when it was unveiled to the world in 1983, it was intended for duty use. It didn’t catch on there for a number of reasons, but it appealed to a select few handgunners for everything from home defense to handgun hunting. It’s one of my personal favorites, and I truly enjoy shooting Model 1911s chambered for it.
The solid aluminum trigger has a grooved face and an overtravel adjustment screw. Our sample’s pull broke cleanly and consistently at 4 pounds, 7 ounces. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
With that said, I fired seven different 10mm Auto factory loads in the Specialist. Bullet weights ranged from 175 grains through 180 grains to 200 grains, and styles ranged from hollowpoints and polymer-tipped FlexLocks through jacketed softpoints and synthetic jacketed flatnoses to lead roundnose flatpoints and full metal jackets. The pistol fed, fired, extracted, and ejected all types perfectly.
Velocity ranged from 1,062 fps to 1,203 fps, measured 12 feet from the muzzle with a Competition Electronics ProChrono LTD chronograph and the company’s indoor light setup. SIG SAUER’s 180-grain JHP load churned up the highest velocity, and Speer’s Personal Protection 200-grain Gold Dot hollowpoint load produced the lowest. I’ll draw your attention to the extremely low velocity extreme spread and standard deviation for the Federal Fusion 200-grain loading. At 6 and 2 fps, respectively, they are some of the lowest single-digit numbers I’ve ever achieved.
As for accuracy, I fired three, five-shot groups with each load at a range of 25 yards from a sandbag benchrest. I then averaged those groups for each load and calculated the overall average accuracy for all loads. The overall average accuracy for all loads was 2.42 inches. Two loadings tied for first place. They are the 
Hornady
 Critical Duty 175-grain FlexLock and the Federal Solid Core 200-grain TSJFN, and their accuracy averages were 2.00 inches. The complete results are listed in the accompanying chart.
After working with the Dan Wesson 10mm Specialist, I’ve decided it is a great tool for home and personal defense, and it will do nicely for hunting deer, hogs, and black bears, too. Every year the average price of a good, solid Model 1911 seems to go up, and the Specialist is not a cheap pistol by any means. However, the MSRP of $3,199 is not out of line for what you get. This timeless, top-drawer Model 1911 is extremely well made; it’s accurate and reliable; and it is very nice looking. To my way of thinking, it’s everything a Model 1911 should be.
