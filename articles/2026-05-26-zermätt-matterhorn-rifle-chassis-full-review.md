Zermätt Matterhorn Rifle Chassis: Full Review

This new chassis is feature-rich and great to look at. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

May 26, 2026

By Tom Beckstrand

If you’ve ever loved the lines and functionality of a stock but wanted the luxurious functionality of a premium chassis, keep reading. There’s lots of good chassis out there, but almost all of them have common appearance and shooting characteristics. 
Zermätt’s new Matterhorn chassis
 looks unlike anything else and has features that others do not.
The Matterhorn is currently available for short-action Remington 700 clones, but medium- and long-action chassis are planned for the future. The chassis body is made from 6061 aluminum with wood composite panels alongside the forend, the grip, and the buttstock. These wood composite sections are important to the function of the chassis.
The composite panels that run the length of the forend are about .375-inch thick and are contoured at the top. In addition to the contouring, there are angled grooves cut into the tops of these panels that function as thumb rests. The brilliance of using these wood composite panels is the dampening effect they offer. This is the only aluminum chassis I’ve found that shoots like a heavy-fill fiberglass stock or Foundation brand stocks. Absent from the Matterhorn is any of the “ping” or vibration traditionally found with most aluminum 
chassis. Smaller aluminum panels serve as attachment points for accessories. They accept any M-LOK rail. 
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The grip is the most modular I’ve seen and is made from the same wood composite. There are two grip choices, vertical and swept, and  four palm shelves that attaches to the bottom of the grip, three of which serve as support for the firing hand. There is a flush “shelf” for use with the vertical and swept grip and three options for use on the swept grip only. Those three options have shelves that protrude from .25- to .5-inch from the bottom of the grip. These choices in grip orientation and how much, if any, shelf to put under it allow the shooter to tailor how their firing hand interfaces with the grip. Considering the wide variety in hand sizes and shooter preferences, these options are welcome.

Vertical and swept grip options are available for the Matterhorn. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The wood composite on the buttstock comes in the form of decorative side panels and an adjustable comb. The adjustable comb runs the length of the buttstock and, once removed from the chassis, adjusts laterally to accommodate faces of different widths. It is also possible to angle the comb to provide full support along the entire cheek instead of just contacting in one point. Having an adjustable comb with this many options allows the shooter’s face to stay in touch with the rifle during recoil, enabling them to spot impacts. A quick-adjust lever loosens tension on the comb, allowing the shooter to move it to the desired location. Once there, tighten the lever and use the thumbwheel to move an internal hard stop up under the comb, locking it in place. This allows the shooter to remove the comb for cleaning then drop it back into the exact same place. 
The buttpad also adjusts vertically by loosening a lever. There is also a ball and detent system that click the buttpad to selected heights and prevents it from slipping when tightened. Length of pull adjustments are easy on the Matterhorn because the buttpad separates from spacers. Add or remove spacers to adjust length of pull in .25-inch increments. 
Steel weights can be affixed to either side of the forend for optimal balance. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Inside the chassis where the barreled action sits also received some thoughtful design. There are deep grooves cut around each action screw and two troughs on the edges where the recoil lug seats. These open spaces allow bedding compound a place to go when a barreled action is skim-bedded to the chassis.

The Matterhorn has an adjustable magazine release that allows this chassis to reliably feed the many varieties of AICS magazines as well as the double-stack AW magazines. Anyone wanting buttery-smooth feeding from their chassis will need to tailor 
magazine height to the action/chassis combination. The Matterhorn allows you to quickly and easily do it. 
Brass weights installed in the barrel channel can boost the weight of this chassis from 6.75 pounds to 12.25 pounds when combined with the steel weights on the forend. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Finally, there is an elegant weight system available for the Matterhorn. Brass weights can be installed in the barrel channel and inside the buttstock. Steel weights can attach to the forend’s exterior. Adding all the weights takes the Matterhorn from 6.75 pounds to 12.25 pounds. The ability to place weights just about anywhere along the chassis enables the shooter to perfectly balance the rifle. 
The stock features wood panels and adjustability in the comb, buttpad, and length of pull. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Yes, the Matterhorn is an expensive chassis, but it offers features not found anywhere else. The use of wood composite deadens vibration, the weights offer an unprecedented degree of balance customization, and it has the classic lines of a stock. Throw in the adjustable magazine catch, the ability to skim bed, and all the adjustment possibilities of the buttstock, and the Matterhorn sets a new standard for premium.
