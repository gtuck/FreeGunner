The Great Joe Balickie: Perfecting the Classic Mauser Model 98

A master stockmaker transforms the legendary war action into a pinnacle of American custom gunmaking.

 Some of the finest custom rifles ever built employ the legendary Mauser 98 action. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)

June 30, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

Long before America designed a viable bolt action, the Mauser Model 98 became the world’s choice for custom rifles. Prestigious British companies like Holland & Holland and Rigby chose Mauser actions on which to build their legendary dangerous-game guns and stalking rifles. German designer Otto Bock engineered his 9.3x62 cartridge in 1905 specifically for African hunting rifles built on the Mauser action. Even here in America the Mauser exerted influence—so much so that when we did launch a good bolt action, we paid Mauser royalties on certain elements of that 1903 Springfield rifle.
As a result, a Mauser 98 action has been a respected choice for a custom rifle for more than a century now. These actions possess a certain something—historic panache combined with German engineering plus Victorian-era elegance.
The rifle profiled here is no exception. It was built by legendary gunsmith Joe Balickie using a Mauser 98 action. It has a double-taper featherweight-type barrel chambered in 7x57mm Mauser. The stock is a spectacular stick of wood, most likely Claro walnut.
I became enamored with this Balickie 
7x57mm Mauser
 because of two elements: the stockwork is superb, and the metalwork is exemplary. Plus, it’s chambered in one of the great classic cartridges.

Mechanicals
At its core the Mauser 98 is a turnbolt action with dual, opposing locking lugs on the front of the bolt. It’s famous for its full-length, massive nonrotating claw-type extractor, which provides reliable feeding and extraction even in tropical climates where fired cases tend to stick in the chamber. The ejector is a fixed blade-type, also proven and popular.
Feeding is via an internal magazine that contains five rounds in a staggered column. When you run the bolt forward it pushes the top-most cartridge in the magazine toward the chamber. As the cartridge pops up out of the magazine its rim slides under the extractor, so it’s captured and controlled as it journeys forward into the chamber. This helps prevent human-induced double-feeds and short-stroke malfunctions.
Original Mauser cocking pieces are heavy and feature a large wing-type safety lever that rotates up and across the top rear of the action. Most sporting rifles—like this one—have an aftermarket bolt shroud and safety that’s compatible with scopes. Bolt handles also must be modified to be compatible with scopes.

Military Mauser actions have robust but coarse bottom metal, so most fine custom rifles built using such actions are fitted with aftermarket bottom metal. It’s hard to say what company produced the metal on this rifle, but it’s superbly made in the Oberndorf style.
When built, the action on this Balickie rifle was beautifully profiled and polished clean of all military markings. Early iterations of Talley-type scope bases were fitted to the action, and although they’re a bit taller than I like (which prevents a low-mounted scope) they function perfectly.
Provenance
I first became enamored with this rifle when it showed up on Lane Arms’s list of fine firearms for sale. Neal Lane is a friend, and his superb photography made me want the sleek, classic custom Mauser with greed that bordered on lust. We got on the phone and talked through its specifications. It was likely made for a woman because the length of pull is just 12.75 inches. I figured I could live with that. After selling a few other firearms to fund the purchase and doing a little horse-trading with Lane, the beautiful custom Mauser was mine.
The metalwork on the rifle is outstanding. The stockwork is purely spectacular and showcases the skill Balickie was renowned for. Presumably, the rifle was crafted sometime between 1950 and 1990, when Mr. Balickie was most actively building rifles.
A fine engraved steel buttplate is perfectly fitted, and it features a checkered oblong trapdoor that conceals a compartment inside the buttstock. The elegant cheekrest is accented with a shadowline. Supergrade-type sling-swivel studs are inletted into raised beds in the toe and the fore-end. Wood-to-metal fit is stellar around the bottom metal, action, barrel, and grip cap. Very fine checkering is present on the fore-end and grip.

Rangetime
One never knows whether a vintage rifle will shoot well, but with one of this quality that’s obviously well-built and is fitted with what was surely a custom barrel, accuracy is likely. And this rifle has it—but finding it was a bit of a merry-go-round.
After installing a lovely Swarovski 3-9X 36mm scope in steel Talley rings, I bore-sighted the rig. Bore-sighting was simple enough, but that’s where the easy part ended. Basic 7x57mm ammo loaded with 140-grain flat-base bullets by Federal and Remington produced 2.5- to 4.0-inch groups at 100 yards. Premium ammo by Federal and Nosler did the same.
I switched to handloads, neck-sizing cases and trying Nosler 160-grain AccuBonds, Speer 175-grain Grand Slams, Swift 150-grain Scirocco IIs, and Berger 168-grain VLD Hunting bullets. Of them all, the 150-grain Scirocco IIs showed the most promise, but even they averaged around 2.5 inches. That’s good enough for whitetails in big woods and African plains game in bush country, but not confidence-inspiring for hunting the wide-open Rocky Mountain West where I live.
Based on the quality of the rifle, I just knew something was amiss. The only gunsmiths I was willing to trust to work on the rifle were Joe and Brett Smithson, of JP Smithson Gunmakers. Brett checked the bedding, made the scope rings lock to the bases more securely, and lapped the rings before remounting the scope. Joe loaded up a handful of 
Hornady
 162-grain A-Max bullets over a charge of H4350 powder and shot a series of groups. Wouldn’t you know it, they all averaged less than one MOA.
Thankfully, I have a few boxes of those old A-Max bullets. They’re not hunting-designated bullets, but when fired at mild 7x57 Mauser velocities, they’re very effective on deer-size game. So that’s that sorted.
Here’s the cool thing about 
vintage custom rifles made on Mauser 98 actions
: They’re a lot more affordable than, say, ordering a new premium custom rifle built of fine walnut and blued steel from Rigby or Park West Arms or the like. I’m not sure what that says about how such rifles hold their value, but the fact is, it would cost me double or triple what Lane was asking for this rifle to commission a new-made rifle of similar quality.
If you long for old-world custom quality but have a pickup-truck budget, shop for high-quality, hand-made sporting rifles built on the great Model 98 Mauser action. You just might luck into a really special rifle.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
