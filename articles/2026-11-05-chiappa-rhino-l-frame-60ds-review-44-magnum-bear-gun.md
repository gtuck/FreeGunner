Chiappa Rhino L Frame 60DS Review: .44 Magnum Bear Gun

Does the revolutionary bottom-bore design tame the recoil of a .44 Magnum wilderness defense revolver?

 A Rhino? In Alaska? This is no Monty Python skit… It’s the Chiappa L-Frame .44 Magnum in bear country! (Photo by Rikk Rambo)

November 05, 2026

By Rikk Rambo 

It was February 25th, 1836, and legendary firearms designer Sam Colt was about to have one hell of a good day, as were handgunners the world over. On that banner morning, a “Buck Rogers in the 21st Century” reminiscent event was brewing. Soldiers, law enforcement personnel, and civilian shooters alike were about to wake up to a brave and innovative new world where muzzle-loading handguns with marginal-at-best accuracy and infinitesimally slow-to-reload characteristics were about to become relics of the past. Lurking just around the corner, thanks to Mr. Colt, were combat effective multi-shot revolvers preparing to usurp their (mostly) single-shot predecessors as the most popular self-defense handguns of their time.
Thanks to Colonel Colt (an honorary title), Valentine’s Day was a nothing-burger in CY (Calendar Year) 1863, because just a week and change after that sappy, overrated Hallmark holiday, Sammy C. was granted the U.S. patent for his revolver design. For the next 173 years, the primary blueprint and architecture of his pièce de résistance ruled the wheelgun world and went virtually unchallenged. All of that would change, however, when a revolutionary revolver design concept came to life and charged into SHOT Show 2010 like…well… a RHINO!
The Rhino shows off its flanks! A clinical view of the Rhino’s silhouette and lines. The recoil-taming Hogue grips are a great choice. (Photos by Rikk Rambo)
The Chiappa Rhino strongly resembles any other medium/large frame revolver, at least until your gaze moves forward from the grips to its Kaiju/Godzilla proportioned ventilated rib and unorthodox barrel, which is positioned below the revolver’s centerline and aligns with the bottom chamber of the cylinder instead of the top (as is the norm for traditional revolvers). The Rhino’s Escher-esque/modern art reminiscent “down under” barrel placement isn’t just performance art or a whimsical, fad-driven affectation. This novel design profile is based on engineering and physics and works to mitigate upwards recoil (muzzle rise) and direct it backwards along the centerline of the pistol. The redirection of recoil results in faster follow-up shots and a modicum of relief for those who are recoil sensitive.
Left to Right: The Rhino’s low-axis barrel crushes muzzle-rise and even reduces felt recoil to a noticeable degree. Another force-multiplier in the Rhino’s battle against recoil and muzzle-flip: Its Hogue rubberized grips. (Photo by Rikk Rambo)
The Problem with Magnums
The Rhino was designed to mitigate muzzle-flip and recoil, which is paramount in effectively engaging a target with multiple, effective hits in a limited amount of time. While healthy brown bears often have the appearance of being plump and roly-poly, they can reach top speeds of 35-plus miles per hour when properly motivated. While one well-placed round directly into the center of bear’s head (in the area between the eyes and traveling down to its nostrils) may provide instant gratification and stop a charge, wouldn’t you rather have a couple of additional on-target impacts as insurance? This is where the Rhino struts its stuff.

I spent an entire day at the range with the new L-Frame Rhino and launched approximately three-hundred full-house factory loads and “bunker-buster” handloads down range. I don’t have any type of contraption to measure the recoil, but my best guess is that the barrel configuration of the Rhino reduced its muzzle rise by approximately half of that experienced in revolvers with traditional configurations. The felt recoil, as a result, also seemed greatly diminished, and this was the universal opinion of three other experienced handgunners who gave the Rhino a test drive.
The Rhino’s cocking/de-cocking procedures and configuration also veer off the traditional revolver path…a bit. When the Rhino is cocked in single action, the hammer falls back into what appears to be an un-cocked and safe position. The reason for this is simple: The Rhino uses an internal hammer and what the shooter is actually manipulating is a cocking lever that returns forward once you’ve “singled-actioned” internal hammer it into firing position. You’ll know you are still hot and ready to fire, however, because a bright red “cocked, locked, and ready to rock” warning rod will extend upward next to the hammer. De-cocking is similar to a traditional revolver…just put your thumb on the hammer, pull it back a skosh, slowly press the trigger, and allow the hammer to lower.
The Rhino is also surprisingly light, weighing in at 2.90 pounds. For comparison, here’s a shortlist of conventional, current production double-action .44 magnum revolvers by weight (ascending):

The accuracy of the Rhino is also nothing to be sneezed at, but we’ll talk about that shortly. In the end analysis, the revolver’s above attributes makes for some effective “bear medicine,” whether your aggressor is of the 
Ursus Americanus (black bear) or Ursus arctos horribilis (brown bear)
 variety.
Rhino on the Range!
Since the Rhino would also make a great white tail (or similar class game) hunting handgun, I added a Trijicon SRO RDS to its bountiful top rail for range-day.
If “variety is the spice of life,” we satisfied that proverb to a “T” with our ammunition selection. For those considering the Rhino as primarily a backwoods/bear defense handgun, we picked up a few boxes of “The Bad News [for] Bears” 300-grain “Hammerhead” WFNGC Hard Cast with a muzzle velocity of 1,400 fps and muzzle energy of 1,306 ft lbs. For those who would prefer to go with a lighter projectile but still have a cartridge capable of putting the kibosh on a marauding bruin, we threw the 200-grain Hornady Custom XTP, and 160-grain Black Hills Ammunition “HoneyBadger” into the mix.
Left to Right: Black Hills Ammunition 160-grain HoneyBadger rounds win the day (and both accuracy and least recoil competition) with a .840-inch group from the 25-yard line. Hornady’s Custom 200-grain XTPs showcases both its paper AND bruin efficacy! Grizzly Cartridge Company’s 300-grain hard casts Hammerhead’s .999-inch group: All of the “thump” without the wrist-numbing recoil! (Photo by Rikk Rambo)
The “winner, winner, chicken dinner” for both speed, accuracy, and least felt recoil was the 160-Grain Black Hills Ammunition HoneyBadger. Just like its tenacious, ornery, real-world namesake, its effectiveness was not measured by its size or weight. It’s average group size at 25 yards (three five-shot groups) was .922-inches and its best group size was an impressive .840-inches.
The Rhino’s close second “pet load” was, surprisingly, the T-72 tank killer of the tested cartridges: The 300-Grain Grizzly Cartridge Company “Hammerhead” WFNGC hard cast, with a 1.30-inch group average and a .999-inch “bestie group!” If “Cocaine Bear” (like the one from that Oscar-worthy movie) ever became a reality and you had to get down to some serious bear defense measures, THIS would be your go-to cartridge!

Hornady’s 200-grain Custom XTP ammunition finished a close third to the Grizzly ammunition, with a 1.35-inch group average and a 1.13-inch “best in show.” I can tell you with complete authority that this round is an absolute machine for handgun hunting black bear. Several years ago, while solo-hunting on the Kenai Peninsula, I harvested a massive, cub-murdering (seriously, it was chasing down a sow with cubs when I took it out of the equation) black bear boar with a heart-shot. The round was so effective, the boar hit the ground immediately and was dead before the round’s thunderous echoes faded away in the surrounding forest.
Take your Rhino for a Hike? You Betcha!
Chiappa’s new L-Frame .44 Magnum surprised me. In the spirit of transparency, I readily admit I had to fight “traditional revolver-centric biases” as it relates to revolvers I’ve enthusiastically hunted with or carried for decades. These biases begin to fade away almost immediately after unboxing the Rhino in relation to its ergonomics, and later after launching several hundred rounds down-range and appreciating its reduced muzzle-rise and “get the job done” accuracy. At the conclusion of testing and evaluation, the Rhino proved that even an upper middle-aged traditional revolver guy can learn new tricks, like accepting and actually having feelings akin to affection for a wheelgun with an “upside barrel!”
There were several Rhino attributes I didn’t fall in love with, but the list is short and Chiappa is only responsible for first two of my “three shortcomings list.”
Left: The Chiappa’s stout, ergonomically curved trigger: phenomenal in single-action mode, acceptable in double-action. Right: One thing I’d change: The Rhino’s abbreviated ejector rod allows for only a partial expulsion of its spent rounds—not a good thing when the gun is “gummed up” or dirty. (Photo by Rikk Rambo)
The first of these was the trigger. The trigger’s “single-action” press is absolutely superb at approximately 2.7 pounds. There’s no creep and absolutely no “burrs” or distractions, just an intuitive, predictable, and clean break at approximately 2.9 pounds (model tested). The double-action trigger press (approximately nine pounds on the test model), while not a dealbreaker, was not as smooth or predictable as the vast number of traditional magnum-caliber revolvers I’ve carried or evaluated over the years.
The second trait that didn’t fit in the “attaboy” list was the Rhino’s abbreviated ejector rod. While I’ll be the first person in to tell you that if you haven’t successfully accomplished a “conflict resolution” session with a brown bear in six shots, you probably won’t get a chance to load six more for a rematch. However, a rapid reload is still a good thing, and Rhino’s short ejector rod could put a hitch in your giddyup…especially if you are under duress or the weapon is dirty and rounds are a bit “slushy” during ejection.
My final gripe before I quit with the “Yes, my name is Karen and I want to talk to Chiappa’s Manager” monologue involves something that Chiappa may not have control over, a lack of commercially available holsters for this unique handgun. Never fear, however, because my contact at Falco Holsters gave me a lifeline that I’m going to pass in the form of the exact web address for their Rhino holster lineup, to anyone on the prowl for a sturdy, handsome, and functional holster for their L-Frame Rhino: FalcoHolsters.com/gun-holsters-chiappa-firearms-rhino-60-ds-6rd (great holsters on that website).
Now for the “attaboys!” As previously mentioned, the most attractive attribute of the Rhino is its unconventional barrel and recoil-destroying rubberized Hogue grips. This combination results in a tamer muzzle-rise and overall felt recoil than experienced in traditional models of that caliber. I also applaud the rail system that runs from the 
Rhino’s top strap to the terminus of its barrel allows the shooter to mount whatever optic they see fit, and if you aren’t a fan of dot sights, it ships from the factory with adjustable fiber-optic iron sights which are prominent and define “high visibility.” Circling back to attachable accessories, the underbarrel rail provides the shooter with a bountiful location to mount their weapon light of choice.
Would I carry this unique but supremely functional hand-cannon into brown bear country? Unequivocally, yes. When it comes to a fully capable next generation .44 magnum revolver, Chiappa nailed it with the L-Frame .44 Magnum Rhino.

Oh, and on a lighter note, for those of you just looking to purchase a “leisure-time” .44 magnum wheelgun with which you can obliterate tin cans, watermelons, and bowling pins, the Rhino has something to offer you as well. In the event you and your friends found yourselves in an “emergency situation” standing around the grill out by the lake with your beverage of choice in your mitts but no bottle opener within several acres, the Rhino has your back with that big, beautiful, and utilitarian ventilated rib!
