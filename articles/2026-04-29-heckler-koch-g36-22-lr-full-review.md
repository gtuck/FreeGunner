Heckler & Koch G36 .22 LR: Full Review

An original G36 may be impossible to own, but the .22 LR-chambered, semiauto HK G36 is a lot of fun, too.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

April 29, 2026

By Tom Beckstrand

I was once on assignment to train with the German army for several weeks as a West Point cadet, and I had the opportunity to use some of their issued equipment. Even back then, I fancied myself a bit of a rifleman, so I looked forward to shooting their service rifle. I fully expected to qualify expert because I easily met the U.S. military standard. Sadly, the only test score they counted was the first time I shot the rifle! There was no test familiarization. I laid down behind the rifle and started shooting after a brief explanation about how to operate it and get a quick zero. I scored just less than the German “expert” standard, which still bothers me today. Twenty more minutes of preparation and I would have had it. I’ll stay salty about that until I die. It’s fine. 
The Polymer receiver of the G36 in .22 LR stays true to the composition of the original G36 series. Unlike the original, though, the .22 LR model is only capable of safe and semiautomatic fire denoted by red “FIRE” and white “SAFE” pictograms. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
I still remember the rifle well. It was a well-­loved 
G3 chambered in 7.62x51mm (7.62 NATO)
, and I was shocked at how much harder it was to shoot than the M16 I qualified with previously. Prior to my visit, it seems the Bundeswehr (German army) had grown tired of the G3 and wanted to move to a rifle that was chambered in the same cartridge that all of its NATO partners fired, the 5.56x45mm (5.56 NATO). Tests for a new rifle started in 1993. Little did I know that the same summer I was visiting, the German army was well into its testing and adoption of the rifle that replaced the G3 — the G36. Heckler & Koch (HK) submitted its contender, called the “HK50,” and it was the star of those trials. Once adopted, the HK50 became known as the “G36.”  The tests concluded and the formal announcement was made on May 8, 1995. Fielding of the G36 started on December 3, 1997, and the G36 stayed in service until December 2022 — almost 25 years.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
G-­What?
The G36 taking the place of the G3 was not a decision the German army took lightly. The G3 story started during World War II, after the arrival of the Sturmgewehr (StG) 44.  The StG 44 proved that stamped-­steel manufacturing could turn out reliable military small arms inexpensively, and in large quantities. The G3 continued in various stages of design from the mid-­1940s through the late 1950s. By 1960, the design was final, and the rifle was in production at HK factory in Oberndorf, Germany. 
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The G3 has all the hallmarks of a cutting-­edge military rifle design of the time. It had a stamped-­steel receiver, a long sight radius to take advantage of the iron sights, and 20-­round detachable box magazines. As I learned during my qualification session with the G3, it is a roller-­delayed blowback rifle that is simple and durable. It also has more recoil than most troops will ever learn to manage effectively. It worked great with iron sights, but there were painfully few ways to attach an optic to the stamped-­steel receiver that could hold zero or place the optic anywhere usable.

With the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989, and the collapse of communism, Germany faced new and evolving threats. By ’92, Germany recognized that it needed to operate alongside NATO allies such as Belgium, France and England. That meant it needed service rifles that fired the same cartridge. Germany’s focus turned to creating a light and reliable low-­cost carbine that could be issued to conventional troops. Money was tight in the early ’90s because the former East Germany needed an infrastructure overhaul during reunification. Remodeling a house is expensive; remodeling an entire country requires a few more zeros.
The desire to move to the 5.56 NATO cartridge was due partially to the need to share supply lines with allies, but also from the desire to reduce recoil and enable soldiers to carry more rounds. What the U.S. military started with the adoption of the M16, the Western European Defense Union accepted in 1976; it moved to the 5.56x45mm as its primary cartridge, and it took Germany another 16 years to catch up.
The G36 .22 LR is disassembled in the same manner as the legend. Remove three push-pins to separate the main components. Maintenance is straight-forward. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
When the German military made the decision to issue a 5.56mm service rifle, it also decided that it didn’t want to spend any money on development; money was in short supply. German military leaders looked around and decided to test two models that were available: the Steyr AUG and the HK50.

HK spent a couple of decades developing the G11 caseless rifle, which utilized polymer furniture in wide panels and an optical sight integrated into the carrying handle. The HK50 has large pieces of polymer furniture and an integrated optical sight like the G11, but it utilized a piston-­operated impingement system and a rotating bolt like the bolt found in an M16. The HK50 started off in 1990 as “Project 50,” not because there were 49 other projects before it, but because it started on an engineer’s 50th birthday.
Enter The G36
Accepted in 1997, the issued G36 had a polymer receiver, pistol grip, forend, carry handle and folding buttstock. The use of polymer kept the rifle’s weight to a minimum and costs low. Tipping the scales at just more than 6 pounds, the G36 was more than 3 pounds lighter than the G3 it replaced.
Using a polymer receiver for the G36 might seem optimistic to riflemen acquainted with the concerns of polymer receivers made for the AR-­15, but unlike the AR-­15 HK designed the G36 around a polymer receiver concept. It worked reliably — mostly. 
The stock of the G36 .22 LR is the same foldable unit used on the HK G36 service rifle. The method of operation is also identical. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The hammer-­forged barrel and barrel extension contained all the pressure from firing once the bolt locked into place, so the receiver simply ensured the barrel, magazine, trigger and buttstock stayed in the appropriate places. HK located the recoil spring around the bolt body’s operating rod, so there is no spring sitting behind the bolt. It allowed HK to put a folding stock on the G36, making it both compact and lightweight.
The G36 met the immediate needs of the German army, but ran into trouble when, in 2015, the operational tempo during the War on Terror revealed design flaws. When used for sustained fire, for example, the G36 became hot enough that accuracy degraded substantially. Polymer is light and inexpensive, but it handled thermal stress poorly and softened when hot. Military testers found that groups opened up so much that hits at 200 yards became questionable. The engineers’ response included instructions to not shoot so much so fast. Some of the guys on the ground didn’t have a choice. The German military decided to start looking for a replacement rifle. It came in the form of the HK 416 variant named the G95A1. It received approval for fielding in December 2022.

The G36 Rimfire
The G36 in .22 LR
 is a faithful replica of the original G36. It is an HK/Umarex collaboration. It has the same forend, sight rail and buttstock as the issued G36, and the manual of arms is remarkably similar. Also — like the original — it fieldstrips by removing three pins, which allows for the removal of the forend, trigger group, pistol grip and stock. Since the stock is the same as on the issued G36, the pins can be stored in holes in the buttstock so they’re not lost in the field. The polymer receiver and bolt differ significantly in operation when compared to the original G36, but they share a similar appearance.
The G36 .22 LR is ideal for anyone interested in the original G36 or who wants a rimfire rifle that shoots like a military-­issued centerfire rifle. The forend is long enough that the G36 .22 LR can be used for positional shooting, even if I can’t recommend sling use. The polymer receiver didn’t handle the stress of a tight sling well, and the point-­of-­impact shifts. However, no matter how fast I shot the G36 .22 LR, heat-­induced gremlins didn’t manifest. It’s likely that the polymer receiver is ideal for rimfire use, unlike centerfire cartridges that produce more heat and pressure. 
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The length of pull on the G36 .22 LR measured 133/4 inches. It’s a bit long for anyone other than an adult. However, it tipped the scales at 51/4 pounds, so it’s light enough for a full day at the range.
The G36 .22 LR is a well-­made rimfire rifle that has an ambidextrous safety and charging handle. Like most semiautomatic rimfire rifles, it is blowback-­operated and feeds from an AR-­15-­sized 20-­round magazine. Ten-­ and 30-­round magazines are also available. There is a bolt hold-­open after the last round is fired, so there’s also no guessing when the rifle runs dry.
The Bolt in the G36 .22 LR has the same profile as the centerfire version, but it functions as a blowback-operated assembly. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The HK G36 .22 LR is the latest in a growing line of rimfire HK replicas. While original HK rifles such as the G36 were never commercially available in the U.S., and the MP5 series is prohibitively expensive, we now have rimfire clones that provide the shooter with a low-recoil experience and most of what these legends had to offer.
