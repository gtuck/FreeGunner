Australian Bolt-Action Hunting Rifle Review: The Lithgow Arms CrossOver

Australian brand Lithgow Arms has been manufacturing small arms since 1912. The CrossOver centerfire is a serious contender for the American market.

 Australian brand Lithgow Arms is now offering its ultra-accurate CrossOver centerfire to American shooters. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)

February 01, 2018

By Brad Fitzpatrick

Last January, during the industry’s annual trade show, I made my way to the booth of Legacy Sports International and caught up with Matt Hornback of the importer’s marketing team. He told me what was new with the brands, much of the news revolving around Howa’s new chassis and 
Kuiu
 rifle models, but he also mentioned that the company was importing a new line of rifles from Australia: Lithgow. The name sounded familiar. Hornback told me Legacy was importing the company’s centerfires and rimfires, then handed me a CrossOver LA102. It looked nice enough, and Hornback went on to tell me about the small groups they’d been getting out of the rifles.
It sounded impressive, but in a world of $500 minute-of-angle rifles from brands whose familiar logos appear on pickup truck windows, cell phone cases and tattoos, the only hope for a rather expensive, relatively unknown import is accuracy that simply can’t be ignored.
Bug-Hole Precision
Fast-forward two months. On a bitterly cold March afternoon, I was one of the few shooters at the 100-yard range at my local club. I looked through the Nikon scope, having just sent two 58-grain Winchester Varmint-X bullets downrange through the .243 Win.-chambered Lithgow. I could clearly see the first hole in the paper but not the second one.
This is either very good or very bad, I thought. I cycled the action, swung the large paddle safety back into the Safe position and recorded the velocity from my chronograph—more than 3,600 fps. I got back behind the scope, settled the sight picture, controlled my breathing and pressed the trigger. When the rifle came to rest, the tiny, single hole in the paper has bulged just a bit. The Lithgow has just printed a one-hole group with factory ammo.

Australian Firearm
Before I dive into the rest of the review, some backstory is in order. In the late 1800s Australians were concerned about the country’s vulnerability due to its isolation from Great Britain. Increasing fears of a Russian invasion prompted Australia’s government to consider developing a stronger military force. The need for a domestic small-arms manufacturing facility was made top priority after Australian soldiers returning from fighting in the Boer War complained that relying on old and sometimes outdated British weapons was a hindrance in battle.
The site the Australian government chose for its new small-arms factory was in the small town of Lithgow, which sat at the western foothills of the Blue Mountains. Despite its relative isolation, Lithgow was serviced by rail and road, and in 1912 a 90,000-square-foot small-arms manufacturing facility began producing weapons there. Pratt & Whitney in Hartford, Connecticut, provided the machines, and between 1913 and 1918 more than 100,000 short-magazine Lee-Enfield rifles left the Lithgow factory for the fields of war.
Lithgow continued to produce firearms used in military conflicts until the Australian government sold the small-arms factory and ceased overseeing firearms production there. The new Lithgow brand, now privately owned, still makes precision rifles, and the LA102 CrossOver is just one example.

Bolt-Action Features
Available in .223, .243 and .308, the bolt-action features a three-lug bolt that allows for a 60-degree lift. The bolt body is the same diameter as the lugs, and it runs through the high-tensile steel action smoothly with no rattle or play. The extractor is mounted on one of the recoil lugs, and the ejector is a plunger.
There’s a heavy-duty steel recoil lug, and the cold-hammer-forged barrel is made from proprietary military-grade steel and is machined on the same equipment that produces barrels for the Australia Defense Force’s Austeyr rifles. The 22-inch medium varmint-weight barrel has an M14x1 right-hand threaded muzzle with cap. It features a 1:10 twist in .243 (1:9 for the .223, 1:11 in .308). All the metalwork has a matte silver Cerakote finish.
The trigger in this rifle is Lithgow’s three-lever-style module that is adjustable for trigger weight, sear engagement and overtravel. It’s an easy trigger to adjust; simply remove two 5mm hex-head screws from the underside of the trigger assembly and the trigger guard, stock and action come apart easily.
The Lithgow employs a bedding block integral with the stock that fits into a cutout in the barrel. It proved to be an accurate setup. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
All the adjustments on the trigger are easy to find and see. The nut at the front of the trigger assembly adjusts trigger weight, the one at the rear handles sear engagement, and there’s an overtravel adjustment as well.
A word of warning, though: The back screw seats in a lug that is dovetailed into the rear underside of the tubular receiver, and it’s easy to lose when disassembling the rifle. I spent about five minutes trying to figure out what metallic object had pinged across the floor when I took the rifle apart.

A latch located at the rear of the bolt shroud makes it simple to disassemble the bolt for cleaning and maintenance, and the bolt knob is oversized and made from hard black plastic with vertical cutouts. The magazine well is narrow. Laura Evans, who handles media relations for Legacy, said this helps improve rigidity for better accuracy.
The rifle’s laminate stock is optimized for sandbag/prone use with a flat toe and sharply angled pistol grip. Up front there are twin swivel studs for mounting both a bipod and a sling. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Stock
The CrossOver is available with either a laminate or a synthetic stock, and I tested the laminate version. It features a straight comb; wide, rounded fore-end; and a cutout on the underside of the rear portion of the stock for resting on sandbags.
There are three sling studs, and the recoil pad is thick and well fitted, more than capable of absorbing what little recoil this eight-pound .243 generated. I’m a fan of the grip angle, which is rather steep and has a palm swell that fills the hand and ensures a stable, comfortable hold on the rifle.
The fit and finish are excellent, especially between the underside of the stock and the large black plastic trigger guard, which has an opening that is large enough to allow plenty of room when shooting with gloved hands. A steel plate inside the stock mates with a cutout in the underside of the action to stabilize the gun during recoil, and the wide channel in the stock allows the barrel to float freely.
Magazine
Each CrossOver rifle is supplied with a plastic straight-feed magazine that holds three rounds in .243 and .308 and four in .223. The magazine fits securely into the mag well and locks in place with an audible click. The release is located just in front of the mag well, and it’s large enough to locate easily yet is recessed so it won’t accidentally release when you are shuffling around for a better shooting position in the field. There’s a rocker-type bolt release lever on the left rear portion of the receiver.
Safety
The Lithgow doesn’t look like your average hunting rifle, and perhaps the biggest difference between this gun and traditional American rifles is the safety. It’s a large wing located on the rear of the bolt shroud and operates essentially like traditional wing-type safeties—with one major difference.
On rifles like the 
Winchester
 Model 70, the safety’s rear Safe position locks the bolt and the intermediate position allows you to load and unload the rifle on Safe. With the Lithgow, these two safety positions are reversed: Full rearward allows the bolt to be operated in Safe mode while the middle position locks the bolt.
It’s very different, but once you have the hang of it, the pattern makes sense. The wing itself is large and easy to find, but its position and weight required me to remove my hand from the pistol grip to manipulate it, especially when trying to do so silently.

Range Time
I topped the rifle with Nikon’s Monarch 3 4-16x42mm scope, and after bore sighting I headed to the range where the CrossOver produced excellent groups with every load tested. In fact, there was at least one sub-inch group with all three loads, and all the groups I shot with the Winchester Varmint X ammo went sub-m.o.a.
The trigger was clean and crisp and came from the factory at 3.5 pounds with no creep and minimal overtravel. The cutout in the rear of the stock helped locked the rifle into the rear sandbag for a steady sight picture. The action was, as previously mentioned, quite smooth, and there were no issues with feeding or extraction.
The overall results indicated what Hornback had originally told me was true. The Lithgow produces some outstanding groups, even with factory loads. If you are a handloader and wanted to spend some time developing a recipe that the Lithgow liked, there’s little doubt it would rival a precision bench rifle. The threaded muzzle means you could shoot it suppressed as well.
Aussie Tough
At eight pounds the CrossOver might be a bit heavy for long hikes into steep country, but its balance is excellent with a midpoint just ahead of the mag well. With two tough-as-nails stock options and a full Cerakote finish, there’s no question this gun would stand up to the absolute worst elements you could encounter.
Time will tell whether shooters are willing to invest almost $1,400 in a rifle that doesn’t have the brand recognition of some of the more familiar domestic guns, but the Lithgow has the accuracy potential to turn a lot of heads.

If you own one of these Aussie rifles and travel to the range, you’ll invariably get questions about the gun you are carrying. You might even field some questions as to why you chose an Australian import. The simplest response to that inquiry, though, is to hang a target, load a magazine and say, “Watch this, mates.”
