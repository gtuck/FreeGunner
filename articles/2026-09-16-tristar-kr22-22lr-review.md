TriStar KR.22 .22LR Review

TriStar's first rifle offering is an affordable .22 that gets the job done. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

September 16, 2026

By Guns & Ammo Staff

TriStar is a purveyor of affordable shotguns, primarily imported from Turkey to its Kansas City headquarters, but it also imports many handguns including Model 1911s. 
The KR.22 represents the first step
 into the rifle segment for TriStar — and they’ve got a good one. It is manufactured by 
Kral Arms in Turkey.
Unlike other rimfire semiautomatic rifles, the KR.22 is standard with a lightweight stock with an adjustable comb. A screw in the side of the stock must be adjusted with one of the provided Allen wrenches to loosen and secure it in place. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Keeping It Simple
As a direct-­blowback .22 LR rifle, the KR.22 is a safe bet for TriStar. The action is straightforward, ammo is plentiful and inexpensive, and the chambering helps contribute to its affordable retail price between $289 and $309. Rimfire rifles also tend to be fun to shoot, regardless of price point, and they don’t require much commitment to own. The KR.22 is at home at the range plinking cans or taking small game afield. However, the design of the semiauto has some quality-­of-­life features that elevate it.
The trigger assembly was tested at 4 pounds, 7 ounces. The polymer trigger housing contains a pinned hammer, trigger, safety lever and crossbolt safety, as well as the triggerguard, magazine well, and magazine catch and lever assemblies. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The profile of the KR.22 is sleek with a synthetic stock and molded receiver that blends into one another. The rifle sent to Guns & Ammo for testing was the FDE variant. The handguard, receiver and adjustable cheekpad all colored opposite the black nitride barrel and stock. Olive Drab Green (ODG)/black and full-black synthetic versions are also available. 
With the magazine removed, two screws allow the barreled upper receiver and trigger assemblies to separate from the stock system. A Vortex SPARC AR 2 MOA red dot was used throughout Guns & Ammo’s evaluation. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Sling mounting points are at the forend of the stock and beneath the stock’s toe.

The overall weight of the rifle measured 4 pounds, 13 ounces. It feels light and balanced at the shoulder.
The blowback action is slightly recessed into the receiver, and the charging handle can be removed and threaded for left-hand use. The KR.22 doesn’t have a bolt hold, so users will want to have a chamber flag. Two flush-­fit rotary magazines come with the KR.22, but this rimfire accepts all first- and third-­party Ruger 10/22 mags also.  
The upper receiver housing is a molded polymer affair, containing two front and rear attachment points and the bolt assembly. A bumpstop at the rear safeguards the receiver from the bolt. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A push-­button crossbolt safety rests just above the triggerguard, while a red trigger blade safety must be completely depressed before firing.

The barrel measures 18.6 inches and is threaded 1⁄2x28. Molded as part of the polymer receiver, the optic rail incorporates square open sights that pair with the red fiber-­optic front sight. The height of the front sight can be adjusted to co-­witness with some optics or clear the muzzle device’s height.
Sharp angles and grooves dominate the profile of the KR.22. Besides black and OD green, an FDE color scheme, tactile grip, red-accent safeties, and flared grip work together to enhance the rimfire’s attractive, forward-looking appearance. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Range Testing
A 
Vortex SPARC AR
 with 2 MOA red dot was mounted to the upper receiver rail for testing. 
On the first day of testing, the a dedicated rimfire suppressor wasn’t available to evaluate the KR.22, so a 
Banish 9 suppressor
 was substituted. Running .22 rimfire through a 14-­baffle 9mm suppressor produces near-­Hollywood levels of noise despite the difference in bore size. It was a blast to send 40-grainers downrange as the rimfire ammo produced next to zero heat in the can. Shooting rapidly changed nothing. Later, a 
Banish 22 rimfire suppressor
 replaced the 9, which felt more appropriate. Noise suppression was excellent, and the weight at the end of the barrel felt was ideal. Iron sights were tall enough for use with both suppressors.
The charging handle can be unscrewed from one side of the bolt body and reattached to the opposite side. Great for lefties, a left-side charger is useful for right-handers behind the gun. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The trigger presented a slight creep, though it only had an average weight of 4 pounds, 7 ounces. The reset was good and follow-­up shots were easy. Even with a full magazine, suppressor and a red dot, the rifle weighed 5 1/2 pounds, making off-­hand shooting effortless. Grooved gripping surfaces were molded into the touch points, providing maximum grip. At the rear, the adjustable cheekrest was useful for aligning the eye behind the reticle for comfortable precision work. 
Southpaw shooters also appreciated the reversible charging handle, which didn’t need tools to change. With no way to hold the bolt open, fixing minor errors such as stuck casings became finicky, but nothing that caused frustration.

The muzzle is threaded 1/2x28, meaning that the KR.22 is suppressor ready. Even with a larger Banish 9 suppressor, the front sight was still usable. A Banish 22 (shown) is ideal, though. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Accuracy was never an issue with the KR.22. Groups were fired at 25 and then 50 yards. Ruger BX-­25 mags were also used to verify function, which helped in making five, five-­shot groups without needing to reload. Testing was done indoors, so accuracy wasn’t subject to wind. 
Unexpectedly, bulk factory ammo produced the best groups from the KR.22. Match loads crept outside of 1 ­inch on average. Velocities and accuracy remained consistent with and without a suppressor attached, too.
One of the stock magazines fed effortlessly, while another produced some reliability concerns when loaded past six rounds. There were a few light primer strikes, too, throughout the day of plinking. It’s possible that more frequent maintenance could have prevented the light-strike issue.
A fixed U-notch, two-dot sight is nestled inside the optic rail atop the receiver. The red fiber-optic front sight is height adjustable. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The KR.22 struggled with shooting more expensive match loads in terms of function, also. The rifle produced several light primer strikes on the match rounds, and sometimes match casings failed to extract, requiring a knife or tool to remove. Comparatively, bulk .22 fed almost effortlessly! We felt that it was fitting considering the price-point of the KR.22. Affordable ammo seemed to suit this plinker, reducing the serious competitive atmosphere and producing more fun. High-­velocity rounds performed the best at getting the action to cycle.
Semiautomatic rimfire rifles can be picky with ammunition types, so we always recommend trying a few different loads before determining a preference. Fiocchi’s 40-­grain Range Dynamics was the most consistent and most accurate round for us. The best overall performing load was also one of the most affordable.
Two 10-round rotary magazines are included with the KR.22, but factory and aftermarket 10/22 magazines are compatible. There were no feeding issues during G&A’s tests. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Parting Shot
The KR.22 is a plinker and a woods gun. There’s enough features to pique anyone’s interest, and the price point makes it an attractive buy. The KR.22 may not be a competition rifle, but its groups will often cluster into one cloverleaf between 25 and 50 yards. It would be a great first rifle, and most onlookers liked its appearance. The KR.22 is an excellent value from TriStar.
