Henry H1 Western Frontier .22 Mag: Best Rimfire Lever Action

Classic Cowboy Style Meets Modern Rimfire Power

 From hunting to plinking the Henry H1 Western Frontier rifle in 22 Magnum makes this a fun and versatile rimfire. (Henry Repeating Arms photo)

October 05, 2026

By Barb Melloni

The Henry H1 Western Frontier Rifle stands out amongst the sea of lightweight, utilitarian .22s that dominate much of the rimfire market. With its octagonal barrel, American walnut stock, polished blued steel, and lever-action design, it carries the unmistakable character of a traditional American lever gun. Chambering it in .22 Magnum adds another dimension, giving the Western Frontier more velocity, energy, and reach than you'd see in other offerings.
The classic checkering on the pistol grip. (Barb Melloni photo)
Design & Features
The 
H1 Western Frontier
 is part of Henry's expanded H1 lineup. The first thing to catch your eye will undoubtedly be its 20-inch octagonal blued-steel barrel. Octagonal barrels have been associated with traditional American lever guns for generations, serving as the perfect nod to the days of the Wild West. This rifle measures 38 inches in length, weighs 6.5 pounds, has a barrel length of 20 inches, and a twelve-round capacity. Six and a half pounds is substantial for a rimfire, especially compared with lightweight polymer-stocked .22s, but the Western Frontier isn't intended to compete with them.
The lever action is smooth and the trigger is crisp. (Barb Melloni photo)
While on the heavier side of the scale, it has a solid, well-proportioned feel that remains easy to handle, whether or not you're shooting from a supported position. This lever gun is outfitted with an American walnut stock, complete with checkering along the grip and forend. The buttstock is pistol-grip-style rather than the straight-grip configuration traditionally associated with many lever guns, such as the one on 
Henry's H6 Big Boy
. This pistol-grip style allows your hand to fall naturally into place as you settle into position. Rimfires are often associated with youth, and with that typically comes a shorter length of pull. The H1 Western Frontier Rifle's length of pull is 14 inches, more typical of adult-sized proportions.
A beautifully checkered forend with a semi-buckhorn adjustable rear sight. (Barb Melloni photo)
Henry equips the Western Frontier with a fully adjustable semi-buckhorn rear sight featuring a diamond insert, paired with a brass-bead front sight. For casual range use and hunting, the brass-bead front sight is easy to pick up, while the adjustable semi-buckhorn rear makes the zeroing process straightforward.

Shooters wanting more precision aren't limited to iron sights. The receiver incorporates a 3/8-inch groove for rimfire scope rings. That is an important feature on the .22 Magnum version because the cartridge offers considerably greater reach than the .22 LR. A magnified optic can make it easier to take advantage of that performance, particularly when testing ammunition from the bench or hunting small targets at longer distances.

It goes without saying that the lever action is also a large part of this rifle's appeal. A rimfire rifle of any action style should be enjoyable to shoot in volume, and anything that makes the action not run smoothly quickly becomes noticeable, especially in the case of lever guns.

Another aspect that became apparent once I started handling the rifle was how different the Western Frontier feels from many modern rimfires. There is enough weight in the rifle to give it a substantial feel without making it difficult to shoulder or maneuver. The pistol-grip stock also contributes to that handling, and when combined with the smooth lever throw, the overall ergonomics make it easy to keep your eyes on the sights when cycling the action. That may seem like a small detail, but during an extended range session, the way a rifle fits and operates can have just as much influence on the shooting experience as its specifications. Throughout our testing, the action on this Henry proved to be nothing short of smooth and effortless to operate.
The hammer in the closed position. To activate the safety cock it 1/4 of the way. (Barb Melloni photo)
Taking the Western Frontier to the Range
The .22 Magnum chambering gives the Western Frontier more versatility than its .22 LR counterpart. It is still well-suited for recreational shooting, but the added velocity and energy of the .22 WMR extend its usefulness for small-game hunting and for shooting at longer rimfire distances. Those added capabilities also make ammunition selection important, since bullet weight and construction can have a noticeable effect on how a particular load performs from the rifle.

For testing, I selected three .22 WMR loads that included a variety of bullet weights and profiles. The first round chosen was Winchester's Super-X 40gr JHP, topped with a 40-grain jacketed hollow-point bullet serving as the middleweight option during testing. It is a fairly conventional .22 Magnum load, making it a useful baseline for comparing the lighter CCI and heavier Federal ammunition. Next up was CCI’s 30gr VNT WMR, also the lightest of the three options tested. This round is topped with a 30-grain Speer VNT bullet. This projectile has a thin jacket and polymer tip, intended primarily for varmint shooting, where higher velocity and rapid bullet expansion are preferred.
Last but not least was Federal's BYOB 50gr JHP. Federal’s BYOB load was the heaviest ammunition in the test, using a 50-grain jacketed hollow-point bullet. Sold in a 250-round bulk container, perfect for an afternoon of target practice, it provided a useful contrast to the lighter 30- and 40-grain loads in both bullet weight and overall performance.
Three types of ammo were tested. (Barb Melloni photo)
The mix provided a good opportunity to see how the Western Frontier performed with both traditional hunting ammunition and lighter, higher-velocity varmint loads.

For accuracy testing, I shot the H1 Western Frontier from the benchrest position 50 yards from our target using the rifle’s stock iron sights. Looking at all three loads together tells more about the Western Frontier than simply reporting the smallest group of the day. The rifle averaged approximately 1.83 inches across the ammunition tested, with the difference between the largest and smallest groups measuring 0.95 inches. Looking at accuracy from this perspective gives potential owners a much better idea of what the rifle can realistically deliver. Also, it reinforces the value of testing several loads before deciding what to carry into the field.
It's worth taking into account that all of this accuracy testing was conducted with the rifle's factory iron sights rather than a magnified optic. With that in mind, the Western Frontier's overall accuracy was respectable and certainly adequate for the type of shooting most owners are likely to do with the rifle. Adding a rimfire scope would remove some of the limitations associated with the factory sights and likely make it easier to determine exactly how much accuracy the rifle and a particular ammunition combination can produce.
Reasons to Consider the .22 Magnum Chambering
One of the strengths of the Western Frontier is its ability to serve as both an enjoyable rimfire and a capable small-game rifle. It retains the mild recoil and easy handling that make rimfires so enjoyable to shoot, but chambering it in .22 Magnum gives the rifle considerably more versatility.
For paper targets and steel at shorter distances, the .22 LR remains the more economical option. The .22 Magnum makes more sense when greater range, increased energy, or hunting performance is a priority. The three loads used during testing also illustrate the variety within the .22 Magnum cartridge. Bullet weights ranged from the lightweight 30-grain CCI VNT to Federal's considerably heavier 50-grain JHP, with the 40-grain Winchester load falling directly between them. That gives shooters the ability to tailor ammunition to their intended use rather than relying on a single load for everything. A lighter, faster projectile may make sense for varmint hunting, while heavier bullets offer another option for small game and general-purpose use.
As the accuracy testing showed, however, choosing ammunition based solely on its specifications doesn't always tell the whole story. Individual rifles can show clear preferences, making some time at the range with several loads worthwhile before settling on a favorite.
The Henry H1 Western Frontier rifle is a pleasure to shoot. (Barb Melloni photo)
Final Thoughts
The Henry H1 Western Frontier pairs the classic look and feel of a traditional lever gun with the extra punch of the .22 WMR cartridge. Design features such as walnut furniture, blued steel, an octagonal barrel, and simple iron sights give it plenty of old-school appeal. At the same time, its 12-round capacity and grooved receiver make it a practical little rifle rather than just a nod to the past.
The .22 Magnum chambering also gives the rifle greater reach and versatility than the .22 LR version, making it well-suited for small-game hunting, varmint hunting, and general range work.
Just as important, the Western Frontier remains enjoyable to shoot. It may not be the lightest or least expensive .22 Magnum available. Still, for shooters who appreciate the feel and operation of a traditional lever action, it offers a combination of character, capability, and range performance that is easy to appreciate. It is the kind of rifle that feels equally at home on the range or carried into the field for an afternoon of small-game hunting. For anyone looking for a rimfire with traditional appeal and a little more punch than a .22 LR, the Western Frontier makes a strong case for itself.
