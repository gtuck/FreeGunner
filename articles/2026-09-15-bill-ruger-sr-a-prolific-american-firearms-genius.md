Bill Ruger Sr.: A Prolific American Firearms Genius

The Mastermind Who Revolutionized Firearm Design and Rebuilt an American Industry

 William Batterman Ruger Sr. achieved much in the gun industry, not the least of which was designing the most popular .22 target pistol ever made in the U.S. (Ken Clubb Illustration) 

September 15, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

William Batterman Ruger Sr. achieved much in the gun industry, not the least of which was designing the most popular .22 target pistol ever made in the U.S.
According to author R.L. Wilson, Bill Ruger Sr. was “a true firearms genius who mastered the disciplines of inventing, designing, engineering, manufacturing, and marketing better than anyone….”
William Batterman Ruger was born on June 21, 1916, in Brooklyn, New York. He learned to shoot at the age of six and spent much time tramping and hunting the rural lands of eastern Long Island. At age 12, he received his very own Remington Model 12 pump-action .22 LR rifle and became a skilled shooter. He attended Alexander Hamilton High School in Brooklyn, where he was a member of the rifle team, and then enrolled in a preparatory school in Salisbury, Connecticut. He went on to study at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill but did not graduate. He hungrily read everything on firearms he could get his hands on. The story goes that he turned an unoccupied college dorm room into a machine shop and started designing what eventually became an experimental light machine gun. He also converted a lever-action Savage Model 99 to semiautomatic. Fortunately, after he showed his light machine gun design to Army Ordnance in Washington, D.C., the Army liked the design enough to hire him as a gun designer at the Springfield Armory. He worked there from late 1939 until the spring of 1940. Not long after that, and after Bill had designed an entirely new machine gun, Winchester and Smith & Wesson offered him jobs, but he held out until Auto Ordnance, who produced the Thompson submachine gun, hired him to develop yet another machine gun in their factory. He worked there until late 1944. For the next few years, Ruger ran his own manufacturing business, supplying record player parts and his own line of deluxe carpenter’s tools. Unfortunately, his company failed and went into receivership. That’s when he looked into his bag of tricks and came up with a .22 rimfire semiautomatic pistol that he had designed some time before.
To finance his new pistol-making venture, Ruger needed a financial backer. In 1948 he formed a partnership with an acquaintance named Alexander Sturm, and as they say, history was made. Sturm was a writer and an artist who came from a wealthy family, and the company he and 
Ruger founded
 went on to become one of the world’s greatest firearms manufacturing companies. Oh, when they began advertising that original .22 semiautomatic pistol in 1949, the MSRP was $37.50. As readers well know, the Ruger Standard Model .22 eventually became the most popular .22 pistol made in the U.S.

I won’t go into all the successful gun designs Bill Ruger came up with because there simply isn’t enough space here to document them all. He designed and built everything from semiautomatic pistols, 
single-­action revolvers
, and double-action revolvers through single-shot, bolt-action, lever-action, and semiautomatic rifles to 
over-under
 and side-by-side shotguns. And Ruger not only developed exciting new gun designs that went over extremely well with customers, but he also developed innovative manufacturing processes that improved efficiency and reduced costs, which enabled the company to make and sell rugged, reliable guns at very reasonable prices to the consumer. For all his ingenuity, he received the Outstanding American Handgunner Award in 1975. He also won many Shooting Industry Academy of Excellence awards.
Ruger’s great success is not without controversy, however. In particular, when he supported a magazine capacity restriction in the late 1980s, he took a lot of flak. But the company survived—and thrived.
William Batterman Ruger Sr. died on July 6, 2002, at age 86, after a long battle with poor health. Mostly self-educated, he had a strong interest in art, literature, history, boats, and cars. He went so far as to design and build the Ruger Special automobile and a 92-foot yacht. But of course, his main interest was guns, and gun enthusiasts have benefitted tremendously from that. We may never see a more prolific American gun designer, but fortunately, the company he founded is still going strong.
