Top 10 Best .22 LR Rifles Under $500 (2026 Edition)

The Ultimate Budget Rimfire Guide for Plinking, Hunting, and Target Shooting

 The author in a field hunting squirrels with a Ruger American Rimfire. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)

August 31, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

Like most shooters, I cut my teeth on a .22 rifle. Looking back, though, it’s a small miracle that I ever continued shooting after my experiences with that gun. It was a single shot of unknown provenance that almost certainly came to us from the local hardware store or—just as likely—as part of a trade for horse tack or tools. The rifle had to be cocked manually and the trigger was atrocious. The “adjustable” ramped sight and fixed front were little more than bumps on a barrel that was pocked with surface rust. It certainly wasn’t a great rifle, but I remember how much I enjoyed shooting it despite its many warts.
Not to sound like a frowning old grump, but kids these days have it easy, and good for them. Today’s .22s are accurate, come with reliable operating systems and crisp, clean triggers. Mounting an optic (either a red dot or variable power scope) is simple, and today’s surface finishes mean that a single trip to the wet hickory woods won’t result in a nasty layer of rust. I’ve tested a handful of today’s .22 rifles and I’m astounded by their accuracy potential. With cheap bulk ammo many will shoot minute of squirrel out to 50 yards, and with premium target ammo like CCI Green Tag or Lapua Midas+ some of these sub-$500 shoot like match guns.
There’s never been a better time to buy a rimfire .22 rifle, but which one is best? We’ve compiled a list of 10 outstanding .22 rifles that are suitable for everything from plinking to backwoods survival. Regardless of your needs, experience, or budget there’s a rifle on this list for you.

Why We Love It
: The 10/22 has long set the standard for accuracy and reliability among .22 autoloaders.
My first real .22 rifle was a 10/22, and it was the antithesis of the single-shot bolt-action with which I began my shooting career. The 10/22 was extremely reliable and astoundingly accurate (my father bought it for me after he’d seen friends shooting hickory nuts from trees with their Ruger’s). The blowback system in the 10/22 is unassailable and has a fifty-year track record of reliability, and the 10/22’s rotary magazine has such a storied reputation for excellent performance that virtually every other bolt-action and autoloading .22 on this list uses that design. There are loads of 10/22 variants, all of which come with a superb trigger right out of the box. If you like to customize your guns then the 10/22 is the ideal place to start since you can find an aftermarket component for virtually every part of this rifle.

Why We Love It
: An austere, affordable .22 from the brand that helped foster America’s love of lever actions
The Ranger is a cousin of sorts to Browning’s BL-22, and while the Ranger lacks some of the Browning’s high-end touches, the Ranger offers reliable performance at about half the cost. Built by a brand that knows a thing or two about lever-actions, the Ranger comes with a lightweight grooved aluminum receiver and a 20-inch barrel. A satin walnut stock and tubular 15-round magazine come standard, and takedown for travel requires removing just one screw. The action is smooth and sleek, and the low operating force required to cycle the action makes the Rabger very easy to run. Couple this with a weight of just 5.25-pounds and a modest price point and you’ll find the Ranger is a .22 LR rifle that everyone can enjoy.

Why We Love It
: An affordable bolt-action, but nothing about it feels cheap.
Some affordable .22 rifles feel as though they were made with bargain bin parts, but the Cascade Rimfire is quite the opposite. CVA’s premium threaded 18-inch barrels are made in Spain and are available with or without straight fluting. Both polymer and wood-stock models are available, and two-piece Picatinny rail mounts allow for easy optic installation. The trigger is crisp and clean, and all Cascade rifles accept Ruger 10/22 rotary magazines so you can expect seamless feeding and function. The accuracy of the models I’ve tested has been excellent, and despite the cascade’s competitive price point nothing about these guns feels like an exercise in cost cutting. This is a .22 LR you’ll enjoy shooting and will be proud to own.

Why We Love It
: Short length of pull doesn’t mean shortcuts in design or construction
New shooters need a safe gun that fits them and is easy to operate safely, and that’s why so many new shooters start out with Savage’s Rascal. The manual safety is easy to reach and operate and the light, crisp 2.5-pound AccuTrigger is smooth and safe. Length of pull is just 11.25-inches, and the overall weight of the Rascal is around 2.7-pounds. This means that even the smallest shooters can handle this gun effectively, and with the adjustable iron sights the Rascal is a surprisingly accurate rifle. Buy a Rascal, a brick of ammunition, and a selection of reactive targets and you’re ready to set up a backyard shooting range that shooters of all sizes, ages, and skill sets can enjoy. Rascal rifles are available in both right and left-handed versions in a variety of stock colors.

Why We Love It
: The little .22 bolt gun with a full-sized feel—and appeal
Springfield’s 2020 Rimfire is a scaled-down version of the company’s popular bolt-action centerfire rifles, and like their larger cousins the rimfire 2020s have the look and feel of a premium firearm. The heavy profile 20-inch threaded barrel is matte blued and threaded ½ x 28, and the Remington 700 hanger-style trigger is adjustable and easy to swap out. Springfield wisely utilizes 10/22 rotary magazines (if it isn’t broken why fix it?) and comes with several class-leading upgrades like a rugged polymer stock with vertical grip, dual cocking cams, and an extended magazine release. There are wood-stocked models as well, and those with higher-grain walnut will push the price well above $500, but all 2020 rimfire rifles are backed by a one-inch accuracy guarantee at 50 yards.

Originally designed as a survival gun for Air Force pilots, the Henry H2 is designed so that the barrel and action fit inside the waterproof, impact-resistant polymer stock. This autoloading .22 LR comes with a pair of eight-round detachable magazines, and when the Henry is folded into its stock for storage in measures just over 16-inches long. Weight is a manageable 3.5 pounds, so this is a gun that can travel in a vehicle, on an ATV, in a saddle scabbard, or in your backpack. On days when you aren’t traversing the back of beyond the Henry makes a fine little plinking rifle that’s perfect for punching tin cans and paper targets. Black and camo models are available, and like all Henry rifles these guns are made in the U.S.A..

Why We Love It
: Like it’s 10/22 cousin, the American Rimfire has become the standard-bearer for its class of rifles.
The American Rimfire is one of the best-selling bolt-action .22s, and when you look at its feature list it isn’t hard to understand why these guns sell so well. An adjustable Marksman balded trigger comes standard, and the American uses the same rotary magazines as its 10/22 stablemate. Removable inserts allow for length of pull adjustments, and the receiver comes with a 3/8-inch machined base and can also accept Weaver #12 bases. The adjustable sights are very good, muzzles are threaded ½ x 28 so adding a suppressor is simple, and the Ruger’s Power Block bedding makes these rifles extremely accurate. I carried an American Rimfire on a squirrel hunt in Kentucky’s Land Between the Lakes and was seriously impressed by the accuracy of this rifle. From a rested position making head shots on grey and fox squirrels was easy, and by midmorning each day I had a limit of bushytails.

Why We Love It
: The affordable and innovative Wildcat is one of the 10/22’s few worthy opponents in the .22 autoloading rifle market.
All .22 autoloaders must face the same daunting question: how does it stack up to the 10/22? For years, companies have been avoiding that question, but the Wildcat takes on the .22 champ and fares quite well. Like the Ruger, Wildcat rifles utilize a reliable blowback-operated action that digests a full range of .22 loads, but unlike the 10/22 you can remove the Wildcat’s entire lower receiver assembly with the push of a button. The Allen wrenches required for complete disassembly and sight adjustment are cleverly stored in the lower receiver. A Picatinny rail atop the receiver allows for optic installation and ghost rings sights come standard. Front and rear sling eyelets are molded into the stock, and there’s an additional length of Pic rail in the front for mounting bipods and other accessories. There are scores of Wildcat models available from Winchester with various stock lengths, finishes, and muzzle configurations, but all Wildcats use 10/22-style rotary mags.

Why We Love It
: The American lever-action with loads of options
As with Ruger’s 10/22, there are so many members of Henry’s H1 family of lever-action .22 rifles that it’s hard to select just one favorite. But all H1 models share some things in common like premium parts and American construction, tubular magazines that allow shooter to use .22 Short, .22 long, and .22 Long Rifle ammunition, and premium American walnut stocks. A compact version of the H1 is available, but the most popular (and best-selling .22 lever-action in the world) is the H1 Classic. My favorite of the lot, though, is the Frontier version (shown here) which comes with a stylish octagon barrel.  And while a few of the Henry rifles listed carry MSRPs over $500 it’s not unusual to find these rifles for sale at or below the $500 mark. Regardless, you know you’re getting a quality American-made lever action every time you purchase a Henry H1.

Why We Love It
: Savage’s entry into the lever-action .22 segment proves a formidable adversary for the henry and Winchester offerings
Sliding in just under the $500 limit is Savage’s new American-made Revel Classic lever-action rifle. This tube-fed .22 LR comes with an 18-inch barrel and a stylish Turkish walnut stock. Magazine capacity is 12 rounds and the receiver is drilled and tapped to accept optics. The Revel is a full-sized rifle with a 14.5-inch length of pull and a weight of 6.1-pounds, but that means it will fit adult shooters better than many compact .22 lever guns. The action is smooth and the trigger on the Revel rifles I’ve tested have been quite good. I particularly like the large lever loop and the easy-to-access crossbolt safety. The Revel is going up against some heavy competition in this market, but it has the goods required to be considered among the best lever-action .22s available today.
