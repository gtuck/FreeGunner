The Great Eight: Remington's Model 8 in .30 Rem

The saga of wood and steel continues as we examine 100-year-old hunting rifles, continuing with the Remington Model 8.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

June 29, 2026

By Dave Emary

At the turn of the last century, significant changes to ammunition and firearm designs took place. Low-­pressure blackpowder cartridges and the guns chambering them were being replaced by modern smokeless-­powder cartridges and new firearm concepts. Several blackpowder cartridges were converted to smokeless powder such as the .45-­70 Government. The “.45” denoted the caliber while the “70” indicated that the case was to be loaded with 70 grains of blackpowder for use in the Springfield Model 1873. By 1898, both the military and commercial loads for the .45-­70 were introduced with smokeless powder backing up a 500-­grain bullet. During the transition, gun manufacturers were forced to evolve stronger steels and designs to accommodate the higher pressure that smokeless powders developed.
The Remington Model 8 was derived from a John Browning patent issued in 1900 for an automatic shotgun. Winchester refused to purchase the patent rights, which led Browning to sell U.S. manufacturing rights to Remington in 1905. The first “Autoloading Rifle” was made in 1906. G&A’s sample was made in 1912 and features a tang sight and short stock with a late-model buttplate. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Before 1905, Remington was selling firearm designs that had been around for 30 years, and it was being beaten by Savage and Winchester. The single-­shot Remington Rolling Block was an example, a breech-­loading rifle produced since 1867. With nearly 1.8 million rifles sold or produced under contract by foreign countries, the Rolling Block was a success, and it accommodated military chamberings such as the .30-­40 Krag and .30-­’03 Springfield. It continued to be popular with users of blackpowder cartridges into the 20th century, including new cartridges such as Winchester’s .30-­30 and .32 Special. Smokeless variants chambered the 7mm and 7.65mm Mauser cartridges, too. However, the medium strength action of the Rolling Block limited its use with several new cartridges of the time and became subject to headspace issues. The single-­shot Rolling Block did not compete well against the 1892 and 1894 Winchesters.
The Model 8 operates using a unique long-recoil action, which features a rotating bolt that stays locked to the barrel. These components recoil together until late in the recoil cycle. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
In 1900, Remington also catalogued the Lee bolt-­action rifle. Primarily designed and patented by James Paris Lee, it was first manufactured and sold by Sharps Rifle Manufacturing Company in 1879 until Sharps went out of business in 1881 and the design was acquired by Remington. The Remington-­Lee rifle is noteworthy because it was the first successful detachable box magazine rifle. The underlying rifle design led to the Lee-­Metford and the Lee-­Enfield rifle. Approximately 20,000 military rifles were sold to China and New Zealand, as well as the U.S. Army and Navy. Military chamberings included the 6mm Navy, .30-­40 Krag, .43 Spanish, and .45-­70. Commercial chamberings included the .30-­30, .303 British, 7mm and 7.65mm Mauser. Remington saw some commercial sales but only 100,000 rifles were sold between 1880 and 1907. Remington needed a modern rifle design and cartridge to address the increasing popularity of the Savage Model 1899 and Winchester Model 1894.
The barrel of the Model 8 is shrouded, and the recoil spring is contained within the shroud around the barrel. The shroud is fixed to the receiver, even as the barrel and spring recoils inside. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Browning Connection
Remington decided to pursue the new automatic rifle concept to try and compete. The first commercial semiautomatic rifles were simple blowback designs for .22-­caliber rimfire cartridges. Winchester released its Model 1903 in .22 Winchester Automatic rimfire, which was scaled up to the Model 1905 that chambered the centerfire .32 and .35 Self Loading (SL) cartridges. These cartridges were on the power scale of the .327 Federal and .357 Magnum but were never regarded for serious big game.

The Model 8 magazine has a five-round capacity, which is fixed and not removable. The small tab between the magazine and triggerguard is the bolt release. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
John M. Browning had been working on a locked breech, long-­recoil-operated semiautomatic shotgun, receiving a patent on October 16, 1900. Browning offered the designs to Winchester’s T. G. Bennett, but after two years of noncommittal and non-­payment, a frustrated Browning demanded a large down payment against future royalties. Unwilling to compromise, Browning parted ways with Winchester until 1918.
Browning turned to Marcellus Hartley, president of Union Metallic Cartridge Co. and Remington Arms Co. in January 1902, soon after the fallout with Winchester. Hartley saw the opportunity for a new automatic rifle that could chamber high-­power cartridges due to its locked-­breech action. Remington paid and had Browning develop a centerfire rifle version. The shotgun became the Remington Model 11 but was delayed due to the untimely death of Hartley.
The Model 8 magazine is top loaded and could be fed using five-round stripper clips. Due to the configuration, scopes were mounted offset to allow top loading. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Browning voyaged to Fabrique Nationale (FN) in Belgium and entered a contract to produce the Automatic 5 in 1903. It was exported to the U.S. until the design was licensed to Remington in 1905 and named the Model 11. The rifle design was eventually produced by FN in Belgium and sold in Europe as the Model 1900 in .35 caliber, but production began in 1910 after its appearance in the U.S. When released to the American market in October 1906, the rifle was called the “Remington Autoloading Rifle.” It was offered at $30 for a Standard Grade and incrementally priced up to $140 for a No. 6 Grade. In 1911, the world’s first autoloading rifle was renamed the “Model 8.”

A straight buttstock with “half pistol grip” was typical on the Model 8. G&A’s sample was either special ordered or modfied with a shorter length of pull, but the stock retains its matching numbers. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
At the same time, Remington designed its own line of cartridges to compete with the Winchester cartridges for the Model 1894. The effort led to the .25, .30, .32 and .35 Remington cartridges introduced with the Model 8 automatic rifle in 1906. The .25, .30 and .32 Remington were rimless versions of the Winchester .25-­35, .30-­30 and .32 Special. The .35 Remington was a unique development, which lives on today.
Remington’s 1909 advert reads, “A battery in itself.” With a lion and lioness illustrated over the profile of the “Remington Autoloading Rifle,” the copy continued, “Even in the face of double danger, you are the master of the situation if you hold the modern Remington which loads itself. It is lightning fast. If you must, you can fire five shots before the first ejected cartridge strikes the ground. Yet your rifle is under such perfect control of the trigger finger that you can exactly time each of the one-­ton knock-­down blows. The Remington is the modern big game rifle of the world. It loads itself by recoil. After military fashion, you shove the loaded cartridges into the magazine from a clip. Your face is absolutely protected by the solid thick steel breech. There is no hammer to catch. The broad flat safety tells plainly whether it is ‘on’ or ‘off.’ It is the latest, safest, and best product of 20th century gun invention. As the modern ocean greyhound, the Lusitania, excels the Clermont, so this modern Remington, which loads itself, excels lever and hammer rifles of the passing generation.”
Buttplates for the Model 8 were considered a shotgun style with checker texture. G&A’s rifle was fitted with a later Model 81 Remington steel buttplate, which was not numbered to the rifle. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 8 is a rather interesting design, featuring five unique patents. It operates on a long-­recoil principle that keeps the rotating bolt locked until the bullet has exited the barrel. The Model 8 appears to have a heavy barrel contour, but, in fact, the barrel has a sleeve over it that contains springs, which are attached to a fixed collar in the sleeve and a collar on the barrel. There is also a spring in the buttstock that is affixed to the bolt carrier. When the gun is fired, the barrel and bolt remain locked and recoil together. When the spring in the barrel sleeve is fully compressed, the barrel stops recoiling and begins to return forward. The bolt unlocks and continues to recoil at this point, extracting and ejecting the spent cartridge. When the bolt spring stops, the recoil of the bolt drives it forward, stripping a cartridge from the magazine and continuing forward until it locks with the barrel. When you fire the Model 8, you can feel and hear a lot of the inner workings. For those interested in the AK47, one look at the Model 8 safety suggests, perhaps, where Mikhail Kalashnikov got inspiration from.
Developed in 1906, the .30 Remington could be loaded with pointed bullets and competed with the flat-point .30-30 Winchester. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The magazine of the Model 8 holds five rounds. It has a single-stack arrangement and is fixed. The gun was intended to be loaded with five-round stripper clips, which are unique to the Model 8. You’ll note a stripper clip guide machined atop the receiver. The gun may also be single loaded by inserting rounds into the magazine through the ejection port. Original stripper clips can be found online but are expensive. Also available are nylon stripper clips.
The standard Model 8 featured a straight-­grip stock, shotgun-­style steel buttplate and a separate forend that was attached to the barrel with a screw. The screw also doubled as a sling swivel. Special order stocks were available with a pistol grip. The back of the receiver was drilled to accept a Lyman or Marbles peep sight, as well. The gun could be configured as an embellished Premier grade, and some were produced this way.

The Model 8 was also popular with law enforcement of the time. Guns could be sent to the Peace Officers Equipment Company in St. Joseph, Missouri, to have 20-­round magazines installed. Notably, Texas Ranger Frank Hamer used a 20-­round magazine-­fed Model 8 in the takedown of American outlaws Bonnie Parker and Clyde Barrow in 1934. “Hamer’s Hammer” was chambered in .35 Remington. Though most of the attention following the ambush featured the BAR, dumping 20 rounds of .35 Rem. through the windshield of the ’34 Ford from 10 feet probably could have gotten the job done alone!
An adjustable rear notch sight was featured on many model 8s. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Model 8 became the Model 81 in 1936, and production ended in 1950. The gun sold reasonably well, but Remington wasn’t satisfied. Nearly 170,000 rifles were sold between 1906 and 1950, and some 112,000 were Model 8 variants. Sales were probably a bit limited by the guns’ rather high cost. Though a bit heavy, the Model 8 was beautifully made and earned a reputation for being extremely reliable and accurate.
A bead front sight was standard on the Model 8, but tang aperture sights were common. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
G&A’s Test Rifle
Our sample was a rather unusual Model 8. The entire gun — other than the buttplate — was original. The buttplate looks as if it was sourced from a later Model 81 with a checkered-­type steel plate, the only part that had a mismatched serial number. The stock was special order with a pistol grip and very short length of pull of just 12¼ inches. Whoever spec’d it must have been of small stature. As acquired by us, the gun came with a Lyman receiver peep sight and an early 1950s-­vintage Stith scope mount and Weaver K2.5 scope. I found the scope desirable because of my rather compromised near-­70-­year-­old eyes. The short length-of-pull stock was perfect for the eye relief and mounting position of the scope, too. Editor-­in-­Chief Eric Poole and I tested the rifle with both the peep sight and the 2.5X scope. With serial number 29389, G&A’s rifle was made in 1912 and chambered in .30 Rem.
The swivel also functioned as a bolt to secure the forend to the barrel sleeve. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The trigger pull was a crisp 3 pounds, 6 ounces, and the gun weighed 7 pounds, 8 ounces without the scope. The gun balanced well when carried at the receiver and was very tight, showing little wear other than some lack of finish from carrying. I scoped the bore with my Lyman borescope, which revealed that it was in excellent condition. The bore was bright and shiny with no pitting.
To reproduce vintage ammunition as close as possible, I consulted a 1923 Remington catalog. It describes the .30 Rem. as being loaded with a 170-­grain flat-­point (FP) bullet for a velocity of 2,020 feet per second (fps). Available powder speeds at that point were equivalent to IMR 3031, 4895 and 4064. The .30 Rem. would most likely have been loaded with 4895 or 4064. I chose to use the 
Hornady
 170-­grain FP and CCI 200 primer. After consulting the Hodgdon reloading manual, I tried both IMR 4895 and IMR 4064. I settled on a load of 30.3 grains IMR 4064. However, the gun functioned better with a little more powder in the load than what could be loaded with 4895. The accompanying table shows our results at 100 yards. Poole did the accuracy testing for consistent comparison. Three five-­shot groups were fired at 100 yards and another at 200 yards. We had very limited production ammunition available for the Model 8, hence the limited number of groups. The cases for our subsequent tests were made from .30-­30 cases by re-­cutting the rims in a lathe and reforming them in RCBS dies.
G&A’s test rifle was equipped with a 1950s-vintage Stith side mount and barrel mount, containing a Weaver K2.5 scope. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
At The Range
We also wanted to explore the effective range of the Model 8 in .30 Rem. with period sights. The mid-­1920s .30 Rem. with 170-­grain bullet is ballistically effective out to 225 yards. With iron sights, we confirmed the Model 8’s hunting range would have been limited by the eyesight of the shooter or inherent accuracy potential of the rifle and ammunition combination.
Pushing up on the Model 8 safety lever on the right side of the receiver blocks the trigger and prevents the bolt from cycling. Pressing down disengages the safety lever. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
We were rather disappointed with the accuracy of our rifle at 100 yards using its peep sight. A small front sight would have probably improved group sizes. We felt the rifle should be capable of better accuracy and decided to re-­mount the scope. With the scope secured in place, accuracy dramatically improved. We shot at a steel target at 200 yards featuring a 10-­inch circle to represent the kill zone of a deer. The iron-­sighted Model 8 was capable of reliably striking the 10-­inch circle between 175 and 200 yards. Even with a period scope in place, the Model 8 far exceeded the ballistic capability of the cartridges it was chambered for.
The short bolt can be manually manipulated using the stubby charging handle. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
We came to really like the scoped version of the Remington Model 8. This rifle configuration was easy to carry and stunningly accurate. The trigger was very nice for any rifle, too, let alone an automatic. Recoil was noticeable but not uncomfortable.
