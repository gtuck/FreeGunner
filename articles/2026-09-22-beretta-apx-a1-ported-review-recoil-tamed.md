Beretta APX A1 Ported Review: Recoil Tamed

Testing the Flat-Shooting Upgrades of Beretta's Striker-Fired 9mm

 Beretta delivers a flatter shooting APX A1 with its new Ported model. (Joshua Swanagon photo)

September 22, 2026

By Joshua Swanagon

Back at the end of March/beginning of April, I was fortunate enough to be invited to Beretta’s 500
th
 anniversary celebration in Gardone Val Trompia, Italy. While we were there, we enjoyed factory tours, dinners, meet-and-greets, the Museum, and more. However, it was at range day that Beretta trotted out some of the upcoming models, like the APX A1 Ported. The range was incredible, and the guns were there to run.
As I am sure you are aware, the APX A1 is not new. In fact, since its release in 2022, the 
APX A1 family
 has grown to 10 models and variations, including this one. Designed for everything from concealed carry to combat, the APX A1 family has something for everyone. Now, building on the full-size model, the 
APX A1 Ported
 takes a proven design and delivers faster follow-up shots.
Let’s check it out.
The barrel features a port and rides in a slide with seven ports to help with recoil mitigation. (Joshua Swanagon photo)
Digging Into the APX A1 Ported
As I mentioned, the new Ported model is the next evolution of the proven full-size. Building on a durable polymer grip frame, the APX A1 Ported has oversized ambidextrous controls. A serrated combat trigger guard surrounds the trigger, providing ample room for gloved hands. The grip features aggressive texturing, working with lightly textured contact points just under the slide, for enhanced retention and indexing.

Riding atop the frame, the slide shows the most noticeable changes. Housed within the slide is the 4.25-inch ported barrel, with matching porting on the slide itself. In total, seven slide ports lighten the slide, enhance the aesthetics of the aggressive serrations, and help reduce recoil. However, with the addition of the slide porting, the sight radius is reduced from 6.1 to 6 inches.
Providing the point of aim are a blacked-out serrated rear sight and an orange high-vis front sight. Likewise, the optic-ready slide accepts the RDS of your choice, with an additional adapter plate. I opted for the Trijicon RMR. However, it is worth noting that the sights are not suppressor height. So, if you want to co-witness, you will have to swap them for aftermarket sights.
The APX A1 Ported builds on the APX A1 full size, with porting on the barrel and slide for reduced recoil. (Joshua Swanagon photo)
The APX A1 family boasts an enhanced trigger system that delivers a light pull, crisp, clean break, and short reset. Likewise, it has relatively short take-up to the wall, minimal stacking (but some), and near-negligible overtravel once the shot breaks.

I have seen reports that the APX A1 Full-Size has a pull weight of 6-7 pounds. So, I tested the APX A1 Ported with my Wheeler Digital Trigger Gauge. I averaged 5.15 pounds over five pulls.
Getting the Right Grip
I am not going to oversell myself by trying to convince anyone that I am the world’s best shot. Although I can ring steel consistently, I won't be competing anytime soon. However, I can hold my own and get on target effectively.
When we went with Beretta to 
Concaverde Shooting Range
 in Lonato del Garda, Italy, we got to shoot the APX A1 Ported for the first time. Although it felt good in the hand, I was not getting the kind of accuracy that I would have liked. It shot great and felt great, but some of my shots were going high.
We moved to another station with a couple of different pistols, and I was ringing all the steel without issue. So, I was determined to try the APX A1 again before moving on to the rifle range.
Since everyone was done shooting it, I had a little more time to really get a feel for it. I found that when my wrist locked naturally, it had a high point of aim, and I was reverting to my natural grip on some of the shots. Once I identified the issue, I was able to ring all the steel, including the 3-inch rounds. Ok, now I could move on.

The APX A1 Ported ships with two additional backstraps, so you can dial in the fit. (Joshua Swanagon photo)
When I received the T&E model, I noticed the same issue. Fortunately, the APX A1 Ported ships with three interchangeable backstraps (S/M/L). So, I installed the large backstrap, and now it consistently points naturally on presentation.
Changing the backstrap was easy and only required a punch or screwdriver. I had it swapped out in under a minute, including disassembling and reassembling the pistol.
Feeding the APX A1 Ported
For my testing, I ran three different ammunition types from Winchester, Federal, and CCI.
The Winchester round is the 115-gr. Target & Practice load that I had kicking around in my ammo pile. According to the box, it boasts a velocity of 1,125 FPS at 25 yards.
From Federal is the 124-gr. American Eagle, one of my favorite target rounds. The box lists the velocity at 1,095 FPS at 25 yards.
Finally, from CCI is the Blazer 147-gr. Subsonic. The box listed the velocity at 900 FPS, and this provided the best performance of the three ammo types.
The APX A1 Ported ships with two 17-round magazines and one 21-round magazine. So, you are ready to rock, right out of the box. I think every company should include at least three magazines with each pistol. Good on you, Beretta.
The author shooting the APX A1 Ported at defensive distance. (Art Vanadia photo)
Range Time
Once at the range, I set up my DOA shooting bench with the Ransom Rest Multi Cal Steady Rest at 25 yards and got to work. During my courses of fire, I took chronograph readings with my Garmin Xero C2, which are listed below. Each ammo type averaged a velocity very close to the FPS advertised on the box.

Advertisement

Of the three types, I got the best results from the CCI Blazer with an average group of 3.75 inches. The best group from the Blazer was 2.5 inches. However, with the other two ammo types, I was getting some odd results, especially with the Winchester. Although I got some decent groups, I also got some odd flyers. Much like in Italy, I wasn’t getting quite the accuracy I would have liked, but it was serviceable. At the very least, everything was on mass.
After bench testing, I moved up to defensive range and ran more rounds in rapid-fire succession to test overall handling. The port, combined with the lighter spring, kept the APX A1 very flat and the dot barely left the viewport. It was easy to quickly cluster all shots in the center of mass.
Over a course of roughly 250 rounds throughout the day, the APX A1 Ported did not suffer one malfunction. It cycled beautifully and stayed on point.
The optic-ready slide is ready for the RDS of your choice with an additional adapter plate. (Joshua Swanagon photo)
Conclusion
After my trip to Italy to visit Beretta, I have been pretty excited about the APX A1 Ported being released. I think that some of that is due to my less-than-ideal experience with it on the range in Italy. Getting a little more time with it would give me an opportunity to get more familiar with it.
Ergonomically, once I swapped the backstrap, it was very comfortable. Likewise, the trigger is light and crisp. And that recoil? Flat and on point. It was a soft shooter that wanted to get back on target in a hurry.

Advertisement

I would have liked to get better accuracy from it. But the truth is, although I have done a lot of shooting over the years, I don’t have a lot of experience with Beretta. Someone more experienced with the Beretta platform would probably drive tacks with this model.
Some pistols fit you so well that you just can’t miss, and it feels like cheating, and some pistols take a little more acclimation. The APX A1 Ported is the latter for me. However, I suspect that a few hundred more rounds would dial us in together nicely. But that is part of the fun of this sport, isn’t it? More trigger time.
