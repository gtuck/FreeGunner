By Bob Campbell

The niche once occupied by the Iver Johnson 55A may be filled today by the Heritage Rough Rider or Ruger Wrangler, but no manufacturer really occupies the niche once owned by the Iver Johnson company. Founded by Norwegian-born Iver Johnson, the company lasted from 1871 to 1993. Its products were affordable and workmanlike, with a lineup that included single-action, double-action, and break-top revolvers.
The double-action 55A Target was manufactured from 1961 to 1978; texts sometimes disagree, but this is the general era. This particular sample features a six-inch barrel; I have never seen the cataloged 4.5-inch version.
Features
The 55A features an eight-shot cylinder. The chambers are not recessed, but the cylinder features a raised section as a blast shield. Each chamber is adjacent to a notch in this shield that allows the hammer to strike the cartridge case rim.
The revolver features a base pin that is removed to allow cylinder removal for loading and unloading. This base pin may be used to drive out empty or unfired cases.

Alternately there is a swinging loading gate that allows loading the chambers, which differs from the 55 Target that had only a groove machined in the frame for single loading. You may use a punch or long pin to drive out the cartridge cases one at a time. It is about as easy to simply remove the cylinder for loading and unloading.
The revolver is nicely finished even after 50 years or so of service. The last two inches of the six-inch barrel are worn, perhaps by a holster or living in a bureau drawer. The hammer spur, top of the loading gate, and trigger face are either serrated or checkered.
The grips are made of Tenite, a type of Bakelite, by Iver Johnson. They’re fully checkered, and the left grip panel features a thumb rest. The grips are well designed and comfortable for most hand sizes.

Trigger
While the 55A is a double-action handgun, the trigger is best referred to as a cocking lever as the sear is exposed and is the true “trigger.” The trigger face, as it may be called, is pressed until it meets and trips the exposed sear. This was a common design at one time, and to the best of my knowledge it began with the 1858 Starr revolver.
The double-action trigger press is horrible! It was off the scale for the RCBS trigger pull scale, but I would estimate it at 20 pounds. The
 single-action
 pull, in contrast, is a crisp and short four pounds.
Other Details
One may ask how you could label a fixed-sight revolver—with its gutter-type rear and square-blade front—a target model, but the wide gutter does provide a clear and decent sight picture. The sights are regulated for 40-grain high-velocity loads.
Despite Iver Johnson’s long and storied history of “Hammer the Hammer,” which was heavily advertised when the company introduced the transfer-bar safety, the 55A doesn’t have a transfer bar. Therefore, the gun should be carried with an empty chamber or by lowering the hammer into a safety notch.
Wrapping it Up
Accuracy with my sample is surprisingly good, at least on the par with
 modern single-action .22s
. I find the 55A an interesting revolver and a link to a time when these guns were common. Prices vary. Mine was picked up in a local shop for $150, and I found several in excellent condition online for $250 or so.
