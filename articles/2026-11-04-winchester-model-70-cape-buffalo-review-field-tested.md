Winchester Model 70 Cape Buffalo Review: Field-Tested

Testing the Iconic Rifle Against Africa's Most Dangerous Game

 Mike Romney borrowed the author’s rifle for a hunt, and shot this monster 45-inch cape buffalo bull from around 200 yards. No photo can do this bull justice—he’s legitimately prehistoric in size—but the rifle looks pretty good here! (Joseph von Benedikt photo)

November 04, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

Once upon a time, Winchester held the unofficial title of best American maker of dangerous-game rifles. During the era of Model 70 Super Grade safari rifles chambered in 
.375 Holland & Holland Magnum
 and stopping rifles in .458 Winchester Magnum, Winchester was a power player in the realm of equipping American safari-goers. The company produced high-class big bores at a working-man’s price, and the quality was such that one could—and often did—bet one’s life on the Winchester rifle clenched in one’s fists.
That was a long time ago. More than sixty years, not to put too fine a point on it. Interim rifles such as the Model 70 Safari Express and Model 70 Alaskan coasted along, functionally but not artistically filling that gap.
In autumn of 2026, Winchester refilled that gap, and yes, did so artistically. The first of a promised series of high-grade African safari rifles named for various iconic African game species and chambered appropriately, the Model 70 Safari Express Cape Buffalo Edition rifle reviewed here is a superbly refined and elegant .375 H&H dangerous-game rifle.
Here the new M70 Buffalo is pictured with a pre-64 Model 70. Both are chambered in .375 H&H; both are reliable and accurate. As a lover of vintage Winchesters it hurts to write this, but the more beautiful of the two is the newer rifle. Stock lines are more elegant; features are more refined; and the wood is better. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
We’ll take a deep dive into what makes this rifle special, but in short, it’s fit with a good stick of French walnut, features a barrel profile and length optimized for balance, weight, and pointability, it has an appealing red recoil pad, is fit with the right sort of iron sights and barrel-band sling swivel stud, and is endowed with an engraved, gold-inlaid floorplate and grip cap.

Winchester chose to use the slim, trim barrel profile of the M70 Alaskan rather than the stout profile used on the 
M70 Safari Express
. As a result, rifle balance is lively in the hands and at the shoulder. However, unlike the 25-inch barrel on the Alaskan, this M70 Cape Buffalo rifle’s barrel is cut to 24 inches. It gives a nice combination of good velocity, useful sight radius, and acceptable muzzle blast.
A brass bead front sight is installed atop a ramp that’s finely checkered against glare. A removeable hood protects the front sight. For fast work on dangerous game I’d remove the hood, personally, and would probably replace the brass bead with a white one that would glow against the black hide of a cape buffalo.
A trim barrel-band sling attachment point is installed a couple inches in front of the lovely ebony-black fore-end tip. It’s the right spot, both traditional and practical, and a benefit to both aesthetics and ergonomics. For those who’ve never shot a stout-recoiling dangerous-game rifle, here’s why the sling’s location matters: During recoil the forend slides rearward in the support hand, and a sling swivel stud in the traditional spot near the end of the fore-end will slam into and bruise your hand.

Rear sights are robust, elegant, and adjustable. Front sights feature a brass bead on a ramp. Removable hoods protect the sight from glare and bumps. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
The rear sight is a sturdy adjustable blade. It combines the wide, shallow “V” that professional dangerous-game hunters prefer with a U-notch in the bottom of that V to enable fine shooting. I confess I’m conflicted; the purist in me wishes it was just a clean shallow V-notch, but the accuracy nut in me likes the finer U-notch. At any rate, it’s an excellent affair machined by New England Custom Guns (NECG), and sits in a sturdy but elegant island-type sight base.
Finish on the Model 70 Cape Buffalo’s steel is a nice satin blue. I love the high-gloss blue on vintage Super Grades and in truth would have preferred it, but I’m constrained to admit that the satin finish glares less in the sun.
The action is Winchester’s legendary Model 70 controlled-feed design that’s renown for reliability and durability. It features the massive Mauser-type claw extractor preferred by savvy dangerous-game specialists around the world. The claw captures the rim of each fresh cartridge as it feeds up from the magazine and controls it all the way in and out of the chamber. This effectively prevents double-feeding of the type common to push-feed actions—particularly when rifles are run in a half-panic.
Similarly, the safety is a three-position affair that both locks the bolt shut and blocks the firing pin—not just the trigger—when engaged in the rear position. The firing pin literally cannot fall, unlike with the common rocker-type side safeties that just block the trigger from moving.
Model 70 actions are Mauser-based and feature the massive, controlled-feed extractor and fixed, mechanical ejector preferred by dangerous-game specialists. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
To enable the shooter to clear the chamber and unload the magazine without taking the safety off, the M70 safety has a middle position that unlocks the bolt while leaving the safety engaged.

My only gripe with the Model 70 Cape Buffalo is with the trigger. It’s beautifully crisp, but requires six pounds of pressure to fire it. I’m all for a reasonably heavy trigger on a dangerous-game rifle intended to be operated when under extreme pressure, but a 3.5-pound pull is plenty “reasonable.” Six pounds is a bit too much. I’m going to send Winchester a check for this fine rifle, and right after I get my receipt I’ll install a match-grade Timney trigger in it.
Bottom metal is machined of one piece, with an elegant trigger bow and sturdy but refined lines. The hinged floorplate is engraved with an African game scene—in this case a massive gold-filled Cape Buffalo that’s looking at you as if you owe him money, to paraphrase the legendary Robert Ruark. A backdrop of African savannah and umbrella-like trees with subtle floral engraving frame the buffalo. Perhaps my favorite decorative element is the outline of the African continent inlaid in gold into the steel grip cap.
Additional tasteful elements include a nice soft-but-not-squishy red rubber recoil pad, a swept cheekpiece, inlet Super Grade rear sling swivel stud, and the afore-mentioned black forend tip.
Importantly, the Cape Buffalo rifle has dual steel crossbolts that reinforce the stock at points susceptible to cracking or giving way from recoil. This .375 H&H will soldier on for decades of hard use.
At the Range
Putting just a trace of thread-locker on the screws, I installed Talley steel bases on the rifle, then mounted a new Leupold 2-10X 42mm VX-5HD Gen 2 scope in quick-detach steel Talley rings. I’ve used these rings on heavy-recoiling rifles for decades, and they hold reliably and come right back to zero when re-installing the scope.
A few shots served to marry point of aim with point of impact at 100 yards, and I was in business. Pulling the scope off, I fired a few shots with the iron sights to get them set. I was pleasantly surprised to find point of impact close when I aimed at a knot in a stump some 25 yards away. A minor adjustment got me hitting center on a target at 100 yards—and would you believe it, my Swift A-Frame ammo put bullets less than an inch apart even with my middle-age eyes aiming those iron sights.
With the scope back on, I tested 300-grain Swift A-Frames and 300-grain Swift Breakaway Solids to see how close together they impacted at 100 yards. Breakaway solids are non-expanding projectiles designed for driving ultra deep on dangerous pachyderms such as elephant, hippo, and rhino, and for charging or escaping wounded cape buffalo that must be raked from stem to stern. A bullet that will travel the full length of a buffalo exponentially increases the likelihood of a major bone or nerve center being impacted and disabled. That, dear reader, can mean the difference between living and dying.

The two loads produced one gratifying cluster of bullet holes on my 100-yard target. I was ready to hunt cape buffalo.
On the stalk, the M70 Buffalo is lively in the hands, quick to the shoulder, and beautifully balanced. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
On the Buffalo Trail in Mozambique
Two weary days of air travel landed me in Mozambique’s vast, remote Coutada 11 for a safari arranged through Global Hunting Adventures. A group of seven of us were in pursuit of cape buffalo and plains game, hunting from a classic tent camp in the middle of nowhere.
The Zambeze river delta offers a different sort of buffalo hunt. You may track, yes, but more likely you’ll rove deep into the swamps via vintage amphibious all-terrain vehicles until you spot distant clouds of white egrets signaling the presence of buffalo. Then, you must dismount, strap on your bravery, and stalk through croc- and hippo-inhabited river channels and across floating papyrus beds. Mud, sawgrass, glaring sun and sweeping rain offer the ultimate conditions in which to aggressively test a dangerous-game rifle.
My pal Rafe Nielsen, head of marketing at Browning and Winchester, had two buffalo bulls on license. He stood knee-deep in swamp water and mud and shot his first—an ancient dagga bull—as it lunged across a 100-yard expanse of river channel. The first 300-grain Swift A-Frame killed the bull; a follow-up added an exclamation point. I stood overwatch with my own Model 70 Cape Buffalo rifle as Rafe waded into the muddy river channel with the PH and trackers to secure a towrope to the old bull so it could be dragged to solid land and processed.

Rafe’s second bull didn’t go down so easily. Hit hard, it escaped into a towering papyrus thicket and turned to wait. Huffing blood and bellowing defiance, the bull stood his ground until Rafe was able to thread the needle with a follow-up shot and bring him down.
Wanting a special old bull with some width, I held out over several days. Finally, many hours into a hunt across remote floodplains, a worn-horned, massive-bodied old bull showed 45 yards away across a river channel and I couldn’t resist. My Swift A-Frame slammed through the bull’s shoulders, taking out both lungs and the top of his heart. Working the bolt fast, I drove a Swift Breakaway Solid into the departing bull’s hip, raking him tail to teakettle. He dove behind a copse of papyrus. Seconds later, we heard that most beautiful sound; the mournful death bellow which signifies that one is not about to spend the evening following a wounded buffalo bull, after all.
Culling opportunities on common reedbuck and waterbuck in Mozambique provided a good avenue for extensive shooting with the new special-edition M70 Buffalo rifle. Predictably, it performed in spectacular fashion—and looked extremely well meanwhile. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Although I’d brought a light rifle for plains game, I enjoyed shooting the .375 H&H so much I stuck with it, taking waterbuck and several reedbuck with it. It points like a part of me, shoots accurately, and kills authoritatively. Even packing it is pleasant, at 10 pounds, 4 ounces, it’s no mountain rifle, but that weight tames recoil and steadies my aim.
Less than a year later, my good friend Mike Romney borrowed the rifle for a cape buffalo hunt in the Eastern Cape of South Africa. An arid land gashed with canyons and crowned with towering escarpments; it offered few buffalo but awesome genetics. Mike’s chance came when they dropped off the rocky rim of a plateau and found two old bulls brushed up 200 yards steeply below. Threading the needle, he drove a .375-caliber A-Frame quartering down into the bull’s ribs, then followed up with a fast shot on the running bull that broke its neck and dropped it.
Even in an area known for big bulls, this one was special. Mike’s PH stood the skull with one horn against a wall, and stretched a measuring tape across to the other. The deep-dropping, huge-hooked horns span 45 inches—a world-class bull.

It’s not even a year old, and already this special-edition Model 70 Cape Buffalo has racked up several impressive kills on dangerous game. I suspect, like the legendary pre-64 Model 70 rifles in .375 H&H that inspired it, this fine model will be running reliably, shooting accurately, and killing authoritatively for a very long time.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
