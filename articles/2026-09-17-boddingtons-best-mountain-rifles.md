Boddington's Best Mountain Rifles

Mountain hunting is a demanding task, and needs the right rifle/cartridge pairing for the job. Here are some that work best.

 On his next mountain hunt Boddington will probably use this Flat Creek Precision 7 PRC with handloaded 175-grain ELD-X. At about 3000 fps, the 7 PRC has more than enough power and range for any sheep or goat on the planet.

September 17, 2026

By Craig Boddington

You know the adage “Beware the one-gun man.” Serious mountain hunters are usually one-gun guys (and gals). They find something that works and stick with it, but that doesn’t mean it’s always the perfect choice.
Gunwriting great Jack O’Connor is best-known for championing the .270 Winchester. He loved his .270s, especially his Biesen No. 2, an unusually accurate Winchester M70 Featherweight, stocked by Al Biesen, used for mountain game (and much else) during his last 20 years. O’Connor wasn’t truly a one-gun man. His first wild sheep were taken with a 7x57, and he used other rifles and cartridges.
Charlie Ren and Jack O’Connor, August 1935, in Sonora on O’Connor’s first sheep hunt. O’Connor hadn’t yet turned to the .270; he’s holding a 7x57 and Ren is holding his 1899 Savage .25-35, “all the rifle I need.”
The guy that introduced O’Connor to sheep hunting was a true one-gun man. Charlie Ren of Ajo, Arizona outfitted in Sonora before WWII. Ren’s client list is a who’s who of the 1930s hunting world: Wilson DuComb, Grancel Fitz, Frank Hibben, Elmer Keith, Jack O’Connor. Ren carried a Savage 1899 in .25-35 and told O’Connor it was “all the gun I need.” Grancel Fitz was also a one-gun man. He was the first person known to take all varieties of North American big game, including all four wild sheep, all with his .30-06 Springfield by Griffin & Howe.
New York ad man Grancel Fitz with his first wild sheep, a Dall ram taken in 1935. He used his Griffin & Howe Springfield .30-06, and would use the same rifle to take all varieties of North American big game, including the four wild sheep.
We could argue that neither the .25-35 nor the .30-06 are perfect tools for hunting sheep and goats, but it’s difficult to argue with success. Truth is, there are many suitable tools. O’Connor argued for the .270 Winchester, while others preferred fast 7mms. Brothers Bert and Chris Klineburger pioneered much of the mountain hunting in Central Asia, using an early M700 Remington in 7mm Rem Mag. My longtime hunting partner Joe Bishop was also a staunch Big Seven guy, taking almost all his mountain game with an out-of-the-box Sako.

Among hunters who have pursued mountain game around the world, it’s tough to find a more experienced group than the winners of the 
Weatherby
 Hunting and Conservation Award. You can’t win the Weatherby without a lot of sheep and goats. A few years back, someone canvassed the living winners. Among this group, I wasn’t surprised to find that the majority used one of the fast .30s. Several used .300 Weatherby Magnums, but .300 H&H, .300 Win Mag, .300 WSM, .300 RUM, and .300 Jarrett were also cited.
Although wild sheep aren’t as tough as goats, some of the Asian sheep are much bigger than ours. This is the Altai argali from Mongolia, largest-bodied sheep in the world. Boddington used his Blaser R8 with .300 Wby Magnum barrel and 
Hornady
 200-grain ELD-X, one shot at about 350 yards.
I accept the wisdom of finding one rifle that you know intimately and trust, but I’m hardly a one-gun man. In part, because I’m a gun guy, fascinated by hunting rifles and cartridges. Also, I’m a gunwriter. Using various rifles gives me more to write about.
I took my first wild sheep, a Stone ram, in 1973, with a .375 H&H. In my defense, it was a mixed-bag hunt, and a chance for a ram wasn’t readily on the table. It worked fine but is the least suitable cartridge I’ve used on mountain game. To me, that means sheep and goats. Even though other animals live in mountains, and some sheep and goats don’t. My complete list also includes: .25 WSSM, .264 Win Mag, 6.5 PRC, 26 Nosler, .270 Win, .270 WSM, .270 Wby Mag, 7x57, .280 Rem, .280 AI, 7mm Rem Mag, 7mm STW, .30-06, .300 H&H, .300 Win Mag, .300 Wby Mag, 7.82 (.308) Warbird, and 8mm Rem Mag.
In chronological progression, and arranged left to right, these are the cartridges the author has used for most of his mountain hunting since 1980: 7mm Rem Mag, .270 Win, .270 WSM, 8mm Rem Mag, .300 Wby Mag, .300 Win Mag. All have proven themselves versatile and effective.
It’s a myth that all mountain animals are taken at long range, as hunters with bows, muzzleloaders, and handguns have taken wild sheep. In mountain hunting, the situation dictates the shot. Terrain, vegetation, wind. Compared to most deer, sheep and goats aren’t all that wary, but you must defeat their near X-Ray vision. As a guide told Jack O’Connor: Sheep see through thin rock.

The turs are tough, big-bodied Asian goats. The author used his Rifles, Inc. .300 Wby Mag to take this Dagestan tur in Azerbaijan.
I’ve taken a few rams and billies past 500 yards, others less than 50. It takes more time and some luck to bank on a short-range shot. Long-range shots are frequently offered. However, the mountains are against you. In broken terrain, judging wind varies from difficult to impossible. With mountain game, if you aren’t certain, you have a “no shot” situation. Mountain hunts are expensive, and it takes so much effort to get an opportunity. Blow it and you must start over. My rule is simple: I don’t shoot unless I’m sure.
The author’s longtime hunting partner Joe Bishop took almost all his mountain game with a Sako 7mm Rem Mag, here with an awesome Bezoar goat from Turkey.
Sooner or later, everybody misses, and close shots can be missed as well as far, but as distance increases the odds stack up. I have no interest in extreme-range shooting at mountain game. If you aren’t sure, best try to get closer. Or try again tomorrow. Once located, sheep and goats usually don’t move all that far overnight.
Instead of my long list, let’s look at cartridges that I’ve used on multiple mountain hunts over several years: 7mm Rem Mag, .270 Win, .270 WSM, 8mm Rem Mag, .300 Wby Mag, .300 Win Mag. In that short list, the obvious anomaly is the 8mm Rem Mag. Goats are tougher than sheep and size varies in both families, but there is no need for any cartridge above .30. Mostly an elk rifle, my 8mm Rem Mag was super-accurate. I took it to Turkmenistan in ’95, and to Tajikistan in ’99. It hammered game but was too heavy, and its power unnecessary.
This huge-bodied Himalayan ibex was taken in Pakistan in 2009 with a .264 Winchester Magnum. It was shot three times in the shoulder with no apparent effect, then came sliding down the mountain. Since then, Boddington has used fast .30s, especially for larger-boded Asian animals.
Since I can’t imagine taking a risky extreme-range shot, the older I get the more I think Professor O’Connor was right. His .270 Win is ideal for most mountain game, with plenty of power, and flat shooting enough. My wife Donna has used her .270s on four continents, and I’ve used a half-dozen .270s. I’m also happy with the 7mm Rem Mag, and hunted plenty with it in the 80s and 90s. In the early 2000s I took the .270 WSM on several mountain hunts: Mongolia, 2nd Marco Polo, and Stone sheep in Yukon. Like the .270 Wby Mag, it’s faster, flatter-shooting, and delivers visibly more energy than the .270 Win.
Donna Boddington with an excellent desert bighorn, taken with her pet MGA .270 Winchester, the rifle she has used for almost all her mountain hunting.
Like many Americans, I have a long history with .30-calibers. Although the Asian argalis are bigger than our sheep, sheep aren’t tough in general. Goats are, and some Asian goats are huge. On my first hunt in Pakistan in 2009, I took a .264 Win Mag. Great for small-bodied urial sheep and the small-bodied Sindh ibex, but not impressive on Himalayan ibex, which can get as big as Shetland ponies. Although the fastest 6.5mms—26 Nosler and 6.5-.300 Wby Mag—deliver more energy than the 6.5 PRC and my old .264, I decided 6.5mm and .270 bullets weren’t quite enough for the larger Asian varieties. Since then, I’ve pretty much reverted to fast .30s.

Like many, the author has gone to larger scopes with dial-up turrets…even though most of his shots at mountain animals are well within “Kentucky elevation” distance. The Leupold VX6 3-18x44 is a favorite, a plenty big enough scope but not huge.
Lex Webernick (Rifles, Inc.) made me a .300 Wby Mag on a left-hand M70 action. Later, I got a .300 Wby Mag barrel for the Blaser. For the past 15 years those two rifles, both tack-drivers, have accounted for most of my mountain game. However, the Rifles, Inc. .300 Wby was built heavy (at my insistence), and my Blaser has a walnut stock that I’ve pretty much ruined. Kenny Jarrett built me a lighter Ridge Walker in .300 Win Mag, synthetic stock, medium-contour 24-inch barrel. I used it for a Dall ram in the Brooks Range, and for a big ibex in Tajikistan in 2022.
Right now, I have no mountain hunts planned. In case I get the itch, Charlie Barnes at Flat Creek Precision made me a 7 PRC that shoots sub MOA with 175-grain ELD-X at 3000 fps, plenty for any sheep or goat that walks, at any distance I’m likely to tackle. As for other new “fast twist” cartridges for extra-heavy bullets: In the specialized world of mountain hunting, they’re probably not for me. In .30s, I shot 180-grain bullets for years, and now I’m  shooting 200-grain “low drags.” Even the largest goats don’t require more, and I’ve had my say on extreme-range shots.
Donna Boddington’s MGA .270 weighs 5.5 pounds with Leupold VXIII 3.5-10X scope. For her height and weight, this rifle is perfect for her. Taller and heavier, the author finds it too light and difficult to steady; he prefers two or three pounds more rifle weight.
Let’s talk about rifle weight and scopes: Donna loves her MGA .270, at just over 5.5 pounds topped with a 3.5-10X scope. This setup works for her because she’s several inches shorter and too many pounds lighter than me. It’s a joy to carry, almost weightless. However, I find it whippy and hard to steady, especially when I’m out of breath, which is my usual condition in mountains.
For me, once again, Professor O’Connor had it right, and my setup is around eight pounds ready to go, compromising between portability and shootability. Today’s mountain rifles are mostly stocked in synthetic, which O’Connor never saw. That saves weight, but we add larger scopes than he imagined, which brings the weight back up.
The author’s Jarrett Ridge Walker in .300 Win Mag weighs in at about 8.5 pounds with a Leupold VX6 3-18x44mm scope. This is a weight that he finds an acceptable compromise between portability and shootability.
O’Connor never hunted with more than a fixed 4X, and that’s what I started with, before variables were perfected. With a Leupold VX6 3-18x44mm, my Jarrett Ridge Walker weighs an acceptable 8.5 pounds, and this is the largest (thus heaviest) scope I’m likely to use on a mountain rifle. Again, because I’m unlikely to shoot at extreme range. And, unlike much hunting, maximum low-light capability isn’t critical. At dawn, you’re climbing. At dusk, you need to get off the mountain safely, better try again tomorrow. Thirty years ago, I was delighted with a 3-9X scope with one-inch tube. Honestly, I still don’t need anything bigger (or heavier). Even so, I’ve been spoiled by magnification and today’s great optics. I’m using larger scopes with dial-up turrets. With limits. On what I’m willing to carry and on shots I’m willing to take.
One must have confidence in a mountain rifle, and nothing begets confidence more than accuracy. The author’s Jarrett .300 Win Mag produces half-MOA groups with Hornady 200-grain ELD-X. This is the rifle he has used on his most recent mountain hunts.

Craig Boddington

 Craig Boddington is a retired US Marine Colonel and career outdoor journalist. He is the author of 31 books and more than 5000 articles on hunting, shooting, and conservation, with hundreds of appearances in films, outdoor television, and speaking engagements. Boddington's hunting experience spans six continents and 60 countries; his honors include the Weatherby Hunting and Conservation Award and Conklin Award. He and his wife Donna have three children and five grandchildren and divide their time between the California Central Coast and a small farm in his native Kansas that has lots of whitetails and never enough turkeys. He is most easily reached at www.craigboddington.com.
