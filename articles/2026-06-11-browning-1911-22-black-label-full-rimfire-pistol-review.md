Browning 1911-22 Black Label: Full Rimfire Pistol Review

Performance testing a premium 1911-22. Is it worth the price?

 Browning makes shooting a whole lot of fun with its newest version of the scaled-down 1911-22 .22 LR Model 1911-style pistol. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

June 11, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

To a lot of shooters—including me—there’s nothing more fun than spending time shooting a 
favorite .22 rimfire gun
. I have a bunch of old .22s that are just plain fun to shoot, and I’ve just spent several hours with Browning’s newest version of the Black Label 1911-22. I have to tell you it’s one fun gun, maybe even the most fun gun I’ve reviewed in a long time.
This new 1911-22 is the Black Label 1911-22 Gray with Rail. It’s part of Browning’s successful Black Label line, which also includes knives, tomahawks, flashlights, earmuffs, packs and bags, and tactical clothes. The pistol I reviewed for this report is just one of six new 1911-22s. The other five are the Black Label 1911-22 Medallion, Black Label 1911-22 Medallion Compact, Black Label 1911-22 Gray, Black Label 1911-22 Gray Compact, and Black Label 1911-22 Gray Compact with Rail. The Compact pistols have 3.63-inch barrels, and the “full-size” guns have 4.25-inch barrels. Browning also has a family of Black Label 1911-380 pistols that are chambered for the centerfire .380 ACP cartridge.
Browning’s scaled-down .22 LR Black Label 1911-22 Gray is about 85 percent the size of a standard centerfire Model 1911. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Features
The Browning 1911-22 was introduced in 2011 in celebration of the 100
th
 anniversary of the venerable Model 1911 and quickly became a standard catalog item. It’s scaled down from the original Model 1911 as John M. Browning designed it, so the 1911-22 is roughly 85 percent the size of a standard centerfire Model 1911.
The new Black Label 1911-22 Gray with Rail that I’ve been shooting has a machined aluminum slide that wears a gray anodized finish. It has serrations at the rear only. The pistol’s frame is a high-strength composite with a machined 7075 aluminum subframe and slide rails, and as its name indicates, it is complete with an integral 1913 Picatinny accessories rail that has three slots. The frontstrap is checkered and so are the mainspring housing and the grip safety’s memory bump. The trigger guard is square.

This gun is 0.78 inch thick at the rear of the slide, 1.12 inches thick at the base of the grip frame (including the grip panels), 1.19 inches wide outside the ambidextrous thumb safety levers, 7.5 inches long, and 4.5 inches high. Grip circumference is 4.5 inches at the top of the mainspring housing. (By the way, a standard centerfire Model 1911 has a grip circumference of 5.25 inches.) Browning lists the pistol’s weight at 11.4 ounces, but according to my digital scale, with an empty magazine inserted into the pistol, it weighs 14.7 ounces. That’s about 2.5 times less than the weight of a regular Model 1911.
Everything about the Black Label 1911-22 Gray with Rail in terms of handling characteristics is classic Model 1911. The beavertail grip safety, the extended ambidextrous thumb safety, the slide stop, and the magazine release button are pure Model 1911. All controls are in the usual Model 1911 locations, and they all operate just like on a centerfire Model 1911. The beavertail grip safety is alloy, whereas the slide stop, thumb safety levers, and magazine release button are steel.
Additionally, the pistol’s firing mechanism has a magazine disconnect safety. That means it will not fire with the magazine out.

The sights are metal, dovetailed, and drift adjustable. They feature red/orange and green fiber-optic inserts. The extended ambidextrous thumb safety, beavertail grip safety, slide stop, and magazine release operate the same as those of a centerfire Model 1911. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The pistol’s dovetailed steel sights are drift-adjustable fiber-optic style, with the rear sight having two red/orange inserts and the front sight having one green insert. The inserts look like bright-colored dots and are highly visible and extremely easy to acquire. Sight radius is 5.13 inches.
As is the norm these days, the hammer is skeletonized and so is the trigger. The ventilated trigger is alloy, and trigger pull averaged 6 pounds, 4 ounces as measured by my RCBS trigger pull scale. That’s on the heavy side, and it had a bit of take-up, but it broke crisply, and as you can see from the shooting results chart on page 44, the heavy trigger didn’t hinder the pistol’s accuracy. Browning warns against dry-firing the 1911-22, so I used a Pachmayr .22 LR snap cap when I measured the pistol’s trigger pull.
The grip panels are black and gray laminate. They are textured and bear the Browning logo, and they are attached to the pistol with black hex-head screws.
Performance Testing
I shot five, five-round groups of 10 different .22 LR loads from a sandbag benchrest at 25 yards, and my best individual five-shot group with the little 1911 was slightly less than 1.00 inch. Accuracy averages for five, five-shot groups with the 10 different loads test-fired ranged from 1.74 to 4.52 inches. Overall average accuracy was 2.69 inches.
I ran more than 350 rounds through the little 1911-22 over four hours, and I didn’t have a single malfunction. I didn’t do any maintenance—not even a wipe down or a quick cleaning of the internals. After all the shooting was done, I disassembled the 1911-22 and gave it a thorough inspection. It was easily disassembled and reassembled—much like a centerfire Model 1911. Bear in mind that a standard .45 barrel bushing wrench won’t fit the 1911-22’s barrel bushing because it’s scaled down in size. I didn’t need a bushing wrench for my Black Label 1911-22 Gray with Rail because the barrel bushing turned easily using just my fingers.

The scaled-down .22 rimfire Black Label 1911-22 Gray with Rail disassembles just like the classic centerfire Model 1911. The magazine is pressed metal and holds 10 rounds. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
To disassemble, after making sure the pistol is unloaded, remove the magazine. Put the thumb safety in the “Off” position. Retract the slide and visually check the chamber to be certain there is no cartridge. Then move the slide forward. Rest the rear of the pistol on a firm, nonslip surface. Press the recoil spring plug down into the slide and rotate the barrel bushing clockwise 90 degrees. Carefully release the recoil spring tension and remove the recoil spring plug. Move the slide rearward until the disassembly recess aligns with the slide stop. Press the protruding part of the slide stop through the right side of the frame, grasp the slide stop from the left side, and pull it out of the frame. Push the slide forward, off the frame. Remove the recoil spring through the front of the slide and lift the recoil spring guide rod from the barrel block. Rotate the barrel bushing counterclockwise until the barrel bushing lugs align with the opening in the front of the slide (about 45 degrees) and remove the barrel bushing. Slide the barrel out through the front of the slide. That’s as far as you need to go for routine maintenance. Reassemble in reverse order.
Summary
You really have to get one of these little pistols in your hands to fully appreciate it. Don’t let the fact that it’s scaled down prevent you from giving it a try. I fully admit that before I handled and fired my first Browning 1911-22 back in 2011, I was skeptical because I like full-size centerfire 1911s so much. But I also admit that upon firing the very first shot those seven years ago I was hooked. It’s a cool little 1911, and in my opinion, the new Black Label 1911-22 Gray with Rail is even cooler than the earlier model.
