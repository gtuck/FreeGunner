The Wilson Combat WCP365 2.0: A High-End Reimaging of the P365

Wilson Combat takes the ultra-popular SIG P365 to all-new heights.

 The WCP365 2.0 is a cutting-edge, capable defensive pistol that will never let you down. (J. Scott Rupp photo)

May 19, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

The SIG Sauer P365 is the most popular small 9mm carry semiauto in the country. It is the pistol that started the micro compact trend in the first place, and despite a glut of competitors, it remains the king of the hill. It has everything going for it: size, ergonomics, capacity and shootability. But Wilson Combat thought it could be better.
The WCP365 2.0 from Wilson Combat is a high-end reimagining of the SIG with every practical and aesthetic bell and whistle you could think of—all courtesy of the artisans working at Wilson’s Berryville, Arkansas, facility.
My sample is built around the base P365. If you like the larger, higher-capacity iterations of the P365, Wilson offers X, XL and two different Macro versions—plus a super high-end model with a Radian Afternburner+Ramjet barrel/compensator setup.
I chose the standard WCP365 2.0 model because I’ve owned and carried the original P365 since that gun made its debut, and I wanted to be able to compare the two. Like my SIG, the Wilson has a 3.1-inch barrel, but here it’s treated to a Diamond-Like Carbon finish over layers of chromium and tungsten for the ultimate in corrosion and abrasion protection.

Features
Wilson also upgraded the metal finish on the slide with the company’s proprietary Armor-Tuff treatment. It provides low-reflective, high-lubricity metal protection and is applied by Wilson technicians after the metal surfaces are sandblasted, dehorned and Parkerized. It produces a finish that can withstand 3,000 hours of salt-spray bath without corroding.
It’s said that looks aren’t everything, but they count for a lot, and the slide on the WCP365 2.0 is striking as well as functional. Up front, a trio of deep, aggressive serrations on the sides is paired with a 0.35-inch ridged top that’s flanked on either side by two inch-long lightening cuts.
The slide bevels are much heavier than on the stock SIG slide, and the rear serrations are more aggressive as well. They consist of a row of five angled cuts. They’re machined at a rearward angle—running in the opposite direction of those on the stock SIG slide—and they’re deeper as well.

“Wilson Combat engineers have redesigned the WCP365 slide with multi-pattern front and rear cocking serrations, slide top serrations, heavy slide bevels and lightening cuts for improved manipulation under stress,” said Wilson’s Tressa Joubert.
Whether you’re racking the slide or doing a press check, you have plenty to grab onto, and the beveling makes holstering super smooth. Wilson’s design also results in a slide that weighs 7.9 ounces, 13 percent lighter than my stock SIG slide. The lighter weight makes for faster cycling, speeding follow-up shots, and can be a benefit when you’re running a red dot by offsetting the added mass of an optic.
The left side of the slide is embellished with “Wilson Combat” inside a border, centered roughly above the takedown lever, with “SIG Sauer P365” above that on the slide’s side bevel. The Wilson Combat “eagle C” logo is engraved on the right side of the slide, just behind the front serrations.
The serial number has a special WCX prefix, which you can see via a cutout in the frame module below the external extractor.
The iron sights include the company’s plain black serrated Battlesight rear paired with a red fiber-optic front whose rod sits in a sturdy blade that’s serrated at the back.

If you decide to stick with the irons, the steel slide cover plate boasts the Wilson Combat logo. But of course these days few people shoot iron sights. The slide is milled for a direct optic mount on your choice of Trijicon RMRcc, Holosun K or Shield RMSc footprints.
The grip module’s starburst pattern and texturing on the frontstrap and backstrap provide excellent control. The carry cut at the base is a big help in concealment. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Grip Module
The other showstopping element of the WCP365 2.0 is the Wilson Combat grip module. The company sells the module as an aftermarket unit in several different configurations, and the one on this particular pistol is the basic WCP365 model. Its accessory rail has the SIG dimensions as opposed to Picatinny, and there are plenty of lights and lasers available specifically for this rail type.
When I say the module is “basic,” don’t take that to mean it’s run of the mill. Far from it. The Wilson grip sports a striking sunburst pattern like what the company uses on some of its high-end 1911s, as well as on its EDC X9 double-stack. On the WCP365 2.0, the sunburst on the sides of the grip is paired with panels of stippling on the frontstrap and backstrap.
The combination is light years more aggressive than the stock SIG, and the control it provides is enhanced by an extended beavertail. While the SIG frame is straight on the sides, the Wilson module has very slight palm swells on either side—boosting grip width by 0.2 inch in the midsection. It’s not a fat grip by any means, though. It’s relieved at the top for good thumb placement and an easy reach to the magazine release, and it tapers gently at the bottom.
Also notable on the Wilson module is a carry cut at the base of the grip. The stock SIG grip has one as well, but the Wilson cut is nearly twice as big. It’s a huge help in concealment by allowing clothing to slide easily off this corner and reduce printing.
The takedown lever, magazine release and slide-lock lever are standard SIG parts. The trigger on the action-tuned model, which I tested, is decidedly not. It’s a flat-face style from Grayguns, and it’s machined from bar stock. It has an adjustment screw that allows you to eliminate overtravel, and it breaks at 90 degrees.
While I didn’t find it significantly crisper or having a snappier reset than my stock P365, it is a good deal lighter at three pounds, six ounces. Because it’s not curved like the original, the feel is completely different, and the 90-degree break results in a better, straight-to-the-rear press, which improves accuracy.
The front of the slide sports deep serrations, plus lightening cuts on either side of a ridged slide top. The slide’s beveling is custom as well, and it’s heavier than you’ll find on the comparable stock SIG. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Range Time
For testing I mounted a Trijicon RMRcc, which is the compact brother to the RMR, and the WCP365 2.0 performed well from the bench at 15 yards. As you can see in the accompanying chart, it loved 
Hornady
 Critical Defense in particular.

Advertisement

There was a single failure to feed with the Lehigh Defense low-recoil load, but that shouldn’t come as a shock since we’re talking about less than full-power ammo out of a new gun that had no prior break-in save for the function testing that was conducted before the pistol left Wilson Combat.
Practical shooting entailed a mix of Bill and X drills, and I shot the Wilson side by side against my own SIG. For those who might need a quick refresher, the Bill Drill entails firing six shots at the center of an IDPA target as fast as you can go. The X Drill focuses on target transitions, drawing and firing at the head box of target A, then the body of target B, followed by the body of target A and head box of target B. It’s tougher than it sounds, especially with a micro compact.
Man, the Wilson folks know what they’re doing. The WCP365 2.0 was a big step up in control over my stock pistol. Typically I’m faster with irons than I am with a red dot, but I didn’t find that to be the case here.
For one thing, while the RMRcc has a relatively tall deck, thanks to the direct-mount setup it sat nice and close to the bore, enabling me to pick it up more quickly. That was especially true on the draw, which is often a problem for me, and also on target transitions.
Further, the sights co-witness and were visible in the lower fifth of the RMRcc’s window. Not to belabor the point, but to my way of thinking, if you’re going to shoot a red dot on your defensive pistol, you want co-witness irons as a backup.

Concealed Carry
The gun comes with two 10-round magazines, one with a flat base and one with a finger hook. The latter allowed me to get a full three-finger grip on the pistol. The WCP365 2.0 also accepted the extended 12-rounder I carry as a backup for my stock SIG, and that gives you a true full-size grip. Wilson doesn’t offer this particular magazine, but SIG does.
As you might expect, the WCP365 2.0 carries like a dream, with one caveat. The grip texturing that provides such excellent control may, depending on your body type, chafe if you’re not wearing the right clothing.
It was winter when I worked with the gun, and on the first day of carrying it—behind the hip in an inside-the-waistband holster—I was wearing a very thin base layer under a wool shirt. The grip did eventually irritate my love handle, so the next day I switched to a heavier base-layer fabric. That did the trick, and a standard-weight T-shirt or similar should prevent any discomfort.
The WCP365 2.0 disappears easily under a cover garment, thanks in no small part to the carry cut. With sight aboard the gun weighs 18.7 ounces, so it’s certainly not going to weigh you down.
Is it Worth the Jump?
Now for the $64,000 question. Should you trade in your stock P365 for the Wilson WCP365 2.0? The Wilson definitely has a lot to offer, with huge improvements over the factory SIG in the trigger, grip and slide departments. And if you have an older P365 like mine, which is not cut for optics, the Wilson gives you the option of mounting a red dot.

Advertisement

But then again, SIG’s own broad array of P365s includes models with improved grip textures, straight triggers, optic-ready slides and other features. What you don’t get, though, is Wilson Combat’s attention to the details that matter.
“Each of these options were developed to enhance ergonomics and recoil control. Improvements in shape, texture and feel result in a grip that mimics a fully hand-sculpted custom firearm,” Joubert said.
So to return to the question at hand. If you have a 
factory SIG P365
 you shoot well and are happy with, you might not find the cash outlay for a Wilson to be worth it. However, if you love the platform but have been thinking about stepping up a level, the WCP365 2.0 is worth the investment.
If you’re in the market for a new micro-compact carry gun and want the best you can get, you will not regret springing for Wilson’s version instead of the stock factory gun. The WCP365 2.0 is a cutting-edge, capable defensive pistol that will never let you down. Further, you’ll own a pistol that’s a cut above what everybody else shoots and carries. For the discriminating handgunner, that’s no small thing.
