Savage A22 Precision .22 LR Review: Truly Competition Ready?

Has Savage built the ultimate "easy button" for jumping into competitive rimfire shooting? 

 Savage’s A22 precision is a great choice for shooters looking to jump into today’s rimfire sports. (J. Scott Rupp photo)

November 11, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

It’s almost a cliché to write about Savage’s well-deserved reputation of providing excel-lent accuracy at an affordable price. I own a Model 116 in .300 Win. Mag., and it shoots great. I’ve tested dozens of other Savage rifles over the years, and with only one or two exceptions, they’ve always delivered on their top-notch accuracy reputation.
At the same time, I don’t think all that many people equate “Savage” with “innovation”—despite the fact that it developed the gold-standard Accu-Trigger and the excellent AccuStock and AccuFit stock concepts.
At last year’s big firearms trade show, a number of editors and writers got a tour of the Savage booth by none other than its new president, Al Kasper. He leads a group of investors who bought Savage from former owner Vista Outdoor. Kasper said we’d see a more innovative, more forward-thinking Savage now that the company was free of its corporate chains. Proof of this is found in guns like the new A22 Precision.
Savage is hardly new to .22 semi-auto rifles. It developed arguably what was the first successful such gun with its Automatic Rifle Model 1912, introduced in that year. Semiauto rim-fires have been part of the company’s lineup for most of its history, and the parent of the A22 Precision, the A22, made its debut in 2016.

Like other members of the family, the A22 Precision features the AccuTrigger. The zero m.o.a. rail is currently being changed to 20 m.o.a. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
The A22 Precision is a straight-blowback .22 fed from a 10-round rotary magazine. It’s chambered in .22 Long Rifle only, and it incorporates the aforementioned AccuTrigger. But similarities to the other members of the A22 family end there because the gun was specifically designed for people interested in the burgeoning long-range/practical rifle games, such as National Rifle League and Precision Rifle Series—both of which now have a rimfire option.
“We knew we could bring the same accuracy standard to our rimfire line as our centerfire line when we made the Precision Series of rifles,” said Jessica Treglia, Savage’s senior brand manager.
Defining Features
Right off the bat you notice the stock, a chassis from Modular Driven Technologies. Savage worked closely with MDT on the design of this chassis, and while it doesn’t provide as many features as MDT’s other offerings, I was impressed by its utility and simplicity.

It is machined from a single billet of aluminum and weighs three pounds. The butt portion has an adjustable comb, and two small thumbscrews on the right side allow the cheekpiece to slide up and down on twin rods. Length of pull can be adjusted by removing or adding 1/4-inch spacers, and the buttpad is a cushy, slightly curved piece that stays in the shoulder nicely. The vertical pistol grip has a rubber outer, and it has palm swells on either side.
The fore-end has M-Lok slots at three, six and nine o’clock. There’s a sling swivel stud at the front for mounting a bipod, or you can bolt on an M-Lok rail section for a bipod.
The A22 Precision’s barrel is 18 inches long with a relatively heavy contour. Diameter is 0.92 inch in front of the smooth barrel nut tapering to 0.77 inch just behind the threaded portion of the muzzle. Thread pattern is 1/2x28, and a knurled thread cap is provided.
The oversize charging handle makes it easy to work the action, and the fact that it’s red adds a bit of visual zing. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
The action sports an oversize charging handle. Here the company decided to add a bit of zing, finishing the handle in “Savage red.” It looks great, and it’s a handy feature to have.
A Picatinny rail is bolted to the top of the receiver. My sample had a zero m.o.a. rail, but Treglia told me the company was currently in the process of switching to a 20-m.o.a. rail. That makes sense because when I shot the rifle at 100 yards, I wasn’t too awful far away from running out of elevation, and I might’ve been out of luck at 200 yards—and 200-yard rimfire shooting is actually a thing these days.

The action does not lock back on an empty magazine, but there’s a bolt-lock lever on the underside of the rifle. It’s on the small side, but I found it easy to operate. It might be tricky with heavy gloves on if you’re shooting a match in the winter, but otherwise it’s fine.
As mentioned, the A22 Precision feeds from a 10-round rotary magazine, which fits flush and has an integral spring-loaded latch release at the front. I initially had problems with it because it didn’t drop free from its well as smoothly as I would’ve liked. It kept wanting to hang up, and I was going to gig the gun on this point.
Disassembly and Reassembly
But after disassembling and reassembling the rifle, the mag started working like it should, dropping free and easy. I guess it’s like electronics. Having problems? Unplug, wait 30 seconds and plug back in. Voilà, it works.
You need to partially disassemble the rifle in order to remove the barreled action from the chassis. After ensuring the rifle is unloaded, with the bolt forward and safety on, slide a small punch through the hole in the back of the recoil assembly’s polymer dust cover. Push in on the recoil assembly tail and lift up to remove the cover, revealing the rear action screw.
Further disassembly to clean the action or to run a rod through the bore is easy. Push in on the recoil assembly—it’s under tension, so be careful—and remove it. At this point the charging handle will pull right out. Savage’s instructions say the bolt will then simply fall out if you tilt the rifle. That didn’t happen for me, but by pushing down the hammer I was able to slide the bolt to the rear and withdraw it.
The trigger group is a cinch to remove by driving out a pin at the back and sliding the polymer-housed trigger assembly forward and out. Note it’s not necessary to remove the AccuTrigger in order to adjust the pull weight. This is easily accomplished by inserting the provided tool through a hole in the back of the trigger guard to engage the trigger return spring.
Reassemble in reverse order. Again, I found I had to push the bolt down slightly against the hammer, then it slid in easily enough. The instructions don’t show the final position of the recoil assembly very well, although it’s not hard to figure out how it’s sup-posed to fit. Take extra care to install the dust cover correctly, as Savage cautions if it’s not done right, it could fly off during firing and cause injury.
The MDT chassis is easy to adjust for comb height via twin thumbscrews. Length of pull is adjustable by adding or subtracting 1/4-inch spacers. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Accuracy Testing
For initial accuracy testing, I mounted a Vortex Diamondback 2-7x35mm with a V-Plex reticle. As you can see in the accompanying accuracy chart, it shot like you expect a Savage to shoot: very well. I concentrated on standard-velocity, target-type loads, but I also included good ol’ CCI Mini Mag, and even this speedy load shot well in the A22 Precision.

A while later I switched to a Bushnell Match Pro 6-24X scope for some 100-yard testing. I also screwed an M-Lok rail section into the fore-end, just behind the sling swivel stud, and installed a Leapers UTG bipod for the 100-yard session.
With the Bushnell cranked up to 20X, accuracy was pretty darned good at the longer distance as well. The average of four five-shot groups at 100 yards is shown in the chart. Thing is, if I threw out the single flyer I had in each 100-yard group, the average shrank to 0.82 inch.
NRL22 Competition Ready
As mentioned, this gun was intend-ed as a competition rifle. PRS doesn’t have established courses of fire, but NRL22 does, so let’s take a look at how the Savage’s accuracy level might fare on the league’s published September 2020 course of fire.
One stage entails shooting at targets from 50, 75 and 100 yards, with respective target sizes of one, 1.5 and two inches. The A22 Precision easily aces this one.
The polymer 10-round rotary magazine fits flush and has an integral latch at the front. With the exception of one load, the rifle fed flawlessly. (J. Scott Rupp photo)
Another stage has a Know Your Limit target rack at 54 yards. I’m not going to give a dissertation on how KYL works, but the rack features target diameters of one, 3/4, 1/2 and ¼ inch. Based on the 50-yard accuracy results I got, the A22 Precision might struggle a little bit on the smallest tar-gets, but it’s worth noting I did all the 50-yard accuracy testing with a scope capable of only 7X at the high end.

Advertisement

When I later got the Bushnell mounted and was able to get a lot more magnification, groups shrank. In fact, I shot a pair of 0.35-inch, five-shot groups with the SK Flatnose load at 50 yards before I moved out to 100, so I do think the 1/4-inch target would be achievable.
The third NRL22 stage involves shooting from a supported position (literally over a barrel, a 55-gallon drum) and from a simulated rooftop. The target is two inches in diameter and is 84 yards away. Easy-peasy, at least in terms of the rifle being able to get the job done. Another barricade-oriented stage uses similar targets at similar distances.
The last stage of note involves firing from standing, kneeling, sitting and unsupported prone (no bipod legs on the ground). Shooters engage a five-inch target at 73 yards from standing and kneeling and a four-inch target from sitting and prone.
As part of my testing, I shot the A22 Precision at 50 yards from standing, sitting and kneeling. It didn’t go all that well with the bipod still attached—too much weight out front for me. But, boy, once I took off the bipod the rifle really pounded the center of a 5.5-inch bullseye target from all three positions when I did my part. So, I would imagine the A22 Precision would acquit itself well on this stage, too.
Function was excellent, with the exception of the Winchester Wildcat load I used to get the Bushnell scope on paper. It didn’t like feeding those for some reason, but everything else ran through the gun just fine.

The AccuTrigger was its usual excellent self. Pull weight from the factory averaged four pounds, and you certainly could argue I should’ve adjusted it down to under three pounds (pull-weight minimum is 2.5 pounds) and maybe wrung out just a tad more accuracy. But I tested the rifle before I weighed the trigger, and I never felt as though the pull weight as it came from the factory was inhibiting how the rifle performed. The comb height is easy to adjust and stays where you put it. The AR-style pistol grip fills the hand nicely, and it’s easy to maintain hand position thanks to the rubber covering.
Final Thoughts
If you’re looking for more capability out of a chassis, MDT will sell you its LSS-RF Gen 2 chassis for the A22—for $400. As is, the A22 Precision certainly delivers the goods, at a suggested retail price of only $600.
That is an excellent deal, especially for someone who is searching for an entry point into this brave new world of rimfire shooting. The Savage is more than capable of allowing someone to be competitive right away in Base (NLR22) or Production (PRS) divisions—classifications that are based on the combined cost of rifle and scope.
If you end up really getting into it and want to compete in Open division later on, then by all means shell out the thousands of dollars it takes to be competitive in that rarefied company. But you gotta start somewhere, and I can’t think of a better place than the A22 Precision.
