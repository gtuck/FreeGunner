Taurus RPC 9mm PDW: Full Review

Taurus' RPC is a PDW with All-­New DNA. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

September 25, 2026

By James Tarr

Line extensions, or variant models of existing guns, are commonplace, which is why new gun introductions get everyone’s attention. When Taurus first told Guns & Ammo about its secret RPC in July 2025, we took notice. The RPC is a completely new firearm design for Taurus, a pistol developed to handle like a 9mm submachine gun and imported into the U.S. as a semiautomatic. It can be configured with or without a side-­folding brace. (G&A’s test sample included a Strike Industries FSA brace.) Even with the brace attached, the package is small, light and possesses many modern features.
Roller-Delayed Details
The RPC is currently available in 9mm, and it is fed by 32-­round magazines. There are 10-­rounders for restricted jurisdictions, and 20-round mags coming that are lower profile and carry better in bags. 
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
Inside, the RPC is a roller-­delayed blowback operating system, a description that conjures thoughts of 
the HK SP5K
 and 
Springfield Armory Kuna with Strike Ind. FSA brace
. The Taurus RPC was designed around a stainless-­steel chassis trapped in the polymer lower receiver. The chassis is the serialized part. 
Within the lower is an AR-­style trigger system, although none of the parts are interchangeable with an AR. The single-stage trigger features the trendy flat-face shoe, and 7 1/4 pounds of pressure will fire the RPC when the selector is thumbed down to the single-bullet pictogram.

The 32-round magazine is standard capacity for the RPC, though 10-rounders are available. Low-profile 20-round mags are coming soon. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Up top is a quick-­change barrel subassembly that floats within the handguard. Using only a 5mm Hex key, the 4 1/2-inch threaded barrel can be removed. The barrel is provided with a thread protector that can be removed to reveal 1/2x28 threads.We used those threads to attach a 
Banish TAC 9K TI
. The quick-change barrel likely has more utility for military or law enforcement in a select-fire configuration, but it is neat nonetheless. Until Taurus (or the aftermarket) starts offering replacement barrels, none of us will be using it for more than a party trick.
There is also a small built-­in handstop at the bottom of the handguard, and you’ll appreciate it. This gun begs for a vertical foregrip, but unfortunately we can’t legally put one on a pistol. You’d have to first register the RPC as a short-­barrel rifle (SBR).
For lefties or those who are ambidextrous, there’s AR-­ergonomic bilateral controls, a reversible charging handle, AR-­pattern grip and 70-­degree selector lever. The reversible, non-­reciprocating charging handle is a steel piece with plastic overmolding. It’s sized and shaped well for intuitive manipulation within the upper.

The muzzle is threaded 1/2x28, and the RPC is well-suited for suppressed shooting. Taurus provides a thread protector with the RPC. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The upper receiver is shaped by machining extruded aluminum, and the lower receiver is molded polymer. Inside the lower, however, is a stainless-­steel chassis that contains the fire control system. 
Also machined from aluminum is the short handguard. It has M-Lok slots on the sides and underneath, and vents up top. When the upper is separated from the lower receiver, just pull the handguard off. No tools are required. The upper and lower receiver are connected like similar PDW types with two captive push-­pins.

Advertisement

Atop the gun is 11 inches of rail space for mounting an optic. We used the opportunity to pair it with the new 
Leupold LCO Pro F2
. At $900, the optic matched the price of the basic RPC lacking the optional brace. 
At the back of G&A’s sample is 
Strike Industries’ FSA brace
. I am a fan. The blade portion of the brace is solid polymer. The connecting arm is machined aluminum, and the hinge and lockup are steel-­on-­steel. This brace was designed for strength. When folded, it locks into place. These braces typically retail for $170. 
The charging handle is easy to grasp and is non-reciprocating, meaning it doesn't move during operation. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
With the brace folded, the RPC measures just 13 inches. Unfolded, the brace extends length to 20.9 inches. It weighs 4 pounds, 9 1/2 ounces, with an empty magazine in place, and the version with no brace is three-quarters-of-an-inch shorter and half-pound lighter.

Springfield Armory’s Kuna, introduced early in 2025, uses a simple roller-­delayed blowback operating mechanism. The RPC, on the other hand, uses a bolt assembly that must have been inspired by the HK MP5. It’s not an exact copy, but the bolt’s rollers look similar, and the locking piece inside the bolt appears as if it came straight out of an MP5, although the dimensions are slightly different.
The RPC has AR-style bilateral steel magazine releases and bolt catches that are easy to reach and manipulate. The bilateral polymer selector has short levers, and between that and the 70-­degree throw, the right-­side lever won’t poke you in the trigger finger. 
There are steel quick-­detach (QD) sling-­swivel sockets at the rear of the lower receiver, on both sides, and another one at the back underneath the brace. 
The RPC can be fired with the FSA brace in its folded position. Collapsed, a braced RPC measures less than 13 inches. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The RPC uses an AR-­pattern grip, so if you don’t like the provided one, it’s easy to swap out.
The trigger is long and straight, with horizontal serrations. The trigger experience was, unfortunately, what most experience on a subgun aimed at minimally trained officers. It’s long and mushy and a bit heavy, and the serrated texture provides tactile feedback. 
Magazines are black polymer, topped with bright yellow followers. There is an over-­insertion tab, but no index holes. Even loaded to 32 rounds, it’s not too hard to seat an RPC magazine on a closed bolt.
Tuning The RPC
There’s always buzz in the industry about a new gun, and I know several writers, editors and content creators. Some have experienced this gun already, and the comments fell into two camps:
1. It’s a fun gun, low recoiling and reliable, or

Advertisement

2. It’s unreliable and jams all the time.
Those are polar opposite experiences that could indicate erratic quality control (QC) on the part of Taurus, either with the gun or the magazines — or not. 
RPC models with the FSA brace command an additional $159. Other vertical rail-mounted braces can be attached to the RPC. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The RPC was originally developed as a select-­fire submachine gun for a Brazilian police contract. It has been sold as a submachine gun (SMG) internationally, meaning it was built for reliability with NATO’s 9x19mm ammunition. Those rounds use full-metal-jacket (FMJ) bullets and are loaded a bit hot, some bordering +P pressures. Guns like this may not be reliable with certain hollowpoints, depending on the geometry of the nose. Then there’s the power of the ammunition.
The bolt with roller system delays unlocking. Inside the bolt assembly is a locking piece, and that piece is what determines the speed at which the bolt unlocks from the chamber. The one installed in the RPC is a 110-­degree locking piece. Taurus also includes an 85-­degree locking piece with its gun. According to the owner’s manual, the 110-­degree locking piece should be used when firing the RPC unsuppressed with standard ammunition. It should be replaced by the 85-­degree locking piece when firing suppressed or when using subsonic ammunition. I don’t know if those in my social media feeds were aware of this, or if they swapped out locking pieces when they had issues. I have a lot of experience with locking pieces, and there are different ones for HK MP5 bolts intended for specific uses. The general rule of thumb is this: Don’t change the locking piece until you need to change the locking piece.
Two locking pieces are provided with the roller-delayed bolt. A 85- and 110-degree part are optimized for different ammunition types. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Extra Testing
Though I was under a bit of a time constraint, I felt obligated to put 150 rounds of CCI Blazer 115-­grain FMJ through the RPC, then 50 rounds of Blazer subsonic 147-­grain FMJ with a small flat on the nose. I then put 75 rounds each of 
Hornady
 Critical Defense 115-­grain FTX, Black Hills’ 115-­grain TAC-­XP +P, Hornady American Gunner 115-­grain JHP, Hornady Handgun Hunter 115-­grain +P Monoflex JHP, Fiocchi CovertX 124-­grain JHP, Black Hills 124-­grain JHP, Black Hills 124-­grain JHP +P, Hornady Critical Duty 135-­grain +P, and Hornady Subsonic 147-­grain XTP. That’s almost an entire case of ammo, mostly hollowpoints. The only problem I had was when hand racking a Black Hills TAC-­XP +P round. That bullet has a big cavity and it jammed. When I locked the bolt open and then hit the bolt release to chamber a round, the next cartridge chambered. The rest of that mag fired without incident. Both subsonic loads cycled without issue, too, using the standard 110-­degree locking piece.

The accuracy of the RPC was exactly what I expected, and the reliability was better than I was led to believe. Beyond the one jam (which I might have induced), the RPC fed everything I listed. Admittedly, I tested for a sample size of one, but G&A’s sample worked great. 
Controls on the Taurus RPC are bilateral, feature serrated touchpoints and made of steel. Placement is similar to AR-style firearms. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Empty magazines locked the bolt back every time. I never lubed the gun, and during testing it got both hot and dirty but kept running. There was a little excitement, but nothing that was the gun’s fault. While at an indoor range, an ejected case bounced off the wall and dropped between my eyepro and my face. It burned my cheek just under my eyelid. It’s not the first time I’ve had a case drop inside my glasses, but it was the first time I got burned. Don’t panic. Simply look down. The case will either fall out, or fall against the lens of your glasses, away from your eyes and skin.
Attachments and Build
Beyond the LCO, I used a Burris Fastfire-­E sight in the lower-third on a Midwest Industries MK2 QD mount. The enclosed FastFire E features Acro footprint and has a 3.5-­MOA dot, great battery life, clear glass and a price that pairs nicely with the RPC.
The braced version of the RPC is priced $159 more than the non-­braced version. As mentioned, the retail of the Strike Industries FSA brace is $170, so Taurus is saving us $11 and mounting it for free. 
The trigger is flat-faced, but with subtle serrations. Though similar to AR-style drop-in triggers, AR components cannot be installed. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
In my opinion, a review of the Taurus RPC wouldn’t be complete without comparing it to the closest competitor, Springfield Armory’s Kuna in 9mm. The RPC is a little shorter and lighter, but not by a lot. Both use a roller-­delayed blowback operating system. Both guns have bilateral controls and are sold with Strike Industries braces and accept AR-­pattern grips. The price of the Kuna has increased significantly since its 2025 introduction, and the version with the brace is now $230 more than the RPC, but for that money you get great integral sights and a better trigger pull. In terms of felt recoil between the them,  — and I shot them side-­by-­side — they were very similar. The biggest difference between them, to me, was in appearance. Here it is subjective: The front end of the RPC seems a little too short to me. The Kuna’s clear magazines look really cool, as well, whereas the RPC’s magazines look like something from ProMag, which produces a negative knee-­jerk reaction that they didn’t earn during this evaluation. Both guns, in my testing, were equally reliable. With the Taurus RPC, you now have one more option in the large-format handgun or “PDW” category. Its unexpected performance won me over.
