## Article Text (extracted)

Is the H&R Retro M16A1 the Most Authentic Colt M16 Clone?

A deep-dive look at Harrington & Richardson's passion for historical accuracy.

 H&R’s M16A1 retro rifle kept all of the original markings - except the location. (Logan Metesh photo)

By Logan Metesh

A decade ago, the idea of mass-market M16 clones seemed silly to some. Today, they’re everywhere. While Colt is certainly the most notable manufacturer of the M16, another company that’s almost as old is churning out clone-correct retro M16A1 rifles: Harrington & Richardson. But with a bit of a twist.
History of Harrington & Richardson’s M16A1
Originally based in Worcester, Massachusetts, the current Harrington & Richardson is based in West Columbia, South Carolina. If the location seems familiar, that’s because it should. The H&R brand was purchased by the same parent company that owns 
Palmetto State Armory
, through which the H&R M16A1 retro rifles are distributed.
The 
M16A1 retro rifles
 that they’re selling today are really good clone-correct versions of the guns that originally came out of the Worcester factory.
Harrington & Richardson got their first M16 contract in April 1968 for 240,000 rifles. Delivery of the guns began in December 1968 and ended in March 1971. The contract specified that the first guns should cost the government no more than $250 each for the first 60,000 rifles and no more than $180 each for the final 180,000. By comparison, Colt had their cost down to $104.39, but to be fair, they were already well established in making the M16. Adjusted for inflation, the costs are ten times more, or $2,500, $1,800 and $1,040.

Additional costs incurred by H&R were $1.24 per magazine as well as a lump sum of $4,500,000 and a 5.5% royalty due to Colt for their agreeing to no longer being the sole source manufacturer. Again factoring in inflation, that’s $45,000,000 for the rights and more than $12 per magazine.
All of the original rifles were assigned serial numbers in the 2,000,000 range and true to history, the new H&R M16A1 rifles are in the same number range. They even kept the historic H&R lion logo on the receiver.
Of course, they are easily recognizable as different from the originals because these retro rifles have an ‘HR’ prefix to the serial number, a different factory location, and of course the most unfortunate difference, no third hole.

The New H&R M16A1
NoDak Spud is one of the most recognizable names in the retro rifle game, and it plays a role in the H&R rifles. Like the H&R brand, NoDak Spud was also acquired by Palmetto State Armory’s parent company, and they made NoDak’s founder, Mike Wettleland, H&R’s CEO. To say the company was in good hands would be an understatement.
The finished result is a really nice looking rifle. Chambered in 5.56 NATO with a 20” chrome-lined pencil barrel, the H&R M16A1 upper receiver is made from NoDak Spud’s proprietary dies, and it has all of the iconic cosmetic features that denote an M16A1. The muzzle device, buttstock, front sight, carry handle, teardrop forward assist, and the triangular handguards with the heat shields are all there – you know exactly what it is upon first glance.
The finish on the upper and lower receivers is even the correct gray color. Now, I would be remiss not to mention that some people have complained about the color of the small parts. On some guns, the parts are a glossier black and they clash terribly. On the gun I received, the parts are most certainly not glossy black, and they blend in with the rest of the rifle’s coloring. Not sure why there is a difference between some, but I thought that it was worth mentioning.
Attention to detail doesn’t end on the outside. Inside, there’s a full auto profile bolt carrier group and the barrel has a 1/12 twist just like the originals. When the M16 was adopted, a 1/12 twist was the compromise made by the military for proper stabilization of both the standard 55-grain ball and tracer ammo. It wasn’t until decades later that the switch was made to heavier ammo that the twist changed, too.
Harrington & Richardson also offers the same rifle but with a 1/7 twist for the modern shooter who uses a wider variety of bullet weights. I shot a version with the 1/7 twist in a limited capacity at a media event, but the rifle I tested more extensively was one that had a barrel featuring the original 1/12 twist.

Rounding out the complete package is the magazine. An H&R-branded floor plate adorns the metal 30-round magazine that comes with the rifle, but they also offer 20-round magazines in both the straight wall and waffle varieties. Personally, I don’t think a retro rifle looks right with a 30-rounder sticking out of the magwell, but to each their own.
H&R M16A1 Range Report
On the range, the rifle runs exactly like you’d expect for a full-length M16-style rifle. If your experience is limited to carbine-length guns with adjustable buttstocks, then there’s a little bit different feel to this gun given the extra 4” of barrel length and the fixed buttstock that brings the full rifle length to 30”.
Really, though, it’s not a big deal. If you’ve ever shot an AR, then you already know the manual of arms for this gun and won’t have a problem getting used to the H&R M16A1.
Between the media event and my test gun, I shot approximately 300 rounds through the two rifles. I didn’t have any issues with either gun, and I shot primarily 55-grain bullets with a couple boxes of 62-grain bullets mixed in. No double feeds, no failures to extract or eject, and no failures to feed.
H&R put the new rifles in the same historic 2,000,000 serial number range. (Logan Metesh photo)
Final Thoughts
Retro rifles like the Harrington & Richardson M16A1 have become popular, due in no small part to the M16’s design being more than 60 years old. That means many American veterans today fielded some form of that platform when in uniform and the nostalgia factor and desire to have a replica of what they were issued is strong.
If you’re in the market for an M16A1 clone, I’d recommend giving the H&R rifle a serious look. It’s well made, shoots well, looks good, and definitely scratches the retro itch. Are they exact copies? No. No company is making an exact copy, but this one is darn close.
