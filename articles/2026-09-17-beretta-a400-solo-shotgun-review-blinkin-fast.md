Beretta A400 Solo Shotgun Review: Blinkin' Fast 

Testing the Speed and Reliability of Beretta's Most Versatile Shotgun

 The test Solo was in versatile Realtree Max 7camo, one of six camouflage patterns, plus basic black. (Craig Boddington photo)

September 17, 2026

By Craig Boddington

No secret that Beretta is a great name in shotguns. Lots of great shotguns, for all seasons and reasons. One of my favorites is an A391 20-gauge, won when I was “top gun” in the 2008 Grand National Quail Hunt in Enid, Oklahoma. Yeah, I had a good day, didn’t win with a Beretta. I was shooting a Winchester Model 12 pump gun, factory Skeet WS1, had it since 1970. We’ll come back to that thought, because it’s important.
I shot the Beretta A391 at return engagements at the Grand National Quail Hunt, didn’t win again. My extra-good-days aren’t that frequent. I love that shotgun. It’s dropped lots of birds with it and my first turkey with a 20-gauge.
So, to Beretta’s newest shotgun, the A400 Solo. Solo means “one,” so it’s a basic gas-operated, semiauto shotgun, intended for folks who want one gun for sporting clays, upland game, and waterfowl. The shotgun comes supplied with butt and pistolgrip spacers, to change length and angle. Although cursed with a left-hand affliction, at 5’9”, I am Joe Average. Most factory stocks fit me well. The test gun is a 12-gauge with 28-inch barrel (plus .75-inch with Beretta’s Optimachoke HP extended choke tube), fits me perfectly. For a gun guy, being left-handed is a pain, but proper fit to standard factory dimensions is a huge blessing.
Shot Patterns
Technically, the test gun is the A400 Solo Synthetic in the versatile Realtree Max 7 camouflage, It’s just one of seven camo patterns, plus black. Starting at $1549 in basic black, the Solo is not an expensive shotgun, but it’s loaded with sensible features and new technology. Initially in 12-gauge only, 2.75 and/or 3-inch, the gas-operated Solo is supposed to digest everything from light target loads to the heaviest 3-inch loads.

This is a Modified pattern at 30 yards, fired with 1 1 ¼ ounces bismuth shot, dense and even, about 60 percent of the patter above point of aim with plenty of strikes on the center clay. (Craig Boddington photo)
That statement poses a challenge, so the first thing I did was dig through my 12-gauge loads. I had light one-ounce target loads, standard target loads, duck loads, and turkey loads in both lead shot and TSS. I had to go to the rifle range anyway, so I grabbed a selection. Sure enough, you can load them in the magazine in any sequence, and Solo feeds them and spits them out.
While there, I also 
checked patterns with a selection of loads
. Solo is supplied with three Optimachoke tubes: Cylinder, Modified, and Full. The Full choke tube is clearly marked “no steel,” the Cylinder and Modified tubes marked “ok steel.” I like to use the Thompson shotgun pattern targets, with quadrants and a side-view of a clay target in the center that serves as a consistent aiming point. Solo has a two-bead vent rib. For me, the stock is straight enough that I’m looking slightly down and stacking the beads. This means much of the pattern will be above point of aim, which is the way I like it. At 30 yards, patterns were even. Density increased with constriction of the choke tubes. With a six o’clock hold, patterns were about 60 percent above point of aim, which is perfect. With all three constrictions, and with various shot sizes, all patterns (including cylinder) showed multiple strikes in the clay target, thus in the center. We also have a steel pattern board and a 40-yard pad. There, I sprayed the 30-inch circle white and tried a Kent 3-inch, 1 5/8 ounces No. 7 TSS, with the Full tube. Good Lord, that was a pattern, dead center with almost no strikes outside of a 12-inch circle.
The B-Lok magazine cap doesn’t unscrew; it just rotates 60 degrees for removal and replacement, simple and easy. (Craig Boddington photo)
MUZZLE TO BUTT
Now that I knew it worked, let’s go over some of the some obvious, not so obvious features. I already mentioned the Optimachoke system, which clearly works just fine, and the ventilated rib. The barrel itself is hammer-forged from an alloy Beretta calls Steelium, with an 80mm double forcing cone contributing to consistent patterns.

The B-Lok magazine cap doesn’t unscrew; it locks in and out with a 60-degree rotation, simple and easy. The cap has the forward sling swivel stud on the end; the rear stud is integral to the toe of the stock. The box containing choke tubes also held a pair of detachable sling swivels.
Both fore-end and pistolgrip have what Beretta describes as “enhanced aggressive grip texturing.” I’d call it more like coarse checkering. Either way, you’re not likely to lose your grip, even if your hands are wet and cold. It is a rough surface, especially on the pistolgrip. My thought was, for high-volume shooting, light gloves might be a good idea. The loading port, bolt release, and crossbolt safety are all oversized, and the trigger guard is generous—all supporting use with gloves. The receiver is aluminum, drilled and tapped for optics, the rotating bolt operated by Beretta’s new B-Link gas-operating system. The gas system deserves some special love, so I’ll save that for a while.
The Solo has a redesigned trigger group, the trigger itself semi-flat, serrated on the bottom half, with a noticeably short reset. The pistolgrip is steeply curved. At least for my medium-sized hands, it offers a good and natural feel to the trigger. I hate to use millennial terms, but the ergonomics are sound. Or, as Beretta describes, “refined.”
The loading port is oversized, making operating fast and easy…and practical with gloves for winter or wet-weather waterfowling. (Craig Boddington photo)
The stock contains three elastomer shock absorbers, working together in the Kick-Off Plus system, reducing felt recoil by as much as 40 percent. The lower, forward two-thirds of the pistolgrip and the comb piece are separate from the stock, Beretta’s B-Steady system, enabling a firm grip and cheek weld, reducing movement and maintaining sight picture for faster repeat shots. The B-uffer (lots of “Bs” in Beretta’s nomenclature) recoil pad is designed to withstand roughest use in waterfowl blinds and offers improved recoil absorption.
SPORTING CLAYS
No bird seasons in August, so I called old friend Kyler Hamman to see if we might use his sporting clays range in the Parkfield Valley. Naturally, it was 100 degrees that August day. We passed Solo back and forth, went through a couple hundred rounds, with some breaks to let it cool. Not a single jam or failure to feed.

These days, Kyler’s a better stick with a shotgun than me, but Solo handles well and swings smoothly. My first thought: It’s a bulky shotgun. It really isn’t. Although deep, the fore-end is slender, and at exactly eight pounds with that barrel length, it’s a good weight for a full-size 12-gauge. Babying a rotator cuff issue, I was pleased with how well I shot it.
Obviously, I don’t have the equipment to measure recoil attenuation. Kyler and I both found it noticeably soft-shooting for a 12-gauge, and incredibly fast for repeat shots. As I said, the pistolgrip and fore-end texturing are so aggressive that I thought shooting gloves might be needed. Naturally, on that 100-degree day, I forgot them…and didn’t need them. Despite sweating hands, the textures offered a firm grip, no discomfort. That contributed to speedy repeat shots, and I noted that the enlarged loading port made reloading faster and more fumble-free.
From light targe loads to heavy magnum turkey loads, the A400 Solo digested all with equal appetite…in any order in the magazine, with equal speed. More than 200 rounds, not a single stoppage. (Craig Boddington photo)
SO BLINKIN’ FAST!
Back to the B-Link Pro gas operating system. With redesigned gas ports and piston, Beretta maintains Solo is 36 percent faster than other semiauto shotguns. I also lack the equipment to confirm or deny but Kyler and I both noted that Solo is very fast. I mentioned my shot-loose Model 12 slide-action? All my life, I’ve believed that an experienced shooter with a pump shotgun could cycle at least as fast as a semiauto. With the many semiauto shotguns I’ve used, I’ve noticed a split-second hesitation, waiting for the action to cycle. Not with the new B-Link gas system on the A400 Solo (also used in the top-of-line AX800 series). It’s so fast that it took a bunch of tries to get a still picture of the action cycling.
I’m no engineer. In fact, I’m mechanically hopeless. I understood redesigned gas ports. I also understood a redesigned piston, with special non-stick finish that enables it to run up 46 percent cleaner than previous systems. As usual, I can’t verify that number but, after a couple hundred rounds, cleaning the moving parts was just a matter of wiping them down, very little residue.
Now, that’s a pattern! 40 yards with Optimachoke Full choke tube and Kent 3-inch magnum with 1 5/8 ounces of TSS No 7. Virtually all pellets were within a 12-inch circle, a devastating pattern for turkey hunting. (Craig Boddington photo)
So, I was curious as to exactly how the B-Link system works. Using expanding gas to cycle the bolt goes back to the 19
th
 Century, not much mystery there, but where was the (new or missing) link, and what did a B-Link look like? I noted that a couple of references spelled it as “Blink.” I should have tumbled, did not. I had to embarrass myself by asking Beretta’s Dina Sanders where the name came from. B-Link Pro is based on new and different gas ports and refined piston metallurgy, working in conjunction with the rotating bolt. The name “B-Link” or “Blink” comes from “fast as the blink of an eye.” Which it is. 12 days until dove season, looking forward to using this blinkin’ fast shotgun!
