Burgo Model 108 .32 S&W Review: A 1960s German Budget Gun

Does This Budget 1960s Revolver Still Hold Up on the Range Today?

 The Burgo revolvers were manufactured by German firm Weihrauch & Weihrauch. (Bob Campbell photo)

October 01, 2026

By Bob Campbell

While the pawn shop revolver had a bronze “Burgo” medallion in the frame, I knew this revolver was actually manufactured by Weihrauch & Weihrauch GmbH & Co. KG of Mellrichstadt, Germany. Weihrauch was a concern that made, among other things, bicycle parts and air rifles, and in the 1960s it manufactured affordable revolvers for the American market.
These revolvers were marketed under the Arminius line—the name of a legendary, fierce Germanic chieftain—and were imported by the old Firearms Import & Export company and others. The line included the HW or Hermann Weih-rauch models produced beginning in 1960 and included .22, .32 and .38 caliber revolvers. The revolver illustrated is an HW 5 in company parlance. The revolver is stamped with European proofmarks and marked Model 108, with the aforementioned Burgo medallion in the frame.
Burgo History
“Burgo” roughly translates to fortress or fortified building. The inference is that the revolver would be used for home defense. These are double-action/single-action, swing-out cylinder revolvers primarily manufactured from a mix of aluminum and steel.
This Burgo is chambered to .32 S&W and had an innovative cylinder featuring seven chambers and a floating firing pin. While the first swing-out cylinder rimfire .32 with a seven-shot cylinder came out in 1860, this wasn’t common among inexpensive revolvers.

The cylinder is opened by pulling forward on a spring-loaded segmented ejector rod. There is no cylinder latch, but the cylinder locks up tight, and the action is relatively smooth—with even pressure throughout the cycle. The hammer spur is easy to cock, and the trigger is wide and controllable in double action. The single-action press is particularly nice with a 3.5-pound break.
The sights are on a raised boss above the receiver in European fashion rather than the usual American-style groove in the topstrap. A front post sight is screwed on. Serrations are cut in the topstrap between the sights.
My sample is nickel plated, with plastic birdshead grips screwed into a frame stub. The grips are nicely checkered.

Performance
I am certain this revolver hasn’t been fired often in its 60-some years of life. I shot the Burgo with 98- and 100-grain loads, and despite the Burgo’s 2.75-inch barrel and fixed sights, it was pleasantly accurate.
At seven yards I fired a group with five shots in the same hole in slow fire from a barricade position. The gun had a nice balance, and this is a revolver that would have served the occasional shooter well.
Final Thoughts
The Burgo was a well-designed and useful niche gun. It may seem less mechanically refined or well finished than more expensive revolvers—it cost a little less than half the price of a new Smith & Wesson in its heyday—but the Burgo was reliable nonetheless.
Unfortunately, the Burgo suffered by association with the truly cheap Rohm revolvers, as some Rohm guns were marketed under the Burgo name by importers. While the Burgo survived the Gun Control Act of 1968, a few years later some states passed metal melting-limit regulations that the Burgo’s alloys could not pass.
This interesting revolver may be found for less than $200. My sample was $135.
