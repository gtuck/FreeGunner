Christensen Arms Ridgeline FFT: A Sub-6-Pound Hunting Rifle

The Christensen Arms Ridgeline FFT combines a carbon-fiber barrel with Flash Forged Technology. A rugged, 5.4 lb hunting rifle designed for the mountains and backcountry.

 To enhance the rifle’s outstanding accuracy potential, Christensen installs an adjustable TriggerTech trigger. Our sample’s trigger pull was very consistent and averaged exactly 3.0 pounds right out of the box. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

May 12, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

Every year I exercise my “editor’s prerogative” and opt to write up a gun myself that really intrigues me rather than handing it off to one of the writers. I’m doing that this time with the Ridgeline FFT from Christensen Arms. It’s a very interesting rifle indeed.
Several features make this rifle special, so let’s take a good look at them.
Technological Advancements
I probably don’t have to tell readers that Christensen Arms has a long history with using carbon fiber and other aerospace materials in their rifles. That’s essentially what the company was founded on back in 1995. But Christensen hasn’t been resting on its laurels. The company has been innovating ever since, and one of the newest technological advancements they have developed is flash forged technology. It’s used in making their riflestocks, and it’s what the FFT stands for in the Ridgeline FFT rifle’s moniker.
I’m not a stockmaker, and I’m not an engineer, but as I understand it, the FFT process involves using omni-directional carbon-fiber strands and resin formed in a compression mold. That allows Christensen to make complicated shapes that would be difficult (maybe even impossible) and expensive to make by other methods. The end result is a much lighter weight yet incredibly strong, rigid, and stable carbon-fiber stock.

Rifle Stock
One thing I noticed right off when first handling the Ridgeline FFT is the feel of the grip. It’s noticeably different than other composite riflestocks I’ve handled recently. I’d describe the grip’s feel as narrow or slim but very solid, with slight palmswells on both sides. In way less technical terms, it just feels right.
The sporter-style stock, according to Christensen, uses spot bedding, with carbon-fiber bedding pillars. The barrel is free floated the entire length of the fore-end, and a dollar bill easily slid all the way down from the rounded end to the receiver on our rifle.
The stock has two sling-swivel studs in the usual locations. And it also has a one-inch-thick, soft, cushy recoil pad. It was quite effective in my shooting session, but I’ll get to that later.

Rounding out the stock’s features, our sample has a dark gray finish with what I’ll refer to as lighter gray splotches. There is no checkering anywhere on the stock.
Of course, the barrel is wrapped in carbon fiber, and the rifle comes with a stainless-steel side-baffle muzzle brake installed at the factory. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Carbon Fiber Barrel
Of course, the barrel is wrapped in carbon fiber. Christensen calls it “Aerograde” carbon fiber. The inner barrel is 416R stainless steel, and since our sample is chambered for .308 Winchester, the rifling twist rate is 1:10. That information is clearly marked on the left side of the barrel. The bore is button rifled and handlapped, and the barrel has a match chamber. The official barrel length is 20 inches long, and the rifle comes with a removable 2.25-inch-long stainless-steel side-baffle muzzle brake installed at the factory. The thread pitch is 5/8-24. The brake has three chevron-shaped ports on each side, and the top and bottom have flat surfaces. The muzzle end of the brake is slightly recessed and measures 0.88 inch in diameter. The barrel’s diameter where the brake is attached measures 0.92 inch.
Perhaps I don’t need to say this, but the basic reasons for using a carbon-fiber-wrapped barrel are to reduce weight and to enhance cooling. Anyone who has done much rifle shooting knows that the barrel heats up quickly when shooting rapid strings, and a really hot barrel usually doesn’t shoot as accurately. Carbon-fiber-wrapped barrels still heat up with rapid firing, but the carbon-fiber wrap is intended to speed up the cooling-down process. The Ridgeline FFT is built for mountain hunting, where light weight and optimal accuracy are top requirements, but shooting rapid fire strings isn’t the norm.
Talking about built for accuracy, to that end, the Ridgeline FFT comes with a TriggerTech trigger. It’s adjustable, but our rifle’s trigger pull was a crisp, clean, very consistent 3.0 pounds, according to my RCBS trigger pull gauge, so I did not make any adjustments to it. Over the 10 measurements taken for average, it varied by just 2 ounces. The fingerpiece is narrow, and it has vertical grooves on the face.
The rifle’s spiral fluted bolt has dual opposing locking lugs, a Sako-style extractor, and a plunger ejector. The bolt handle has lightening holes, and the bolt knob is aluminum. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Receiver and Bolt
The Ridgeline FFT comes without sights, but its precision-machined Model 14 action is drilled and tapped for Remington Model 700 scope-mount bases, so it’s very easy to set up with a scope. I used Warne bases and rings and my trusty Trijicon AccuPoint 4-16X 50mm scope for this project. The receiver has a matte silver finish (Christensen calls it natural stainless), and the bolt is finished in black, as are the trigger, the magazine release, the bolt release, the safety, and the sling-swivel studs.

The Ridgeline FFT’s bolt is spiral fluted, and it has dual opposing locking lugs, a Sako-style extractor, and a plunger ejector. The bolt handle has three lightening holes, and the enlarged bolt knob is aluminum.
The safety is positioned on the right side of the receiver, within comfortable reach of the thumb, and it is a two-position mechanism. Forward is “Fire,” and rearward is “Safe.” The bolt can be removed with the safety in either position. The improved bolt release is located traditionally on the left side of the receiver.
The Ridgeline FFT’s hinged floorplate is aluminum, and it has “CHRISTENSEN” etched into it in large block letters. The floorplate release is located inside the front of the trigger guard, and the internal magazine holds four rounds of .308 Winchester ammo. The company says the Ridgeline FFT is compatible with an aftermarket AICS magazine conversion kit for riflemen who prefer a detachable magazine.
One more feature of the action needs to be mentioned. The rear of the ejection port has been enlarged to facilitate clean, reliable ejection of fired cases. During my shooting session, I noted that empties ejected well clear of the firing position, to the right approximately 1.5 to two feet.
Lightweight Hunting Rifle
The Ridgeline FFT weighs 5.4 pounds without a scope. It measures 41.5 inches long overall. And the length of pull is 14.0 inches. Like I said earlier, our rifle is chambered for .308 Winchester, but this model also is offered in other chamberings, including .22 Creedmoor, .22-250, .243 Winchester, 6mm Creedmoor, .25 Creedmoor, 6.5 Creedmoor, 6.5-284 Norma, 6.5 PRC, .270 Winchester, .270 WSM, 6.8 Western, 7mm-08, .280 Ackley, 7mm Remington Magnum, 7mm PRC, 
7mm Backcountry
, .28 Nosler, .30-06, .300 WSM, .300 Winchester Magnum, .300 Remington Ultra Mag, .300 PRC, and .375 H&H. Some chamberings are offered in left-hand actions. And the Ridgeline FFT is offered in other color schemes too. The MSRP ranges from $2,049.99 to $2,149.99, depending on the configuration.
The Ridgeline FFT is a very lightweight hunting rifle. It weighs just 5.4 pounds unloaded and without a scope. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Sub-MOA Accurate, Guaranteed
Before I get to the details of my shooting session with the Ridgeline FFT, I have to relate a personal story having to do with the .308 Winchester cartridge. Just about every writer I’ve read (beginning back when I was a teenager [I’m 65 now]) and/or edited has said something along the lines of “the .308 Winchester is easy on the shoulder in terms of felt recoil.” Perhaps that is technically correct when you compare recoil energies on paper. But I am here to tell you that one of the hardest-kicking rifles I’ve ever shot was in .308 Winchester. (For the record, I’ve fired a lot of big boomers, including .338 Lapua, .458 Winchester Magnum, .458 Lott, and .50 BMG.) Anyway, the rifle I’m talking about happened to be the first .308 Win. rifle I ever shot. I was a 125-pound teenager (soaking wet) at the time. The rifle was a fairly heavy old military battle rifle that someone had rebarreled to .308 Win. The stock was a clunky piece of wood with no cheekpiece or recoil pad. It was just about the straightest buttstock I have ever encountered. I was shooting from a bench, and when I touched off that first round, well, let me tell you, I was caught off guard by how hard the rifle kicked. It didn’t knock me down, but it sure felt like it knocked my socks off! The point is, to this day, I am apprehensive whenever I shoot a .308 Win. rifle.
I needn’t have worried about the Ridgeline FFT, even though it’s light in weight compared to that old battle rifle. I can honestly say, that with some of the ammo I fired in it, it was easy on the shoulder. No doubt the effective recoil pad and muzzle brake helped make the rifle so comfortable to shoot.
Of course, the rifle was extremely accurate. In fact, the company says it’s guaranteed to be sub-MOA. That means three shots inside 1 MOA (1.047 inches at 100 yards), according to their website. Overall average accuracy at 100 yards for the eight factory loads I fired in it was 0.76 inch. As I’ve said so many times before in these pages, I am a mediocre shooter at best, so imagine what the rifle can do in the hands of a better shooter. Full disclosure, some of the ammo I test-fired was quite old, and I used some match loadings that are not meant for hunting, even though the rifle’s purpose is hunting, because I just wanted to see how they would shoot in this fine rifle. The results were very satisfying.

My top accuracy came with the Black Hills 168-grain BTHP ammunition. It averaged 0.50 inch for three, three-shot groups. (I fired three-shot groups because the company’s accuracy guarantee is for a three-shot group.) The average velocity for that loading was 2,582 fps, measured 12 feet from the gun’s muzzle. All chronograph figures in the accompanying chart are the averages of five rounds.
The Christensen Arms Ridgeline FFT is built to be light in weight, and it succeeds in that. However, it is no lightweight when it comes to performance. Our review sample was extremely accurate, even in the hands of an average shooter. Obviously, a lot of development and innovation went into designing and building the Ridgeline FFT. I think every rifleman can appreciate an accurate rifle. Most hunters can appreciate a lightweight rifle. Put the two qualities together, and you get the Ridgeline FFT. I don’t think anything more needs to be said.
