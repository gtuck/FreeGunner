High Standard H-DM/S: The OSS's Legendary Silenced Pistol

How a suppressed .22LR handgun became one of the most successful clandestine weapons of the war—and helped define modern American espionage

 Discover the story of the OSS High Standard H-DM/S silenced pistol, William Donovan, secret agents, and the legendary suppressed handgun of World War II espionage. (Courtesy Springfield Armory Museum) 

September 03, 2026

By Patrick Sweeney and David Fortier

In 1944, the head of America’s wartime spy agency allegedly walked into the Oval Office carrying a suppressed pistol, waited until President Franklin Roosevelt was distracted, and quietly emptied a magazine into a sandbag he had brought with him. The pistol was a High Standard H-DM/S. The man holding it was William J. Donovan, founder of the Office of Strategic Services (OSS), and the purpose of the demonstration was simple: convince the President that his new intelligence organization needed specialized weapons unlike anything then issued to the regular military.

Whether every detail of the story is exactly as remembered decades later is almost beside the point. The tale survives because it perfectly captures the personality of Donovan and the unconventional nature of the OSS. More importantly, it highlights one of the most interesting firearms of World War II, a suppressed .22LR pistol designed for espionage, sabotage, and clandestine warfare. While the 
M1 Garand
, 
Thompson submachine gun
, and Colt 1911 receive most of the attention from historians, the High Standard H-DM/S played a unique role in the shadows of the conflict.
The OSS Needed a Different Kind of Weapon
The OSS itself was a revolutionary concept for the United States. Prior to World War II, America had no centralized intelligence service comparable to Britain’s Special Operations Executive or Germany’s Abwehr. President Roosevelt turned to Donovan, a decorated veteran of World War I and a trusted advisor, to build such an organization from scratch. Donovan’s challenge was immense. He needed personnel, training programs, communications equipment, sabotage tools, and weapons suitable for missions that often required stealth rather than firepower.
For many OSS operators, the standard military sidearm was not ideal. The Colt 
M1911A1
 was powerful and reliable, but it was neither small nor quiet. A clandestine operator infiltrating occupied territory, meeting resistance contacts, or conducting sabotage often needed something different. That requirement led the OSS toward one of the most respected target pistols of the era, the High Standard H-D.

A Classic from High Standard Gets the Nod
High Standard had built a strong reputation before the war for producing accurate rimfire pistols. The H-D was chambered in .22 Long Rifle and featured excellent reliability, good ergonomics, and practical accuracy. Those qualities made it a logical foundation for a suppressed weapon. Rather than designing a completely new pistol, the OSS and High Standard modified an existing and proven platform.
A combat veteran of The Great War, with multiple citations including the Medal of Honor, “Wild Bill” Donovan had a bright future ahead of him. (Public domain)
The silencer was developed not by a firearms manufacturer but by engineers at Bell Telephone Laboratories. Better known for advances in communications technology, Bell Labs was deeply involved in wartime research and development projects. Externally, it resembled the commercial pistol, but the barrel had been extensively modified. The pistol's barrel was drilled with forty-four ports arranged in four rows. These ports vented propellant gases into the suppressor body before the bullet exited the muzzle. Inside the suppressor were stacks of bronze mesh screens and other sound-reduction elements that slowed and cooled escaping gases. The complete assembly measured roughly 7.75 inches long and brought the pistol's overall length to nearly 14 inches.
By modern standards the design appears primitive, but wartime engineers were solving problems under very different constraints. Precision-machined baffles were expensive and time-consuming to produce. Wartime production required simplicity, speed, and ease of maintenance. The screen-and-washer system could be assembled quickly and serviced with minimal tools. Bell Labs had created a suppressor that was durable, relatively easy to maintain, and highly effective. Contemporary reports credited the system with reducing the sound of firing by roughly 90 percent. Modern measurements vary, but there is little doubt the pistol was exceptionally quiet for its era. The resulting handgun was designated the High Standard H-DM/S, standing for High Standard H-D Military/Silenced.

The Advantages of .22 Long Rifle
The choice of .22LR also contributed significantly to the pistol’s effectiveness. Standard-velocity .22 ammunition was already relatively quiet compared to service pistol cartridges. Combined with a suppressor, the result was a firearm capable of discreet operation at close range. Although Hollywood often portrays suppressed firearms as nearly silent, no firearm is truly quiet. Nevertheless, the H-DM/S offered a dramatic reduction in noise and muzzle flash, making it well suited for specialized missions.
Interestingly, wartime planners became concerned about ammunition selection. The Hague Convention restrictions regarding expanding bullets influenced military thinking, leading to the development of a full-metal-jacket .22LR loading for OSS use. Whether this decision was driven entirely by legal concerns remains open to debate. Anyone who has fired large quantities of lead-bullet rimfire ammunition through a suppressor understands how quickly fouling accumulates. It is entirely possible practical maintenance considerations played a role alongside legal ones.
Service Across the World
The pistol found its way into the hands of OSS personnel operating across multiple theaters. Agents inserted behind enemy lines, resistance organizers, saboteurs, and special operations teams all required equipment tailored to unconventional warfare. The H-DM/S was never intended to replace standard military arms. Instead, it filled a specialized niche where concealability and discretion mattered more than firepower.
By the 1940s, ex-colonel Donovan was well-positioned to take on the task of being the new American spymaster. (Public domain)
The H-DM/S quickly became one of the OSS's most widely distributed weapons. By mid-1944, hundreds had already been issued in Europe, the Mediterranean, and the Pacific. OSS operatives carried them into occupied France, Norway, Italy, and the Balkans. British Special Operations Executive personnel also received quantities of the pistols for use throughout Nazi-occupied Europe.
Among the men recruited into the OSS was actor Sterling Hayden. Standing 6-foot-5, Hayden hardly looked like the stereotypical secret agent, yet Donovan saw potential in people who could think independently and operate under unusual circumstances. The OSS attracted a remarkable collection of personalities, from Ivy League academics to professional athletes, artists, adventurers, and future celebrities. They were united by a mission that demanded improvisation, creativity, and often considerable courage. The High Standard accompanied many of them into dangerous environments where discovery could mean torture or execution.

The pistol proved useful well beyond its intended role. Army History records that the Alamo Scouts, an elite reconnaissance unit operating in the Pacific, even used the suppressed High Standard to hunt game while deep behind enemy lines. Such use illustrated one of the weapon's greatest strengths: versatility. A pistol designed for clandestine warfare could also quietly gather food without revealing a patrol's location.
The famous Roosevelt demonstration remains one of the most enduring stories associated with the pistol. Skeptics point out that firing a gun in the Oval Office sounds absurd. Yet the circumstances of the time make the story more plausible than modern readers might assume. Donovan was not an ordinary visitor. He was a former U.S. Attorney, a trusted presidential advisor, and one of Roosevelt’s oldest associates. Security procedures were far less restrictive than they would become after the war.
Roosevelt himself was also not an inexperienced civilian unfamiliar with firearms. In his younger years, he had been a competitive rifle shooter and was certainly familiar with firearms. Add in the relative novelty of suppressors, wartime informality, and Donovan’s forceful personality, and the story becomes surprisingly believable. Whether every detail happened exactly as described, the tale survived because it reflected the confidence Donovan had in both the weapon and the organization he was building.
Sterling Hayden, a USMC Lieutenant fresh out of OCS, was assigned to the OSS. (Public domain)
From the OSS to the CIA
When World War II ended, the OSS was dissolved, but many of its functions eventually migrated into the newly created Central Intelligence Agency. The High Standard went with them. Postwar production runs were completed for CIA use, including examples that lacked the military markings found on wartime guns. Some were shipped in plain packaging and were intended specifically for clandestine service.
The pistol continued serving through the Cold War. It appeared in Korea, Vietnam, and numerous covert operations conducted throughout the developing world. Perhaps its most famous postwar user was Francis Gary Powers. When Soviet forces shot down his U-2 reconnaissance aircraft in 1960, Powers was carrying a High Standard H-DM/S. The pistol was recovered by Soviet authorities and remains one of the most recognizable artifacts associated with the U-2 incident.
Today, original H-DM/S pistols rank among the most desirable OSS collectibles. Their rarity, historical significance, and direct connection to clandestine operations have made them highly sought after. Surviving examples command substantial prices whenever they appear on the collector market. Many remain hidden away in private collections, museums, and government inventories.
The High Standard H-DM/S was never intended to win battles or equip armies. Its mission was far narrower and, in many ways, far more dangerous. It was a weapon built for a world of secret meetings, resistance networks, sabotage operations, and intelligence gathering. While larger and more powerful firearms dominated the battlefields of World War II, this quiet little .22LR pistol helped define the covert side of the conflict. More than eighty years later, it remains one of the most fascinating spy guns ever produced.
