For the People: Ed Brown's 1776 Commemorative 1911

The Ed Brown 1776 Commemorative 1911 blends limited edition with practical use. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)

July 04, 2026

By James Tarr

In honor and celebration of America’s 250th birthday — which is the “Semiquincentennial” in case you were wondering — Ed Brown announced two special 1776 tribute models. The first was a one-off, fully hand-­engraved masterpiece that sold for $24,995 at the 2026 SHOT Show. There will be no others like it. There was also a model that was spec’d to be more affordable — that price being relative for an Ed Brown 1911. 
The 1776 Commemorative Edition
 is a Government Model with a bevy of old-­school custom features, and Guns & Ammo secured one for testing.
The Ed Brown 1776 Commemorative Edition 1911 celebrates 250 years of American exceptionalism. The restrained configuration incorporates many patented parts pioneered by Ed Brown, including the beavertail and thumb safety. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The 1776 Commemorative Edition is a single-­stack 1911 chambered in .45 ACP. It’s all stainless steel, including the 5-­inch barrel, slide and frame. Everything about the gun is classic. In fact, the feature set is basically what you’d get if you ordered a custom carry 1911 from any premium gunsmith in the mid-­1980s.
Angled slide serrations tastefully incorporate stars and stripes of the American flag. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The chamber is properly throated and polished. At the front of the slide, the stainless-steel barrel bushing is marked “1776”, engraved below the muzzle. The bushing is fit perfectly with absolutely no play, but you can still remove it with your fingers — no bushing wrench needed. The edges on the bushing are gently rounded, and the same is true with the front of the slide as well. Ed Brown ever-­so-­subtly knocked the sharp edge off the bottom of the slide, too, so it won’t cut your thumb when you shoot with a proper thumb-­high hold.
Marked “1776”, the match-grade bushing is fitted to arrest any movement of the muzzle. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Old School, New Touches
This old-­school 1911 has the original short recoil spring guide. Between that and the checkered front of the recoil spring plug, it’s easy to press-­check the pistol, which is an old-­school technique to confirm the status of the chamber. It has a round-­topped slide.

At the back is a Novak-­style no-­snag rear sight. Wayne Novak designed this pattern of sight, and it was hugely popular in the 1980s. There is no edge on the front and the back forms almost one continuous line with the rear of the slide, hence the “no-­snag” appellation. You get a simple, plain notch.
The dovetailed front bead is gold plated and won’t tarnish. It fills the square-shaped rear sight notch of the Novak ramped rear sight. The flanks of the sight also feature angled, tactile serrations. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
At the front, the dovetailed post front sight has a gold bead. The cylindrical steel insert has 14-karat gold plating, and sights like this were the original so-called “night sights” before tritium sights became a thing. The gold bead catches the light and is easy to see under all lighting conditions. Gold doesn’t tarnish. If it gets dirty, just wipe it off.
On the right side of the slide, there is the standard “CUSTOM BY Ed Brown” marking. On the left side of the slide, laser etched, is “250 years of freedom” and below that “1776-­2026”. At the rear of the slide on both sides — at the bottom of the flat-­bottomed angled slide serrations, or “stripes” — are the stars. They turn the serrations into a representation of the American flag. The detail is so fine and so perfect that it’s clear it was accomplished by machining, a testament to the quality and precision machining Ed Brown is known for. Slide-­to-­frame fit was perfect, with just the tiniest bit of play, which is helpful to keep the gun running even when it’s fouled. Barrel lockup was tight and secure, with no play whatsoever.

Ed Brown’s 1776 Commemorative Edition was configured on a Series 70 Government platform with a 5-inch .45-caliber barrel; long, solid aluminum trigger; left-side controls, short guiderod and standard recoil spring. The polished burlap Micarta grips mimic the appearance of wood, and the seven-round magazine is Ed Brown’s. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Inside the slide, you won’t see a firing-pin safety because this gun uses a Series 70-­type fire control, as John Browning would have it. It’s better though given the steel used in this era is better. With precision CNC machining, the tolerances and consistent results are like nothing Browning experienced. To that note, Ed Brown started with the original 1911 and then redesigned the entire firearm. Parts start out intentionally oversized in critical areas, so they can be fit correctly by their gunsmiths.
A simple message, “250 years of freedom/1776-2026” indicates what this model of 1911 was spec’d to honor. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
In the 1980s, a properly set-­up 1911 became known as the “professional’s pistol,” and a few gunsmiths rose to prominence. One of those was 1911 part supplier Ed Brown. While Ed Brown Products Inc., is no longer a one-­man shop, after 58 years the company continues to produce some of the finest 1911s.
From the rear, the shooter will note the black, ramped, Novak rear sight, the combat hammer serrated for thumb cocking, the blended beavertail grip safety with Speed Bump, and the internal extractor. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Pieces of History
At the rear of the pistol is Ed Brown’s famous Memory Groove beavertail. Let me take a moment to lay down some history if you are unfamiliar with custom 1911s or Ed Brown, the man. Back in the 1970s and ’80s, if you wanted a Model 1911 with any of features on this gun, you needed to buy a GI-­style 1911 and give it to a custom gunsmith — along with a wad of cash. No factories made guns like this until the 2000s. Cutting-­edge 1911 gunsmiths began designing and manufacturing upgraded parts for the 1911 though, including triggers, hammers, safeties, barrels, sights, and more. Ed Brown made a name for himself with a number of parts. In my opinion, his most significant contribution was the beavertail grip safety with memory grooves and a “Speed Bump” to deactivate it. Gunsmiths cut the frame to fit and install these, often reshaping the frame and safety to blend them. You really needed one if you were going to run the gun reliably. Bill Wilson’s cut was easier to make, but Ed Brown’s frame cut and beavertail positioned the hand up higher on the gun than other grip safeties. Brown’s helped us better manage the recoil of the .45 ACP. It sweeps up so high that it’s countersunk. A cocked skeletonized combat-­style hammer nests protected inside the beavertail. Later, Brown added the Speed Bump at the bottom to better ensure deactivation for shooters with smaller or thinner hands, then horizontal serrations called “Memory Grooves.”
The extended thumb safety is Ed Brown, fitted flawlessly to the backstrap of the frame and to the sear and hammer. The safety snaps off intuitively to fire, and requires intent to positively engage. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Brown’s innovative single-­sided extended thumb safety was slightly oversized, too, for a gunsmith’s fitment and intuitive use. This one clicks up and down with that same positive authority. There should be no doubt whether or not the thumb safety is engaged on a 1911. It demands enough resistance that it doesn’t get bumped off unintentionally.
Inside the triggerguard is a long, solid aluminum trigger — the type that was popular among serious 1911 shooters in the ’80s. I’ve always loved the look of a solid trigger as opposed to the Videki-type three-­hole trigger.

The triggerguard isn’t technically undercut, but it is cut straight back to the frontstrap. It helps to put our hand higher on the gun. The trigger pull was exactly what we want on a 1911 that’s to be carried at least some of the time, too. Total pull weight was 4¼ pounds with a crisp release, short take-­up, and even shorter reset.
The front strap is completely checkered, 25 lines per inch. A notch at the bottom affords room for the magazine floorplate. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The frontstrap and mainspring housing were given 25 lines-­per-­inch (LPI) checkering. Historically, the only way to get this quality was to have a gunsmith carve it by hand. Companies are now able to machine-­checker guns to perfection, providing all the performance without the wait or cost. Twenty-­five LPI is a good middle ground — aggressive but not overly so — and is generally considered ideal for a carry gun.
Controls feature serrations at the contact points, except the top of the slide-lock lever and magazine release are checkered. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Constitutional
The magazine release is checkered but not extended, and it does not stick out as far from the frame as the grips so we don’t have to worry about accidentally dislodging a magazine. As for the grips, the somewhat jaded employees at my gun store wanted to know what they were made of because they are quite interesting. They almost look like bamboo due to the pattern, but in truth they are hand-­polished burlap Micarta. Micarta is a layered composite often used in knife handles that is impervious to moisture.
Two Ed Brown seven-round magazines are provided with the 1776 Commemorative Edition 1911. Bumpers and fasteners are also supplied, which can be attached per user preference. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
At the bottom of the frame, the magazine well opening is beveled to smooth reloads. The company supplies two of its stainless-­steel Performance Magazines. The seven-­round magazines feature non-­tilt followers, index holes and polymer basepad kits. When I’ve previously reviewed Ed Brown 1911s in .45 ACP, my only complaint was that they were supplied with 7-­round magazines when reliable 8-­rounders have been available since the late 1980s. For this somewhat retro-styled tribute, seven-­round magazines seem appropriate.
At the range, this pistol ate everything I fed it and it was fun to shoot. A full-­size Government Model is heavy until you start touching off 230 grainers, about the time you realize the weight helps absorb recoil. Other calibers are still judged against he .45 ACP. 
(Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Ed Brown’s 1776 Commemorative was as accurate as one would expect of a custom 1911 made by the first 1911 gunsmith to offer a lifetime warranty. It is a collector’s piece, in my opinion, one that was built for carry.
