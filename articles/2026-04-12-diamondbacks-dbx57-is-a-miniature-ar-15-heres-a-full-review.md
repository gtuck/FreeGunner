These advantages make PDWs popular defensive firearms that are carried in a pack, stored in a vehicle, or kept on hand for home defense. 
The DBX57 from Diamondback Firearms
 is an interesting PDW chambered in 5.7x28mm. 
In the late 1980s, FN Herstal began development on the 5.7x28mm cartridge and the FN P90 simultaneously. The 5.7x28mm was designed from scratch, with no parent cartridge. The intent was to provide NATO with superior ballistic performance to the 9mm. 
While the 5.7x28mm did not replace the 9mm, its attributes have made it a popular choice for military, law enforcement, and civilian use. In recent years, the 5.7x28mm cartridge has steadily gained steam. Several major manufacturers are producing 5.7x28mm chambered firearms. FN’s Five-seveN, Ruger’s 5.7, and Smith & Wesson’s M&P 5.7 are among the most popular pistols, offering higher capacity than similarly sized 9mm pistols. Despite the 5.7x28mm’s significantly higher velocity, it produces noticeably less recoil, making for faster and more accurate hits. 
Twenty rounds feed from ProMag FN magazines. (Photo by Yamil Sued)
A PDW in 5.7x28mm is even easier to shoot than a pistol thanks to its additional weight, longer barrel, and mounting options. Unlike a 5.56x45 chambered PDW, the 5.7x28mm doesn’t suffer from reduced velocity, which could hinder terminal ballistics.

The DBX57 is stockless and can be fired one of four ways. You could fire it like a pistol but with your support hand on the handguard. This is doable but awkward due to its 8-inch barrel and relative heft. Adding a single point sling enables you to drive the DBX57 out until the sling is taught for increased stability, comfort, and accuracy. 
You could fire the DBX57 with a cheekweld and a “C-grip” on the handguard. While shooting a PDW in this manner may seem uncomfortable at first, I’ve found it to be a viable option — especially when negotiating the firearm in close quarters. 
Though stockless, a rear-mounted rail allows for a folding stock or brace to be attached. (Photo by Yamil Sued)
For instance, when clearing a structure, your barrel extending beyond a doorway or around a corner can enable a bad guy to know where you are before you know where he is. With a PDW like the DBX57 cheek mounted, you can avoid telegraphing your position without having to depress or raise the muzzle as you would do with a rifle or carbine.

Finally, you could use a pistol brace. Diamondback Firearms sells a DBX-57 Folding Brace Kit, which includes an SB 1913 brace, a 20-round ProMag FN magazine, and a Magpul hand stop ($199). If you opt for a folding brace, make sure it either folds to the right or you swap the stock selector switch for one that’s ambidextrous. 
While less than ideal, close confines and exigent circumstances may dictate that a PDW be fired with the brace folded. If your brace folds to the left, you will be unable to thumb the stock selector switch when the brace is folded, meaning you can’t switch from Safe to Fire. Deploying a pistol brace adds time that you may not have to spare if you were suddenly faced with a deadly threat. 
The DBX57’s muzzle device will help cut down on the already minimal recoil from the 5.7x28mm. (Photo by Yamil Sued)
The handstop is a good idea even if you don’t opt for a brace. Like a hilt to keep your hand from sliding onto a knife blade, a handstop helps prevent your hand from accidentally crossing the muzzle. It also affords better control to drive the muzzle to the target and rapidly transition from one target to another. Since I prefer the “C grip,” I would mount the handstop using the M-LOK slots on the left side of the handguard as opposed to below it. 
A 7075 aluminum frame and 6061 aluminum handguard keep the DBX57’s unloaded weight to a mere 3 pounds — not much heavier than some steel framed, full sized pistols. Without an optic, the DBX57 measures 15.25 inches in length, 7.3 inches tall, and 1.75 inches wide. Lightweight, compact, and low recoiling, the DBX57 is convenient to store and handy to shoot.
The Magpul MOE-K pistol grip, selector switch, and trigger are all AR-15 style. The reciprocating charging handle is on the left side. The handle can be swapped to the right side, making it more user-friendly for left-handed shooters.

The slide stop lever on the left side of the DBX57 is a departure from the AR-15 design and is a larger version of what you’d find on most semiautomatic pistols. The magazine release is located on the right side of the firearm, designed to be pressed with your right index finger. It’s flush fit, so you aren’t likely to accidentally eject a magazine, but it’s more difficult to use than what you’d expect to find on an AR-15. The 20-round polymer magazine loads into an AR-15 style magwell. 
The AR-style trigger and controls will be familiar to shooters. (Photo by Yamil Sued)
The DBX57 features a lock breech and a dual gas piston operating system. The gas is adjustable, so you can tune it to the load you’re using. Best of all, this is done with a flathead screwdriver without having to disassemble the firearm. The positions are marked 1 through 4, with 1 being the smallest port and 4 being the largest. The operating system along with the 5.7x28mm chambering and muzzle device contribute to the DBX57’s minimal recoil. 
Its thin, flat profile makes the DBX57 easy to grip and to control when firing. Shooting the DBX57 was enjoyable, and hitting the target at 25 yards was a cinch with the 
Burris RT-1 red-dot sight
. During accuracy testing, I braced the handguard on a rest and pressed the receiver against my cheek. I obtained a .8-inch five-shot group using Federal American Eagle 40-grain FMJ. Only one group measured over 2 inches. Most were between 1 and 1.5 inches. 
Accuracy was not a problem. The overall accuracy average with all three 40-grain loads was under an inch and a half. However, out of about 85 rounds fired, the DBX57 had a total of eight light primer strikes (with different loads). There were also two failures to eject using Fiocchi FMJ. 
The light primer strikes with each load were baffling. Light primer strikes are often attributed to ammunition, but when all three loads produce light primer strikes, it suggests an issue with the firearm. 
Accuracy during testing was maximized by the Burris RT-1 red-dot sight. (Photo by Yamil Sued)
As the range session wore on, the DBX57 malfunctioned less, but if I were going to employ the DBX57 for defensive purposes, I would pick one load and make sure the gas piston system was tuned for reliability. While any firearm could malfunction, I wouldn’t designate a firearm for self-defense if I had any doubts as to its functionality. 
Despite its commonality with the AR-15, the DBX57 is different. Field stripping and assembly are more difficult. You’ll need a hammer and a punch because the takedown and pivot pins are under considerable tension. Fortunately, detailed instructions are in the 
DBX owner’s manual available at diamondbackfirearms.com
. 
Overall, the DBX57 is a well-designed PDW. Its chambering makes it much easier to shoot than a 5.56, and you won’t have to worry about decreased velocity hampering the effectiveness of your rounds on target. The average overall velocity for the three loads tested was 1,841 fps.

If you’re in the market for a PDW, the DBX57 is a solid choice. Its AR-15 modularity enables you to customize it to your liking, yet despite its AR-15-like functionality, the size and weight make it much more practical to store.
