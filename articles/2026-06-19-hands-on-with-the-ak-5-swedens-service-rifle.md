Hands On With the Ak 5: Sweden's Service Rifle

A rare test fire of Sweden's take on the FNC.

June 19, 2026

By Eric R. Poole

*Editor's Note: This story originally appeared in the 2013 edition of the AK47 SIP.
Designed in the late 1970s, the Ak 5 wasn’t produced for nearly six years after the Carl Gustaf prototype appeared in 1980. Bofors Carl Gustaf AB is the Swedish armament firm currently responsible for manufacturing Sweden’s small arms, including this variant of the Fabrique Nationale FNC. The Ak 5 has afterward been carried by Swedish armed forces and police since entering service in 1986 and has most recently served alongside American troops fighting the War on Terror in Afghanistan. 
The Ak 5 replaced the Ak 4, which was also a licensed-produced version of another battle-proven rifle, the HK G3. In the mid-1970s, Swedish armed forces agreed to follow the global transition toward smaller-caliber ammunition and directed the Swedish Defence Materiel Administration (FMV) in Stockholm to procure a suitable replacement that utilized NATO’s standard 5.56x45mm cartridge. Specifically, the rifle had to prove itself highly reliable in the near-artic climate of northern Sweden while offering users ease of use, simple maintenance and a high level of accuracy.
The FMV evaluated 11 different 5.56 NATO-chambered contenders between 1979 and 1985 before selecting the Belgian FN FNC, but the FNC would need several modifications to address Sweden’s harsh training environments.

The Details
The Ak 5 was given a larger triggerguard and cocking handle for use with thick winter gloves, which could be expected of troops operating in temperatures falling to -22 degrees Fahrenheit. The Ak 5 also features a larger handguard than the FNC, a different side-folding stock, unique iron sights, a gas block, a modified bolt and a corrosion-resistant green finish. Although early Ak 5s didn’t wear the black finish usually seen on the FNC, short-barreled versions and those issued to Swedish police would eventually be finished in black. 
The FNC’s three-round-burst capability was disregarded for full-auto firing capability in the Ak 5, and detachable box magazines were modified to work interchangeably with the M16’s STANAG mags. During my inspection of a police-issued Ak 5 in Malmo, Sweden, I noted that these black shorties were once select-fire automatics now converted to semiauto only.
This is the Ak 5D still supporting certain units within the Swedish military. The Swedish police model has its full-auto capability removed, is painted black and is given backup sights and a B&T quad-rail forend.
The first generation of Ak 5 rifles were manufactured by FFV Ordnance AB under license from FN. Deliveries to the Ministry of Defence started in 1986. Though these Ak 5s are still kept in inventory with the Swedish armed forces, they are no longer issued to soldiers, as they have been replaced by the Ak 5C and Ak 5D models. The original featured fixed sights capable of engaging targets at 400 meters, meeting the requirements for practical accuracy.

The Ak 5B was the designated marksman version of the Ak 5. It was fitted with a 4X SUSAT L9A1 tritium sight, given a cheekpad on the stock and lacked metallic sights. This rifle is usually issued to squad leaders and weighed between 10½ and 12 pounds. It’s estimated that 5,200 of these rifles have been manufactured. 
Meet Caesar
The Ak 5C was the result of Sweden’s future soldier program, beginning in the year 2000. The military called for improved ergonomics, reliability and a forend rail system to accommodate accessories such as weaponlights and lasers. Rather than adopt an entirely new rifle, Sweden opted to modify the Ak 5B to reduce program risks and costs. 
Certain units within the Swedish armed forces were provided with an experimental model designated the Ak 5CF. More than 1 million rounds were fired through these rifles during their trial period. They produced a Mean Rounds Between Stoppage value of 3,500. This is an improvement of 1,500 rounds between failures over the performance of the original Ak 5. Uniformed evaluators indicated that the test rifles were too heavy and long, so the barrel length was reduced to 13.78 inches to reduce weight to 8.38 pounds and shift the handling fulcrum closer to the user’s body. 
Testing was complete in June 2005, and four months later the FMV contracted the modification of nearly 40,000 Ak 5 rifles, requiring four years to complete. The Ak 5C (not pictured) was first issued to frontline units serving with the ISAF in Afghanistan as well as soldiers in Chad and Kosovo. 
The Ak 5C attempts to follow the tactical gains achieved by a growing number of modular rifle systems in use throughout the world. The inclusion of the Picatinny rail system allows the Ak 5C to accept a variety of optic configurations and accessories like Sweden’s standard-issue Aimpoint red dots and magnifiers.

All Ak 5s are now equipped with Aimpoint optics. Designed to endure Sweden’s extreme cold-weather climate, these Ak 5s functioned flawlessly during our evaluation.
The Ak 5C now includes a folding stock that’s more comfortable to shoulder and adjustable for length of pull. Though slimmer, the grip is still based on the FN-type ergonomics, but each rifle now includes a set of folding backup iron sights. Unlike the earlier Ak 5, the Ak 5C utilizes an automatic bolt-catch design once magazines run dry, and the receiver now offers an ambidextrous selector lever. Steel and some translucent 30-round magazines are issued to Swedish soldiers, but the Ak 5C will still readily accept the NATO-standard magazines in wide circulation. The Ak 5C has also been given a new flash suppressor, a formerly absent bayonet lug, a detachable vertical foregrip and a Viking Tactics sling. 
The Aimpoint CompM2 with four-MOA red dot reticle is the standard optic for the Ak 5C. However, some units use the CompM3 and CompM4 as well as the lightweight Micro T-1.
The charging handle reciprocates during its cycle of operation. To send the bolt forward after it has been locked to the rear, simply press (or slap) the knob of the charging handle protruding from the right side behind the ejection port.
Sweden's New SBR
The Ak 5D is distinct with its short, 10.24-inch barrel. Swedish ranger units and those assigned to special urban warfare units and vehicle crews are issued the Ak 5D. Certain Swedish police units are also equipped with a specialized version of the Ak 5D, designated the CGA5P. Built on modified Ak 5D military carbines, these SBRs are given a black finish, a 
Brugger und Thomet (B&T)
 forend rail system and fixed sights. A hex screw blocking the full travel of the selector switch indicates that automatic-fire capability for these law enforcement carbines has been disabled. 
Rounds Downrange
Larry Vickers and I were provided an opportunity by Aimpoint to study and evaluate two Ak 5s on a small outdoor range near Malmo, Sweden, in June 2012. We sampled a former Swedish-military Ak 5 and a newer police-issue CGA5P. 
We fieldstripped each and recorded a number of interesting differences between the Swedish design and the FNC it is based on. For example, two spring-loaded stainless steel detents push up between the upper and lower to improve fit between the receivers. The Ak 5 details for each model can be seen in the accompanying photos.
This select-fire Ak 5 illustrates the enlarged winter triggerguard and FNC-type pistol grip.
I fired each rifle from a supported prone position, since a bench wasn’t available. The ammo provided was the German DM11 NATO ball, a 62-grain SS109 load from Metallwerk Elisenhutte GmbH that typically runs just over 3,000 fps out of a 16-inch barrel. Unfortunately, there was no chronograph equipment available for our tests, and our host restricted shooting on this range to a distance of only 50 meters at reduced-size targets designed for zeroing Aimpoints. Still, I was grateful for this opportunity. 
I decided to make the most of it and patiently took my time, making each shot count, and skillfully applied the fundamentals of marksmanship. Larry spent most of his time evaluating the other rifle by performing rapid mag dumps for the camera while I slowly fired five five-shot groups. (I probably pissed off Larry during this process.) Once I finally finished printing groups, we switched. 
The military Ak 5 variant dominated accuracy testing by averaging .99-inch groups at 50 meters. This result was possibly skewed by the fact that my last five-shot target posted a nearly dead-center .44-inch result. The newer LE model averaged 1.4-inch groups at this distance, with the best cluster measuring 1.09 inches. It’s difficult to say whether these close-range results are representative of the true accuracy potential in either of these rifles, but I’ll leave that conclusion to others.
