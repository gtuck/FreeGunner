PSA Dagger Compact Full Review: A Glock 19 Gen 3 Clone 

An Affordable 9mm Performer: Is This Threaded Barrel Clone Worth It?

 The PSA Dagger Compact is a Glock Gen 3 clone that’s one steal of a deal. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

June 11, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

There’s a sea of striker-fired 9mm polymer-frame pistols on the market today, so for a gun to really stand out in this crowded field requires something special. For 
Palmetto State Armory
’s Dagger, that “something” is a long list of features and add-ons at an unbeatable price.
This “more bang for your buck” ethos has been PSA’s bread and butter in recent years, and it’s one of the reasons why the Columbia, South Carolina-based firearms manufacturer has become so popular in a relatively short period of time. Palmetto State Armory was founded in 2008, originally supplying AR-15 parts and accessories. Eventually, the company began building its own guns, which led to the introduction of the Dagger.
The Dagger comes in full-size, compact and micro versions. The Dagger Compact I tested is based on the Glock G19 Gen 3. In fact, virtually all the Dagger’s components are interchangeable with G19 Gen 3 parts, and that has led some people to wonder if the Dagger is simply a rehashed Glock that’s cobbled together from aftermarket components. Having shot a Dagger Compact for the better part of three months, I can tell you this is not a fair assessment. In fact, in some ways I like the Dagger more than the G19 and similar clones.
The Combo Deal
The Dagger Compact has a 4.5-inch barrel and weighs 22.4 ounces. Overall length is 7.15 inches. It’s 5.25 inches tall from the top of the sight, and just 1.25 inches at its widest point, making it very concealable. Capacity is 15 rounds. The Cerakoted slide is available in black, Sniper Green or flat dark earth finishes. I selected the Sniper Green version, which comes with a matching green polymer frame.

My particular sample was a “combo pack” that Palmetto offers: the gun, a zippered carrying case and not one, not two but 10 magazines. The base suggested retail price for the Dagger is in the low $300 range, and even with this combo pack the sticker price is only $470.
The SW1 slide windows offer a look at the threaded, DLC-coated barrel. They replace traditional forward serrations but function in the same manner. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Slide Windows
PSA offers the Dagger with a wide array of different slide window cut options. My sample had the SW1 style, which sports three side windows and has no front serrations. Other variations include different numbers and sizes of windows or ports, including the company’s concave Gator Mouth cut on the front of some slides. For the more traditional-minded there’s also the ECC slide, which lacks any windows or ports and has serrations front and rear.
The milled windows of the SW1 slide on my gun shows off the black Diamond Like Carbon-coated barrel and stainless steel recoil spring. More importantly, the cutouts offer a secure grip for the support hand for conducting press checks or man-ipulating the slide.

I perform press checks on every gun every time I head to the range, and some pistols make the process challenging because the hand doing the pressing keeps slipping off the slide. Not so with the Dagger, and that’s also thanks in no small part to the Cerakote finish. It’s not as slick as a black nitride finish, but the Cerakote has a slightly “grippier” surface for operating the pistol.
The stainless steel slide on my sample Dagger also features what PSA refers to as Extreme Carry Cuts, a series of machining touches that remove the rough edges on the Dagger’s slide and make the pistol easier to operate. The six angled rear slide serrations offer added texturing for better control when racking the slide, and the front and rear edges of the slide have been machined at an angle—eliminating the “Glock block” profile and improving the gun’s aesthetics.
Although the Dagger I tested is the Compact version, the barrel is threaded 1/2x28 and capped. The barrel has a 1:10 twist rate, and as I mentioned my test sample features a rugged black DLC coating.
The Dagger’s grip geometry will work for most shooters, and the micro-texturing is comfortable, modern and functional. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Frame
One of the Dagger’s best features is its excellent polymer frame. There’s a slight swell on the left and right sides of the grip, along with a single finger groove. The Dagger’s grip geometry keeps more of the surface of the grip in contact with the shooter’s hand for better feel, control and comfort. It fits my hand more like a 1911 with rounded grip panels than the industrial square grips found on some striker-fired 9mms.
There’s an ample undercut to allow for a high grip on the pistol, and a cutout on the base of the grip allows the shooter to manually strip sticky mags. PSA molds a single-slot rail into the dust cover for adding accessories.

Sights & Optic Ready
Unlike some striker-fired pistols, the Dagger features good sights. Both the front and rear are made of metal, and because they are on the Glock pattern they can be easily swapped out for aftermarket G19 sights. However, I really like the PSA sights—far more than Glock’s factory sights.
The Dagger’s notch rear sight is trapezoidal in shape and aligns with an extra-tall black post front. The iron sights are tall enough to co-witness with optics, especially given the Dagger’s optic slide cut. There are no white dots, lines or tritium lamps to distract the shooter’s eye when using a dot.
The Dagger’s slide is milled for a specific optic footprint—in this case the Trijicon RMR. The sights are plain so they don’t distract the eye, and they co-witness with the RMR. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Modern pistols are almost all cut for optics, and so it is with the Dagger. But PSA doesn’t send a bunch of different plates for optics mounting. Instead, PSA Dagger slides are cut for a specific optic or optic footprint. The options include the Trijicon RMR and Docter—along with the RMSc for the Dagger Micro model.
The Dagger I tested was cut for the Trijicon RMR, which I requested because I had
 Trijicon’s new green-dot RMR 
on hand. The Dagger showed up with a cover plate installed, along with the proper screws and wrench to install an RMR-footprint optic.
Controls
The Dagger’s controls are similar to the familiar Glock layout. There’s an L-shape slide stop on the left side of the pistol, and a transverse takedown tab. The magazine release is recessed in a molded cutout on the left side of the grip and is reversible for left-handed shooters.
The Dagger does incorporate a trigger safety, but instead of the traditional blade-style safety used by so many pistol manufacturers, PSA utilizes a two-piece trigger with a hinged safety shoe that allows the face of the trigger to remain flat—a feature I prefer. There is no manual safety, but in addition to the trigger safety the Dagger also employs a firing pin block.
Range Time and Accuracy
The Dagger performed much better during accuracy testing than its price tag might suggest. My best group was a 1.5-inch five-shot cluster produced using 
Hornady
’s 115-grain Critical Defense ammo. It was only slightly better than the 1.6-inch five-shot group produced by 115-grain American Gunner.
I fired Mozambique drills at seven yards using both the pistol’s iron sights and the Trijicon green dot, and I found the pistol to be above average in terms accuracy. The Dagger’s iron sights are large and co-witness with most optics, but they do take up considerable space in the optic window, which some shooters may not appreciate.

As mentioned, I’m glad PSA was wise enough not to add tritium lamps in these sights. Green tritium vials would fight for your focus next to the sight’s green dot and would be a bit too much in low light.
I found the PSA’s trigger to be similar to a Glock factory trigger’s weight and feel. There’s a bit of take-up with both guns, and after more than 300 rounds the Dagger’s trigger broke at 5.5 pounds, which is within a quarter-pound of my G19 Gen 3.
The Dagger’s grip geometry is outstanding. The slightly rounded grips and single finger groove offer a comfortable and secure hold that allows for maximum control under recoil. PSA’s micro-texturing is modern and effective without being overly aggressive. There’s even a minor cutout in the frame just ahead of the takedown pin that provides a consistent landing spot for the forward thumb. It’s not a “gas pedal,” but this does help keep the muzzle flat during recoil.
Overall reliability was good. The supplied Magpul PMags work great, although for my own shooting I prefer a slightly heavier magazine for a faster and surer mag drop.
Speaking of mags, having 10 of them allowed me to set up all sorts of drills with this pistol. One of them was the one-shot drill where I’d fire a round, drop the mag, reload a mag with a single round, and repeat the process. That’s also where I learned that the Dagger’s slide doesn’t always lock back following the last round.

I also found that the included spring is pretty soft. At times it seemed the spring was almost struggling to strip a round from the magazine and return to battery—although it never actually failed to do so. That’s an easy problem to solve, since compatible springs are so widely available and so cheap.
If this were my gun, swapping springs would be on my to-do list. However, in PSA’s defense this may well have been a media test pistol that had been fired many times before I got it. I’d have to test a brand-new factory gun to be certain.
I really like this pistol and was impressed by its accuracy. The Dagger may be a G19 clone, but it certainly has its own personality. And while the Dagger’s pre-cut slide may not appeal to all shooters, I liked it because the RMR version matched up to a sight I already had—enabling me to mount it to the pistol quickly without the need for special plates.
Summary
I love that simplicity, but it only works if you have a sight that matches up with the available cuts on the Compact. I also like that the Dagger is available with a threaded barrel and has an accessory rail. Shooters like to set up their pistols to meet their specific needs, and this gun allows for that. I think the Dagger Compact with case and 10 magazines at $470 is an outstanding deal and an impressive American-made striker-fired semiauto 9mm pistol.
