A Tasteful Tribute: Ed Brown 1776 Commemorative 1911 Review

Precision Engineering Meets American History in This Premium 45 ACP.

August 20, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

If you’re going to honor this country’s semiquincentennial with a special handgun, it has to be a 1911. Arguing for, say, a Colt Peacemaker won’t cut it: The 1911 has served our armed forces, law enforcement and everyday citizens longer than any other American handgun design. And if you’re Ed Brown Products, the thought of building anything other than a 1911 would never cross your mind in the first place.
The company—which cut its teeth on building the very best parts for 1911
s and then the very best complete guns—has produced two pistols commemorating America’s 250th. The 1776 Hand Engraved was a one-off with full-coverage engraving, gold inlays and mammoth ivory grips. Price tag? It went for a cool $25,000.
The extended thumb safety snicks on and off smartly, and the company’s Memory Groove beavertail grip safety promotes a good grip and sure disengagement. The solid aluminum trigger boasts an excellent break. Ed Brown didn’t go over the top with embellishments, and the left-side engraving is tasteful but still stands out on the satin-finished stainless slide. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The gun you see here is the Ed Brown 1776 Commemorative Edition, a traditional and subtly beautiful pistol with a $3,420 price tag. Unlike the one-of-a-kind, fully engraved model, this gun is a limited edition. Ed Brown Products’ Travis Brown told me they’re going to build and sell them until the end of the year and then end the run.
Features
It is a five-inch Government model, and it’s chambered only to .45 ACP—as is proper since the .45 is the all-American caliber, and it wouldn’t be appropriate to have a U.S. commemorative chambered for that, sniff, European cartridge.

It’s an all-stainless pistol, and its satin finish allows the minimalist embellishments on the slide to stand out. But still, I wondered why such a traditional gun didn’t sport a more traditional blued finish.
“While a blued 1911 is beautiful and as classic as it gets, we are known for our stainless,” Travis explained. “It’s what we started with back in the 2000s with the Kobra Carry, and it’s still, to this day, what we are known for, as the Kobra Carry has been our best-selling gun for over 25 years.”
The slide is a traditional roundtop style with a ball end cut, and “250 Years of freedom 1776-2026” is engraved in the cursive Coronet font on the left side. The right side carries the “Custom by Ed Brown” signature that’s a hallmark of the company’s 1911s.

Befitting a 1911 of this type, there are no front slide serrations. The rear serrations are in a flag pattern, and you might remember these as a feature on the Ed Brown FX2. The American flag-motif serrations look good on the FX2, but they look even better here. The FX2’s slide already has a lot going on—industrial finish, avant-garde forward serrations—but with the Commemorative there’s nothing to distract from the stars and stripes engraving. Further, the choice of this same engraving pattern was a no-brainer, according to Travis.
“We think this is what looked best, and as the saying goes, if it ain’t broke, don’t fix it,” he said.
One cool, subtle touch is the 1776 engraving on the face of the bushing. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
There’s one other aesthetic touch in keeping with the gun’s theme, one you almost might miss: “1776” is tastefully engraved into the front of the stainless steel bushing. I actually didn’t notice it until I’d field-stripped the gun after a day on the range. It’s a subtle detail with a strong visual impact.
Speaking of field-stripping, when you do this you’ll discover the gun employs John Browning’s short-recoil system, although here Ed Brown uses a flat-wire recoil spring instead of the standard round wire. Flat-wire springs promise a longer service life—up to 10 times the lifetime compared to traditional springs, Travis told me—and that will pay off in years of faithful service.
Atop the slide you’ll find Ed Brown’s combat rear sight, which is drift-adjustable with a securing screw in the front. It’s a Novak-style sight with a plain black 0.115-inch square notch that’s recessed so it won’t pick up sun glare. Should you decide to carry your 1776 Commemorative, you’ll appreciate the sight’s rounded edges, which won’t snag on clothing.

The rear is paired with a 0.125-inch-wide black front blade that’s set in a dovetail. The blade sports a gold bead, which is not only stylish but functional. More on this later.
The hand-polished Micarta grip panels are from VZ Grips and have a “burlap” appearance. The mainspring housing and frontstrap are checkered at 25 lpi. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The Frame
The stainless steel frame sports 25 lpi checkering on the frontstrap and the flat mainspring housing. I think this is my favorite checkering pattern because I think it hits the sweet spot between being overly aggressive like 20 lpi and super fine like 30.
The grips are something special. They’re from VZ Grips and made of hand-polished Micarta, a durable laminated material used in knife handles, guitar fingerboards, hard hats and more. The panels exhibit a warm wood color and feature a weave pattern, and they are set off with stainless Torx grip screws.
“The grips were something new that VZ offered, and we thought they did a great job and it really gave it a simple colonial type of look, like a burlap sack,” Travis said.
Hand-Fit Parts
As I alluded to at the outset, Ed Brown Products got its start when namesake founder Ed Brown—an avid handgun competitor and machinist who was unhappy with the quality of the 1911 parts then available—began fashioning his own parts. These finely crafted parts are still at the heart of the company’s 1911s. They’re machined in-house and are oversize in certain areas so the firm’s master gunsmiths can hand-fit them with precision.
The single-side thumb safety clicks on and off with just the right amount of tension, and the serrated button magazine release operates smoothly.
The grip safety is almost a work of art. It sweeps high, allowing you to get your hand deep into the gun. This makes the 1911 point better, and also helps manage recoil because the pistol sits nice and low in your hand so recoil tends to come straight back. The famed Ed Brown Memory Groove makes it easy to deactivate this safety each and every time—even if you have small or slender hands.
The Commander-style hammer nestles into a recess at the top of the grip safety, and the 70 Series trigger is fantastic. It’s a long, solid design, and this one broke like glass at three pounds, one ounce on average. I’m partial to short triggers, so it is a bit long for me, but it didn’t affect my ability to shoot the gun well.

The stars and stripes-motif rear serrations are the same as found on the firm’s FX2 pistol, and they go nicely with the gun’s understated elegance. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Performance
And shoot well it does. As I’ve mentioned several times over the years, I am not the best handgun bench shooter, especially with iron sights. But with the 1776 Commemorative I was able to get some of the smallest 25-yard groups I’ve ever fired with an iron-sighted gun—including a 0.95-incher with 
Hornady
’s American Gunner 185-grain load. All the averages were great, as you can see in the chart.
A lot of the accuracy I was able to achieve was due to the trigger, but I will also point to the excellent sights. Everyone’s eyes are different, and mine aren’t that great anymore, but every once in a while I get to shoot a set of sights that are crystal clear for me. That was the case here. The relationship of the rear notch and front blade has just the right amount of light on either side, promoting pinpoint accuracy.
Today’s trend toward large dots on front sights
, particularly the luminescent ring/tritium vial combo, is definitely a good one when it comes to defensive shooting. However, at least for me, those big dots make it difficult for me to get a laser focus on the top edge of the sight. Here, though, the gold bead doesn’t dominate, and I found it a ton easier to really concentrate on the blade’s top edge.
But make no mistake: The front sight on the 1776 Commemorative is not just for punching paper. A gold-bead front was one of the earliest efforts to create a sight that capably handles a variety of lighting conditions. Its reflective surface and bright color show up well in low light while not washing out or glaring in bright light. It’s far superior to a plain white dot for defensive uses, and it’s a lot classier than a modern day/night dot—making it the right choice for the 1776 Commemorative.
The company’s rear sight is a Novak style, with a recessed plain black notch that won’t pick up glare in bright sunlight. The hammer nestles in a recess in the high-swept grip safety. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Magazines
The pistol ships with two of Ed Brown’s excellent seven-round magazines. They’re made of 0.025-inch 410 stainless steel that’s heat treated for strength and corrosion resistance. The tubes are TIG welded to the bases, and the tubes’ interiors are polished for smooth feeding.

Advertisement

The heat-treated steel followers have extended front skirts and an anti-friction finish. The 1776 Commemorative magazines ship with flat baseplates, but thick (0.590 inch) and thin (0.275 inch) black polymer basepads and installation screws are included.
Price
I’m not gonna lie: $3,420 is a lot to pay for a handgun, and I asked Travis about the sticker price.
As he pointed out, the company machines its parts from “the highest quality steel there is,” and the gun benefits—as most all Ed Brown Products pistols do—from meticulous hand fitting. You can certainly feel the results of this handwork, with a slide-to-frame fit that exhibits zero play, controls that operate perfectly, and outstanding accuracy and 100 percent reliability.
“Compared to our competition, we believe we have the best prices while maintaining the highest standards,” Travis said.
As I’ve written before, when I turned 50 I treated myself to an Ed Brown Special Forces 1911, and while it was a stretch to be able to afford it back then, I’m still glad I bought it and am proud to have it in my collection. Would I buy the 1776 Commemorative? Yes, if I had the money.

Advertisement

If I did buy one, would I shoot it? Again, yes. Now, I wouldn’t take it to a bunch of matches or subject it to daily carry or a lot of dry-fire draw practice. However, it is perhaps the ultimate barbecue gun and definitely one to bring to the range when you and some of your best buddies decide to burn up some ammo together.
Closing Thoughts
The Ed Brown 1776 Commemorative Edition is too good a shooter to live solely in a safe, and it honors the country we call home—celebrating an anniversary unique among the world’s nations. It’s a tastefully done tribute, a well built and accurate gun that you and your heirs can enjoy for years to come. It’ll still be shooting well when the tricentennial comes around—and well beyond.
