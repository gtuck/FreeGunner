Kimber Mountain Ascent Legacy vs. Revival: What Has Changed?

Kimber updated its signature ultra-lightweight rifle and slashed the price. We field-test the new version to see how it measures up to the original's performance.

 Kimber’s Model 84 Mountain Ascent rifle is back after a five-year hiatus, and it sports an all-new carbon-fiber stock. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)

July 16, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

Kimber’s ultra popular 
Mountain Ascent
 rifle is back. “Finally!” I can hear readers saying—and I echo the sentiment. COVID killed the stock supplier that Kimber used pre-2020, and it took a half-decade for the company to bring its lightweight mountain rifle line back into production.
The classic Mountain Ascent model, which I’ll unofficially call the Gen 1 version, was fitted with a carbon-fiber stock that had wonderful classic lines derived from the great British rifles. Open grips, sleek fore-ends with oval cross-sections, and a very lively feel in the hands were hallmarks of those Gen 1 rifles.
Features
In contrast, the new Mountain Ascent comes with an H-S Precision stock. It’s a great pairing. H-S is renowned for quality and innovation, and the company helped pioneer premium composite stocks.
H-S stocks are made of a combination of carbon fiber, fiberglass, and Kevlar. Kimber chose a modernized stock profile for its Gen 2 Mountain Ascent rifle, characterized by a hand-filling vertical grip; a high, parallel comb; a thumb hook/bag cutout at the toe of the stock; and a square-ish, flat-bottomed fore-end with dual sling-swivel studs.

Unlike the Gen 1 carbon-fiber stocks, which were known for shotgun-like handling characteristics that maximized responsiveness, pointability, and balance in offhand and field-type positions like kneeling and sitting, the new Gen 2 stocks are engineered for supported shooting positions, such as prone, or improvised field positions using a bipod propped against some terrain feature. They’re optimal for shooting from a benchrest, as well. It’s a different approach; one that caters more to modern extend
Unlike previous Mountain Ascent rifles, which were glass bedded, the new version utilizes a machined aluminum bedding block molded into the stock. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
ed-range rifle shooting methods and less to traditional hunting methods.
Tech Specs
In addition to modern stock dimensions and ergonomics, the H-S Precision stock utilizes a different system to mate to the barreled action. Rather than being glass bedded (Kimber used to use a slave action to glass bed every stock), the H-S stocks feature a machined aluminum bedding block. It’s strong and rigid but does not provide quite the snug, near-perfect individual fit that glass bedding does.

A more tangible downside to the use of an aluminum bedding block (at least in the ultralight mountain-rifle world) is added weight. H-S Precision does not list the weight of this stock on its website, so I pulled the one off my test rifle and put it on my scale. It tipped the balance beam at an eyebrow-raising 34 ounces. That’s 10 ounces more than the 24-ounce stock on a Gen 1 Mountain Ascent rifle. Plus, there’s an additional 5 ounces for the hinged bottom metal that the Gen 1 didn’t have. In essence, the new Gen 2 Mountain Ascent model weighs roughly a pound more than the original Gen 1 version did.
Whether that’s a problem is a matter of personal opinion. Candidly, since the rest of the Mountain Ascent rifle is so diligently optimized for extremely light weight, my opinion is that Kimber would have been well-served to use a lighter stock. Best-in-class carbon-fiber stocks for true mountain rifles typically weigh 20 ounces or thereabouts, with custom work from masters of the trade like Rifles Inc. tipping the scales at a mere 11 ounces.
It’s worth pointing out that the new Gen 2 Mountain Ascent rifles are about $300 to $500 less than the Gen 1 Mountain Ascent rifles. In a world of ever-inflating cost of living and prices, that’s a beautiful thing.
Unlike original Mountain Ascent models, the new versions are fitted with hinged floorplates rather than blind magazines. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Moving on. In terms of metal configuration, aside from the addition of a hinged floorplate, the new Gen 2 Mountain Ascent is very much like the original, and the action is most impressive. Not only are the 84L (long action for .30-06 family cartridges) and 84M (short action for .308 Winchester family cartridges) smaller in diameter than most bolt-action designs, they are aggressively skeletonized to reduce weight. The result is a superbly petite all-steel action.
You can purchase a Mountain Ascent in 7mm Rem. Mag. or .300 Win. Mag., but those rifles are built on Kimber’s full-diameter 8400 action. They still feature weight-reducing machining but are much larger, considerably heavier full-size actions.

Kimber’s actions are true controlled-round-feeding designs. They feature Mauser-type full-length rotating claw extractors and mechanical fixed ejectors, both of which are highly prized by dangerous-game specialists around the world. (When an action type is preferred by those whose lives depend on their rifles, one should take note.)
Savants of rifle actions value the three-position, wing-type safety of the Winchester Model 70 style that’s utilized on Kimber’s rifles. Unlike typical two-position rocker-style safeties on Remington 700-type rifles, Kimber’s safeties actually lock and block the firing pin. Even if the trigger mechanism fails and releases the cocking piece assembly, the firing pin cannot fall and detonate the cartridge in the chamber. It’s an extremely safe design.
Another much-appreciated feature of the three-position safety is that when it’s in the rearward “Safe” position, the bolt is locked closed. The middle “Safe” position allows you to work the bolt so you can clear a live round from the chamber with the safety engaged.
Barrels are slender and long-ish, particularly for this era of hunters who covet suppressor compatibility. Short-action cartridges are paired with 22-inch barrels. Long-action versions, such as the .270 Win., .30-06, and .280 Ackley, have 24-inch barrels. And the 7mm Rem. Mag. and .300 Win. Mag. rifles have 26-inch barrels. That’s not counting the included radial muzzle brakes.
From the first Mountain Ascent rifle that hit the market, this model has featured half-fluted barrels. Clean, straight flutes are machined into the rear half of the barrel where there’s enough wall thickness to support it. The flutes stop just forward of the fore-end tip because barrel walls become too thin.
Being ultraskinny in diameter also affects the muzzle threads. Rather than having standard 5/8-24 threads or even the 1/2-28 threads common to .22 centerfire rifles, the Mountain Ascent rifles have 7/16-28 threads. That’s very small indeed.
If you’re wondering whether you can remove the muzzle brake and install a suppressor, the answer is yes. However, you’ll have to purchase a special 7/16-28 direct-thread insert for your suppressor.
The Gen 2 Kimber Mountain Ascent rifles feature H-S Precision stocks made of carbon fiber, fiberglass, and Kevlar. The stocks feature a comfortable near-vertical grip and a rear bag cutout. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Another difference from the original Mountain Ascent models is the addition of Kimber’s well-respected KimPro II finish. It’s a proprietary finish that’s corrosion- and abrasion-resistant. The original models were all stainless steel, as are these newer versions, but even stainless can corrode and scratch. Additional protection is never a bad thing, particularly since we’re getting it for less than the cost of the original Mountain Ascent.

Range Test
To wring out my new Mountain Ascent 84L Peak chambered in .280 Ackley, I installed a Zeiss 3-18X 50mm Conquest V6 scope that I’ve been saving for a special rifle. My preference is to mount optics on lightweight mountain rifles using a one-piece ring/base combination like Talley’s lightweight alloy rings, but the only 30mm ones I had on hand were too low. Mounted in them, the 50mm objective bell on the Zeiss scope contacted the barrel. So instead, I installed a Picatinny-type one-piece rail made by Leupold and mounted the scope in extra-low Seekins Precision rings. I have to point out that the rail restricts access to the magazine, so it’s a bit fiddly to top-load the rifle.
Up front I mounted a Spartan Guide adapter and popped a Javelin bipod on the fore-end tip. After bore-sighting the rifle, I fired a shot, made an adjustment to the scope, and cut the X in the 100-yard bullseye with my next shot. What an auspicious start!
With a bunny-ear sandbag wedged under the toe of the H-S Precision stock, I began firing three-shot groups at the 100-yard target. Because I like thin-jacketed, soft and heavy bullets for breaking in a barrel, I started with 
Hornady
’s 162-grain ELD-X ammo. Right from the get-go it was obvious that it was going to meet Kimber’s sub-MOA accuracy guarantee. Sure enough, three consecutive three-shot groups measured an average of 0.89 inch. Muzzle velocity was mild but adequate, clocking 2,848 fps.
My first impression was that even though the new Mountain Ascent is a relatively light rifle, recoil was pleasantly soft. This is in part due to the radial muzzle brake, but without question the design of the H-S Precision stock helped too.
The next load I tested gave some trouble. It was 
Weatherby
’s factory-loaded 150-grain Swift Scirocco II ammo, and it proved stubborn about both chambering and extracting. Scuffs around the rear half of ejected casings suggested that either the chamber—advertised as “match grade”—is a bit too snug, or there’s a burr around the back edge of the chamber. Either way, tolerance with the Weatherby ammo was a bit too tight to allow the ammo to feed and extract smoothly. Stubbornly, I pressed on and got my three groups fired for average.

Next, I tested Barnes’s 152-grain LRX factory load. It, too, was a bit snug in the rifle’s chamber but not so snug that I couldn’t run it through the rifle fairly well. This ammo turned in both the best accuracy and the lowest standard deviation in speed. Muzzle velocity was impressive at 3,020 fps.
Two out of the five loads tested in the new Kimber Mountain Ascent met the sub-MOA accuracy guarantee. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Nosler’s 160-grain Partition ammo posted an accuracy average just above an inch but was fast too—3,040 fps. Feeding and function was tight and questionable.
The only other load that fed, fired, and extracted as smoothly as the Hornady ammo was Federal’s 155-grain Terminal Ascent ammo. It has nickel-plated cases, and it was the smoothest cycling of all the loads. I suspect this is due to the surface hardness and natural lubricity of the nickel plating. (For readers interested in how the Gen 2 Mountain Ascent 84M Mojave in 6.5 Creedmoor performed in the field, see David Faubion’s hunt report starting on page 44 in this magazine.)
Wrestling with the action to get it to feed and eject left me a bit disenchanted. Of the dozens of other Kimber rifles I’ve tested over the past 20 years, I’ve never experienced anything but smooth feeding and function.
My inclination is to give this new Mountain Ascent the benefit of the doubt. Hopefully the issue is limited to this rifle and not widespread. I’m going to get a gunsmith to examine the .280 Ackley Improved chamber with a borescope, and if there’s a simple burr around the rear, it’s an easy fix. If, on the other hand, the chamber is too snug for 60 percent of the factory ammo on my shelf, that’s a more serious issue and may indicate that Kimber’s minimum-spec, match-grade chamber is worn and needs to be replaced.

When the dust settled, my biggest takeaway was how good the H-S Precision stock feels. I do not love the way it looks, but functionally it’s superb. Being a mountain-rifle addict, I’m also not a fan of the extra pound that the stock and the bottom metal added to the new Mountain Ascent, but I do like the fact that it’s several hundred dollars less expensive than the original iterations. That’s a big win for hunters.
Ten years ago, the Mountain Ascent was the tip of the spear in the world of extreme ultralight mountain rifles. Cost, at around $2,200, reflected that, as did its 4.9- to 5.4-pound weight. Now, a plethora of other manufacturers occupy that realm, and they are offering some very nice cutting-edge rifles. The race to the bottom in terms of weight has outclassed the original Mountain Ascent, too; 4-pound rifles are now factory produced. Prices run from $2,200 to $4,000 for standard-production models, and they are so popular gun shops can’t keep them in stock.
Kimber seems to have positioned the new Mountain Ascent in a more moderate role. It’s an ergonomic, light-enough rifle at a slightly more affordable price. It has a rigid stock that will help hunters shoot the rifle well. And here’s what I like best—Kimber offers it in my all-time favorite Western big-game cartridge: the grand .280 Ackley Improved.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
