Titanium Featherweight: Inside the 4-Pound Weatherby Mark V Backcountry Capra

How Weatherby built the lightest hunting rifle without sacrificing performance.

 Weatherby’s new Backcountry Capra mountain gun is endowed with the best weight-reducing features and components available. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)

July 21, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

Not many standard-production rifles have ever weighed less than about 5 pounds. Of course, currently, there are the New Ultralight Arms (NULA) rifles made by Wilson Combat, the original Kimber Mountain Ascent, and the Howa Superlite rifles sold through Legacy Sports that weigh slightly less than 5 pounds. Now there is the brand-new 
Weatherby
 Backcountry Capra that weighs just 4 pounds in its lightest form. The Capra—named for the Latin genus of mountain-dwelling wild goats and a play on the acronym G.O.A.T—is absolutely the lightest rack-ready centerfire hunting rifle ever brought to market.
Weatherby has always pushed the boundaries, and the company’s pursuit of the ultimate mountain rifle is no surprise. It doesn’t hurt that the quest is also fueled by company president Adam Weatherby’s love for hunting in wild, challenging country.
Mark V Capra rifles are endowed with the best weight-reducing features and components available. Metal is titanium wherever possible and is fluted and skeletonized aggressively. Stock material is thin, premium carbon fiber. Diameters and shapes are optimized not just to weigh little, but to weigh the least possible.
I’ll dig into the features and specifications of the Capra, but first it’s worth addressing why the market needs and wants a 4-pound rifle. Because, to the surprise of bona fide mountain hunters everywhere, there are some very vocal detractors of lightweight rifles.

Why Go Ultralight?
Lightweight hunting rifles are currently trendy. From a business standpoint, that alone is reason enough to bring the best of the type to market. This is in large part driven by the popularity of backcountry hunting and in part as a side-effect of the suppressor movement. (And yes, in case you’ve been living under the proverbial rock, perhaps the only hunting-related tools that are more trendy than lightweight rifles are suppressors.)
Installing a suppressor on the end of a hunting rifle invariably adds 8 to 20 ounces, depending on the specific suppressor. If you start with a lightweight rifle, you’ll end up with a hunting tool you can still comfortably tote at high altitude and in rigorous terrain.
Modern scopes, too, tend to be heavy. Unlike the 12-ounce 3-9Xs our grandfathers used, the optics many modern hunters mount atop their rifles are more akin to a Hubble telescope. Sporting over-zealous magnification, dial-up turrets, side-focus parallax adjustment, and often heavy main tubes of 34mm, many so-called hunting scopes weigh 2 pounds or more.

The point of all this is that if you start with a wand-like ultralight rifle, you can add that cutting-edge suppressor and a big scope and still end up with a hunting rifle of acceptable weight.
Sheep-hunting and gun-writing legend Jack O’Connor wrote that a 7.5-pound rifle—complete with scope, sling, and magazine loaded with cartridges—was just about right, and it provided a system light enough to pack up big mountains yet still shootable enough to make good clean shots.
O’Connor used a simple leather strap for his sling and a compact fixed-power or low-end variable riflescope that weighed little. Still, his bare rifle had to weigh 6.5 pounds or less to achieve a combined weight of 7.5 pounds.
If we tally the total, even with the best light-ish modern scopes on the market—such as Leupold’s 20-ounce VX-6HD 3-18X 44mm—plus a 12-ounce suppressor, plus a 4-ounce mountain-weight sling and three to five cartridges, plus a well-built scope base and strong rings that add another 6 to 8 ounces, modern hunters generally add 48-plus ounces to the bare rifle. Yep, up to 3 whole pounds.
Use one of the popular so-called “precision hunting” scopes by Vortex, Nightforce, Revic, and the like, and that add-on weight will be more like 4 pounds.

If you start with the same 6.5-pound rifle O’Connor had, you’ll end up with a 9.5- to 10.5-pound rifle.
However, if you start with a 4- to 4.5-pound rifle, you can still meet that 7.5 pounds. And let me tell you from experience, folks, that it’s a wonderful thing to carry a 7.5-pound rifle fully equipped with your favorite suppressor and a super-capable scope. I guarantee Mr. O’Connor would have been a fan.
Weatherby is fluting Capra barrels with a double-helix pattern, and the barrels are free-floated in the stock for consistent accuracy. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
The Capra Up Close
For the ultimate compact Capra, Weatherby engineered a short-action version of its outstanding six-lug Mark V action. The six-lug Mark V is one of the most petite actions ever made, and it is in fact too slender to house traditional magnum cartridges with traditional magnum-size case heads.
All Capra actions are made of titanium. And yes, Weatherby does both short- and long-action versions of the Capra. Short-action rifles are chambered in .308 and several of the Creedmoor-family cartridges. Long-action versions are chambered in .240 WBY Magnum, .25 WBY RPM (Rebated Precision Magnum), 6.5 RPM, .270 Win., and .280 Ackley Improved.
Barrel length was carefully researched and optimized to provide maximum weight savings while giving up no more velocity than acceptable. As a result, lengths are somewhat unconventional. Short-action Capra rifles wear 17-inch barrels, while long-action Capras have 21-inch barrels.
These barrels feature a double-helix type of fluting, which Weatherby touts as providing as much stiffness as possible while weighing as little as possible. For those who question whether the wispy “pencil” (or “noodle”) barrels have the potential for good accuracy, rest your concerns. All Capra rifles come with a sub-MOA accuracy guarantee.
Yes, that’s impressive—and some would say brave of Weatherby. Read on, dear shooter. You may find that the accuracy test results of the Capra I reviewed for this report surprise you.
Barrel muzzles are threaded 1/2-28 and come fitted with a slender radial muzzle brake. A thread cap is included with each rifle for those who don’t want to use the brake or install a suppressor. As an aside, the 1/2-28 thread pitch is more commonly used for .22-caliber centerfire rifles than for larger calibers, but it’s the largest that can be used and still leave an adequate shoulder for a suppressor to square up against.

Advertisement

All Capra actions are machined out of titanium and are aggressively worked to eliminate all superfluous material. Bolt bodies are deeply fluted, and bolt knobs are hollowed out.
In a rare move, Weatherby gave the Capra model a weight-reducing blind magazine. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Unlike most Mark V rifles, the Capra is built without a floorplate. Instead, a simple trigger bow protects the user-adjustable TriggerTech trigger, and a blind magazine is created inside the stock. Savvy from decades of hunting in ultrachallenging environmental conditions, Weatherby drills a small weep hole in the bottom of each blind magazine to allow rainwater and accumulated water from snow and ice to drain out.
A brand-new ultralight stock was designed specifically for the Capra by Weatherby’s sister company, Peak 44. Named the Bridger stock, it’s even lighter than Peak 44’s extraordinary Blacktooth stock. Uniquely, it features finger grooves in the fore-end and inset panels molded into the buttstock—both of which reduce weight while providing increased rigidity.
Weatherby’s own hex recoil pad contributes to the ultralight magic. It’s the lightest functional recoil pad in existence, and while I don’t love how it looks, I do find it effective in taking the bite out of recoil.
Less visible characteristics, such as a subtle negative comb, free-floated barrel channel, and abbreviated fore-end, add ergonomics and function. One acquaintance suggested the Bridger stock is ugly. I do not find it ugly at all—and even if I did, the performance it provides is attractive.

A Special Rifle Requires a Special Test
Although most hunters will set up their Capra as described earlier—with a capable precision scope, suppressor, and so forth—I decided to take a different direction and test the sleek ultralight rifle in its true form, as the lightest production-made hunting rifle on the market. My goal was to put together a 4.5-pound hunting rifle, complete with scope. This would enable me to evaluate whether it is actually too light for accurate shooting.
I was fascinated by the idea of a 300-yard-capable, 4.5-pound rifle suitable for hunting deer and elk. So, I made it my intention to first accuracy-test the rifle in clinical conditions and then accuracy-test myself with the rifle on vital-size steel targets out to 300 yards, and perhaps 400 yards if all went well.
My test Capra arrived chambered in 
6.5 Creedmoor
. While the 6.5 Creedmoor is not as interesting to me as the newer .22 and .25 Creedmoor and not as classic as the .308 Win., I do appreciate its versatility. It’s perfect for whitetails anywhere and of course all the proper mountain game like wild sheep. Plus, with correct bullet selection it can capably step up to elk.
Before installing a scope, I put the Capra on my scale. It weighs 4 pounds, 1 ounce. To keep the rifle as light as possible, I chose a lovely Leupold 2.5X FX scope, which weighs a tad less than 5 ounces. I mounted it in Talley lightweight alloy rings/bases, which weigh a whisker over 2 ounces.
Holding my breath, I put the scoped Capra back on the scale. 
Voilá!
 It weighs 4.5 pounds exactly.

Advertisement

After some consideration, I secured a Spartan Guide adapter for my 6-ounce Javelin Pro Hunt bipod to the front sling-swivel stud. The adapter added an additional 1.5 ounces, but I’m pretty entrenched in hunting with the little bipod and preferred to accuracy-test the Capra with it rather than over a sandbag.
After bore-sighting the scope, I fired a three-shot group with my son William observing through a spotting scope. Impacts weren’t visible through my low-power scope, but when William leaned back from the spotter, he had a surprised look on his face and declared, “You’re about 5 inches high, but that’s a sub-inch group!”
When the dust cleared, I’d tested four different factory loads through the Capra, and two of them had handily met Weatherby’s sub-MOA accuracy guarantee. Federal’s 130-grain Terminal Ascent load took top honors, averaging 0.82 inch for three consecutive three-shot groups.
Velocity was low, as expected, due to the shorter-than-usual 17-inch barrel. Predictably, ammo ranged from about 140 to 250 fps slower than advertised on the boxes.
To test reliability, I usually load all rounds into the magazine and feed from it as I shoot a group, just as I will while hunting. This enables me to find any quirks in the rifle and to get accustomed to the feel of fresh cartridges feeding from the magazine and into the chamber. To my surprise, I found that I could load three rounds into the Capra’s magazine but could not close the bolt over them. The top one had to run into the chamber. Checking the specs, I discovered for the first time that the Capra is rated as having two plus one capacity.

Advertisement

That actually surprised me. The Capra doesn’t seem particularly shallow through the midriff—a condition that would have indicated reduced room inside. While I wasn’t necessarily concerned by the limited capacity, it was the first characteristic of the Capra that I didn’t like. I have a couple of fine custom ultralightweight rifles built by masters of the art, and both hold four rounds in their blind magazines.
On a more favorable note, the flyweight rifle fed, fired, and extracted flawlessly throughout my testing. I took a moment to adjust the TriggerTech trigger down to 2.88 pounds in pull weight.
Weatherby’s sister company Peak 44 designed an all-new stock for the Capra, called the Bridger model. It features an indented stiffening panel in the buttstock, a negative slant to the comb, and a Hex recoil pad. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Recoil is brisk because there’s little mass in the Capra to dampen it, but it is not punishing. Lively in the hands, quick to the shoulder, and natural to point, the rifle is a joy to carry. I will say that its feather-like weight makes it difficult to hold steady on target while shooting from the standing position.
Moving off the shooting bench, I wandered out on my farm where I have 15-inch round steel targets hung at 100-yard intervals. I’d zeroed the Capra dead on at 200 yards. Lying prone in the grass, I fired and sure enough, the bullet centered the 200-yard target.
My ballistic calculator showed 8 inches of drop at 300 yards. A bit of wind cooled my left cheek, so I held for 6 inches of drift and fired. My bullet impacted a tad left of center, suggesting I’d over-compensated for wind. Still, it would have 10-ringed a deer, let alone an elk.

Advertisement

Heat waves made the 400-yard steel target shimmy like a hula dancer. Even with the 2.5X scope, the steel gong appeared so small it was hard to aim at. Plus, I couldn’t dial for distance because of the capped turret. Holding as close as I could figure to 23 inches above center and on the left edge for wind drift, I squeezed my shot off—and rang the gong cleanly. Impact was a fraction right of center.
Holy moly! I’d just proved to myself that with a 4.5-pound rifle equipped with a 2.5X scope, I could make first-round hits on elk vitals out to 400 yards. Heck, all my shots would have nearly centered a deer’s vitals, too. I was elated.
That said, that 400-yard gong had clearly showed me that the fixed-power 2.5X scope has limitations. I’d be happy hunting with it when shots were expected to be no more than 300 yards. However, if I expected to shoot any farther, I’d swap the scope for an 11-ounce Leupold 2.5-8X VX-3HD with CDS-ZL turret. With that scope, total weight of the Capra would increase to right at 5 pounds. I think even the pickiest mountain hunter would find that acceptable.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
