America's Rifle: The Colt M5

The Colt M5 Carbine is a worthy successor to the M4. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

July 03, 2026

By Jack Oller

I experienced immense pride after swearing in, but it took some time before I felt like a soldier. Upon the oath of enlistment, one enters the U.S. Army. Swearing in may not qualify one for active service, but the oath is sacred. Once spoken before an officer appointed by the commander-­in-­chief, the formal commitment is binding — and it does not have an expiration.
It’s impossible not to feel martial inclinations after being handed and tasked with the care and custody of a service rifle. My M4 was manufactured, as many are, by Colt.
Bilateral controls on the M5 Carbine offer familiar accessibility for both left- and right-handed shooters. Added material reinforced the bolt catch/release, mag release, and long trigger pins. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Once serial numbers were confirmed, drill sergeants began an elaborate ritual. Standing before the company, we ceremoniously named our carbines. 
The experience impressed a sense of ownership and the need for accountability. Most named our arms for girlfriends, crushes or spouses back home. A similar emotion accompanied the receipt of my next M4, just ahead of my 2014 deployment to the Camp VI Detention Facility at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba. I named that carbine, too.

For the third time in my life, I now feel the urge to name a gun. I’m not in the habit of naming firearms, but it’s tradition at this point. This gun evokes similar sentiments as those I remember while serving in the Army. It’s not exactly a service rifle — yet — but the name suggests it wants to be: 
Colt M5 Carbine
. A sample was issued to Guns & Ammo for review, and I received the assignment. The name I’ve given the M5 is “Kali,” a shout-­out to my beautiful wife.
The two-stage trigger, hammer, safety selector, and springs are standard for the M5. The trigger housing maintains a wall behind the safety to prevent installation of a select-fire sear assembly. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A lot of technical information accompanies the Colt M5 Carbine. It’s not just an M4 with a fresh coat of paint. Colt has been recording feedback on the M4 since it entered service in 1994. The M5 was soft launched in 2018, and Colt did the same. Foreign special forces were the first to receive the M5 for several years and valuable feedback was received. For 2026, the M5 and Enhanced Patrol Rifle (EPRII) were improved and reintroduced for law enforcement and commercial customers.
The Lower
The lower receiver exhibits the most visually obvious changes, which are almost entirely bilateral. The only exceptions could be the bolt catch, which can only be engaged on the left side, and the forward-­assist button. Otherwise, the bolt release, magazine release and safety selector are confirmed “ambi.” The bolt and mag release use a push-­button pivoting lever design instead of a sweeping-­lever linkage-­type action. Pushing is more natural since many of us are already comfortable with using a button in this manner to release an AR’s magazine. I found that operating the bolt release with my shooting finger on the right side was faster during reloading drills than a left-­side bolt-­release slap. Its placement was instinctual.

Proven and popular with users of M4- and AR-15-pattern carbines, the Colt M5 wears a no-rattle, adjustable Magpul CTR stock complete with sling slots and socket-style attachment points. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
From the perspective of a right-­handed shooter, the left-­side mag release was also ideally placed for reloading a mag from the prone position. I confirmed that the controls on the M5 worked as intended for weak-­side shooting. Another subtle improvement made to the lower was additional material incorporated around each control’s spring and pin housing to fence in and safeguard against unintended activation and increase overall durability. As with the upper receiver and handguard, the lower is made of forged 7075-­series aluminum and finished with a Type III hardcoat anodizing.
Aluminum was built up around the trigger pins, too. Colt utilizes longer-­than-­industry-­standard trigger pins to decrease wear. Longer pins don’t need to be captured due to the extra support material in the lower. Also, the longer pins are not necessarily more compatible with cassette triggers since those typically need the captured pins since those typically need the captured pins connected by a bar to have the same anti-wear properties. The two-stage trigger measured 5 pounds, 4.3 ounces, more than acceptable at the range for shooting groups.
Despite the Magpul CTR stock and MOE Enhanced triggerguard, the M5 retains the standard A2-pattern grip. Above it is Colt’s two-position bilateral safety selector. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Familiar Handling
Colt turned to Magpul for the stock, triggerguard and 30-­round PMAG. The CTR Carbine Stock is an obvious choice given that it’s an established design in the military, law enforcement (LE) and commercial market. The MOE Enhanced triggerguard is an interesting addition to the Colt carbine knowing that many of us served with the flat, swing-­down aluminum triggerguard for decades. It turns out that sometimes it’s just simpler to partner with Magpul for a better mousetrap instead of trying to beat the patent game.
The one piece on the lower that looks out of place against the Magpul furniture is the A2-­style grip, which almost appears retro in comparison. There are a lot of variances in personal preference, though. Knowing that most of us will likely replace the A2 grip with another preferred grip, some cost savings was applied here.
The M5 barrel nut and gas tube are distinctive. The barrel indexing pin was relocated inside the receiver. The barrel nut and receiver interface center and true the barrel’s alignment when tightened. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The M5 Carbine buffer tube contains a standard recoil spring and H2 buffer. I noted that the M5 retained many parts we know on the M4. Used in more than 90 countries, the Colt M4 and its clones have a lot of supply parts scattered around the globe. There was no need for Colt to challenge supply chains and replace every part on the M5, so enduring components such as the buffer spring assembly didn’t get updated.

Over The Top
The upper receiver is where Colt engineered some magic. Inspiration came from the upper on the proven Colt Monolithic Carbine (CR/LE 6940). The handguard on the M5 is not one piece as it is on the Monolithic Carbine, but it was designed to fit and function with similar uniformity. A traditional barrel nut does not secure the barrel and support the handguard. Instead, Colt extended the upper receiver beyond the standard, over-­the-­barrel nut. The handguard attaches directly to the upper receiver and almost perfectly free-­floats the barrel.
The M5 features a heavy-profile barrel lacking M203 cuts. The gas block features a bent carbine gas tube. Gas ports are aligned and the gas block is secured to the barrel with tapered pins. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A product of this design approach means that heat is drawn away from the barrel nut and moves down toward the front of the handguard. Heat is better drawn away from the action, and due to the increased surface area of the handguard it doesn’t get too hot unless you mag-­dump several 30 rounders. I swapped the Colt M5 lower for one containing a forced reset trigger (FRT) to function test the upper receiver’s durability under continuous rapid fire through several magazines. The handguard of the M5 did not become unreasonably hot in my bare hand. Of course, extended full-­auto fire greatly increases the temperature around the handguard, but the M5 forend effectively drew heat away from the upper receiver and spread it out.
Another advantage to the M5 upper receiver design is the barrel index pin. Colt moved it to the receiver rather than its usual location on the barrel as it is on the M4 and most AR-­pattern rifles. On the M5, the barrel self-­centers and self-­trues to the receiver, reducing the possibility of so-­called “flyers” and accuracy bias. The barrel nut isn’t timed to the gas tube, either.
The front post is elevation adjustable and the flip-aperture is windage adjustable.(Photo by Mark Fingar)
Since the M5 handguard is so securely mated to the receiver and uniform in alignment, laser sights and night-­vision optics can be removed and re-­attached to the handguard with minimal shift. It’s always recommended that you confirm zero hasn’t changed, but only minimal adjustments should ever be necessary for accessories attached to the handguard. For those who occasionally remove the handguard, the M5’s is secured with T-­30 Torx fasteners instead of Hex- or Allen-­head screws.
The Bullet Tube
The Colt M5 Carbine features a heavy-­profile barrel with light-profile taper behind the gas block. It’s still made of a secret steel alloy slurry, and inside the bore is still chrome lined. Both made M4 barrels famously durable. Colt indicated that its exact metallurgy recipe for the barrel steel is secret, but we assume its 4150 Chrome Moly Vanadium (CMV) steel per military specification. It has a 1-­in-­7-­inch twist rate, and the barrel is complete with a manganese phosphate finish.
Troy Industries BattleSights are folding to support optics, but as stand-alone sights they are durable and accurate. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A difference between the M4 and M5 barrels is the barrel taper extending to the muzzle. Historically, M4 barrels could bend when paired with heavy or hard-­recoiling attachments such as the M203 grenade launcher, or subject to violent impacts from hard objects such as turrets and steel doors or hatches. The thicker profile on the M5 came as military feedback urged Colt to address this issue. The thicker, almost free-float barrel with subtle taper also benefited the accuracy potential of the M5. In the hands of soldiers, modern grenade launchers primarily attach to the M-­Lok handguard and don’t stress the barrel, further isolating it. As such, the M5 does not include M203 cuts, or a bayonet attachment point for that matter. Colt does offer military customers an M-­Lok bayonet attachment for use on the M5.
On the civilian-­version of the M5, the gas port is the standard size, feeding gas into a standard carbine-­length stepped tube. Other gas port sizes are used with different barrel lengths and chamberings, though. The gas block is secured with tapered pins — not screws. With the 15.1-­inch handguard, the original fixed front sight and gas block was changed. In its place are a set of folding sights. With the front sight mounted closer to the muzzle, the sight radius was increased. Be prepared to use the full adjustment range to zero the irons. My windage adjustment placed the rear sight almost completely to the left side of the housing. For left-­handers, I would predict that you will need to adjust the rear all the way to the right. There’s nothing wrong with this, but it can be surprising to people who typically shoot red dot sights or scopes exclusively.
Colt turned to Radian to equip the M5 with its Raptor charging handle. It’s an ambi-­type charging handle that works smoothly and is efficient to grasp with either hand.

The Colt M5 upper also retains the forward assist. It’s arguably unnecessary today, but it’s still required by most military contracts, so we civilians have it as well. The bolt assembly and carrier group are also untouched from the M4. Made of Carpenter 158 steel, this is another legacy part that didn’t require an update, according to Colt. These standardized components benefit potential customers who keep an inventory of M4 spares.
At The Range
The first thing I noticed with the M5 Carbine is how well-­balanced it was. The weight is just shy of 7 pounds, about the same as the M4. However, the M5 felt more heavy at the rear and light in the support hand. It’s more likely than not that most LE and military will run the M5 with a suppressor; the same may eventually be true for civilians. Attaching a heavy can to the muzzle shifted the balance and made it more comfortable to level the sights for long strings of fire.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
With and without a suppressor attached, the M5 ran flawlessly. No brass catcher was needed. I could’ve set a small box to my 4 o’clock position and caught almost every piece. Ejection sent the brass toward the 2 o’clock position when suppressed, equally consistent.
I used a shot timer to determine how much faster I could perform reloads with the bilateral controls. From a left-­side hip-postioned magazine holster, I averaged a 2.3-­second reload with a shot on target. From my plate carrier, I averaged 2.8 seconds. On that note, the Colt M5 was comfortable to shoot with a plate carrier, too.
The M5 demonstrated a preference for heavier 5.56 NATO loads. The Black Hills 77-­grain OTM turned in one five-­shot group that measured just more than half an inch. One strange occurrence was with the Fiocchi 62-­grain FMJ. My Garmin C2 chronograph displayed extreme spreads (ES) and standard deviations (SD) in the hundreds, whereas accurate rifles typically produce numbers in the single digits. The ES and SDs were normal for the other loads, including Fiocchi’s 77-­grain SBR Enhanced round. Regardless of load, again, the M5 never malfunctioned.

Parting Shots
I thought I was long over my M4, but I’ve always had yearning for another Colt. The M5 Carbine is simple when compared to more expensive carbines, but I actually prefer it. Too many frills don’t always equate to a superior firearm. Mainly, I love the feeling the M5 gives me. It is reminiscent of the M4 carbines I served fondly with. I loathe cliches, but I’m compelled to borrow from the Marines’ “Rifleman Creed”: “There are many like it, but this one is mine.”
