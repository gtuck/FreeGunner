Browning X-Bolt 2 Speed in 6.5 PRC: Full Review

Desert sheep Hunting with a Browning X-­Bolt 2 Speed in 6.5 PRC — Made especially for Lefties.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

August 05, 2026

By Craig Boddington

Gunwriters and farmers are a bit alike. We never know when dry spells might come or when it’s going to pour. Browning’s X-­Bolt has been around since 2008. In these pages, I did one of the early stories on the X-­Bolt. Nice rifle. It was an improvement on the long-­running and excellent A-­Bolt. I was especially impressed by its super-­strong eight-­screw scope mounting system, which, if you look at the naked receiver rings from above, gives it the signature “X.”
Then came a dry spell. For 15 years I had little interaction with the X-­Bolt. No reason, our paths just didn’t cross much. The rains came with 
2024’s X-­Bolt 2
. During the last 18 months, I’ve spent a lot of time with three different X-­Bolt 2 Speed rifles and hunted with all.
The X-­Bolt 2 retains the three-­lug bolt with short uplift and two-­position tang safety. However, it was more than just a cosmetic facelift. Significant changes include a different bolt body and receiver, a slim detachable rotary magazine, and a bolt-­unlock button at the root of the bolt handle.
More Speed
Browning prides itself on being an upgraded line. I think of most of its products as falling into the broad “medium” price range. As such, there are other companies that sell larger numbers of firearms. However, Browning has never been shy about offering a wide range of options. I went to 
Browning's website
 and came up 
with some 69 X-­Bolt 2 variants.
 Wow! Wood, synthetic, blue, stainless, long barrel, short barrel, heavy barrel, extended detachable box magazine, action lengths scaled to cartridges, and more! You can have an X-­Bolt 2 any way you want it.

The X-­Bolt 2 Speed has been especially popular. As a hunter, not a competitor, thinking deer-­sized game and elk — not varmints — the Speed appeals to me. At less than 7 pounds stripped, it’s light but not whippy. Although I’m far from modern, I like the modern, versatile Browning OVIX camo on the composite stock. This is Browning’s Vari-­Tech stock, adjustable for comb height and length of pull, with inserts to change the grip angle from a gentle laid-­back pistol grip to the near-­vertical grip now preferred by many. (I stick with “laid back.”) The finish is tough, attractive, non-­glare Smoked Bronze Cerakote. The slender barrel is fluted, threaded M13x.75, and comes with a supplied muzzlebrake and thread protector. Barrel length varies with the chambering.
My first X-­Bolt Speed was a short-­action .308 Winchester with 22-­inch barrel. It had the ideal weight and was accurate. I did some hog hunting with it and bought it for our Kansas deer camp. It accounted for a couple nice whitetails during our 2025 Kansas season. The second was a suppressor version, identical except for 20-­inch barrel and 6.8 Western chambering. Also very accurate, averaging just more than a half-­inch for five three-­shot groups, and 1¼ inches for five five-­shot groups. It flattened an elk last fall. I intended to take it on a late deer hunt, but that didn’t materialize. With that kind of accuracy, I was dithering whether I should buy it or return it when I went to the 2026 SHOT Show. The only problem I had with either the .308 or the 6.8 Western Speed rifles: Both had right-­hand bolts.
The pictures are not reversed! In 2026, Browning released several left-­hand X-­Bolt 2 models. The ejection port, bolt handle, and bolt-­release button above the handle were engineered for lefties. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Mirror-Image
To my surprise and delight, at the ’26 SHOT Show, Browning unveiled the left-­hand X-­Bolt 2. From here on I may lose some of you. I know that most of the right-­hand majority don’t give a darn about left-­hand guns. Got it, but bear with me. Like most lefties, I can operate a wrong-­handed bolt almost as fast by reaching over the top with my left hand — maybe faster if I’m on a solid rest and run the bolt with my right hand. The only thing — and not just for lefties but anyone with a left-­handed child, spouse, partner, buddy — there is a safety factor. In the case of a catastrophic failure such as a case-­head rupture, blowup, all right-­eject firearms are designed to vent the bad stuff to the right, away from the right-­handed shooter’s face. A lefty shooting a right-­eject gun is going to get hurt. Left-­eject guns vent the gas and shrapnel off to the left.

It doesn’t happen often — hopefully, not once in a lifetime — but it only takes once. I’ve had that once, with a right-­hand bolt. There was lots of blood and my eyes were saved by shooting glasses. Sure, I still shoot right-­handed guns; it’s part of my job.
Donna is also left-­handed. Our personal firearms are left-­eject or ambidextrous. Our kids — so far, grandkids, too — are right-­handed. We don’t let them shoot our left-­eject guns. There aren’t many left-­hand bolt guns, so I get excited when I see a new one. We lefties are used to vanilla ice cream, accustomed to left-­hand rifles in limited options and only the most popular chamberings. With several ambidextrous actions and the BAR, A-­Bolt, and X-­Bolt offered in mirror-­image, Browning has long been one of our champions, now continuing with X-­Bolt 2. No, there are not 60-­some left-­hand options. Still, X-­Bolt 2 enters the left-­hand market with six variations: Hunter and Medallion, in walnut and blue; Hunter Composite in blue and synthetic, plus the Speed with standard-­length barrels, the Speed Suppressor (SPR), with shorter barrels, and Speed Long Range (LR) with forend rail strip and extended box magazine.
The left-­hand bolt retains spiral fluting and three large bolt lugs for a short, fast-­cycling lift. The X-­Bolt 2 operates using a push-­feed bolt head with pivoting claw extractor and a plunger for the ejector. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
I hunt and shoot in places where suppressors aren’t allowed, so a Speed with standard barrel lengths interested me most. I was delighted to see the LH Speed introduced in a generous dozen chamberings. Naturally, these included the usual suspects: .243 Win., 6.5 Creedmoor, .270 Win., 7mm Rem. Mag., .308 Win., .30-­’06, and .300 Win. Mag., plus five newer chamberings, all three PRCs — 6.5mm, 7mm, and .300 — and 6.8 Western and .28 Nosler.
It’s fun for a gunwriter to be first to use a new rifle or cartridge. Announced in January 2026 and not immediately available, it’s unlikely the X-­Bolt 2 Left Hand saw use in the 2025 to ’26 hunting seasons. My mind started spinning. I had just committed to a desert sheep hunt in Baja Sur, a leftover permit expiring in 90 days. The sheep down there are the smallest race of bighorn, Ovis canadensis weemsi, typically dark, with horns that often flare outward and retain their tips rather than brooming (i.e., rubbing off) as with most bighorns. Don’t ask me why I had to do this; I should be past such things. I just couldn’t resist.
I was planning on taking my Flat Creek 7mm PRC, hadn’t yet given outfitter Armando Klein the information for a Mexican gun license. Hmm. It would be fun to use a brand-­new left-­handed rifle, I thought. Browning didn’t yet have a full selection, so I nixed 6.5 Creedmoor and .308. Those are not flat-­shooting enough for the shots I envisioned. None of the 7mms nor 6.8 Western would be available in time, so the choice came down to 6.5 PRC or .300 Win. Mag. The .300 was tempting, an old favorite; I had lots of ammo and load recipes. For the medium-­sized sheep, I decided on the 6.5 PRC.

Getting Ready
In early February, I received a double-­verified serial number and passed it on to Klein for the permit. The excitement lifted and I questioned what I had done. To me, all hunts are important. This hunt was important and expensive, and I’d banked it on a rifle I hadn’t yet seen. Well, that card was played. I’d never seen a new Browning rifle that couldn’t get the job done, so there was no sense worrying.
With more than two months to go, I kept working out and hoped the knees hold up. Using Leupold mounts, I topped it with a Leupold VX-­6HD 3-­18x44mm scope. Eight screws on the bases, eight more on the rings. Comforting. In Kansas, the rifle came into zero quickly and produced sub-­MOA three-­shot groups with 
Hornady
 Precision Hunter 143-­grain ELD-­X.
I made three simple modifications. Joe Average in height and arm length, the out-­of-­the-­box length of pull of 135⁄8 inches was perfect, but the comb was a bit low with that scope in medium rings. Using a hex wrench through the Inflex recoil pad, I raised the comb a notch to get the cheekweld just right.
As supplied, the trigger was crisp but a little heavy. The X-­Bolt 2 has an easily adjustable DLX trigger. I did the unmanly thing and read the directions, undid the action screws, removed the triggerguard assembly, found the screw hole in front of the trigger and used a 5⁄32-­inch (4mm) hex wrench. Turn clockwise to increase pull weight, counter-­clockwise to decrease it. Simple. For mountain hunting, expecting to be under pressure and out of breath, I want it light but not too light. A quick adjustment and I had it down just more than 21/2 pounds; still crisp, no creep or overtravel.
Finally, I removed the forward sling swivel stud and replaced it with a Spartan magnet for their bipod/tripod system. It was just a matter of shooting it as much as I possibly could and working up a load.
The Vari-­Tech composite stock wears Browning’s OVIX camo and is adjustable for length of pull and comb height. The grip angle can also be changed to personalize fit. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Although I’ve shot and hunted with several 6.5 PRCs, I hadn’t loaded for it. I’d already decided on the 143-­grain ELD-­X. It’s good accuracy with the factory load, and I’ve been happy with ELD-­X performance in several calibers and cartridges. Propellant was an issue, though. Data suggests that Alliant’s Reloder 26 is ideal.
Several Alliant powders are tough to find right now, not a prayer to get RL26. I had a keg that was almost gone, enough to load up three or four boxes. That’s the powder my 7 PRC loves, so I decided to save that for fall hunting season. The next best available choice was clearly Hodgdon H1000.
Although I haven’t been a 6.5 PRC guy, I am an old 6.5mm guy. I have lots of experience with the .264 Win. Mag. and know the trajectory. With the 143-­grain bullet, my hope was to get as close to 3,000 feet per second (fps) as possible. However, Hornady’s data cites a 26-­inch tube. In the X-­Bolt’s 24-­inch barrel, I couldn’t quite get there. I settled on 58.0 grains of H1000 and a CCI 200 primer. There were no pressure signs but I approached the published maximum. It’s likely to be hot in Baja in April, and I don’t need sticky extraction. Best to stop there. That load yielded 2,946 fps; close enough. Later, Hornady told me that 58 grains of H1000 was one of its developmental loads for 6.5 PRC.

I got similar velocity, the same point of impact and slightly better accuracy with Hornady’s Precision Hunter factory load, which probably contained RL26. Unfortunately, I couldn’t find enough for adequate practice, G&A’s protocol, and for the hunt. So, as is often the case when handloading for hunting, I compromised between accuracy, velocity, and reliable functioning. I loaded up and shot it all over the place for the next two months: Kansas, California, Texas and Georgia.
The X-­Bolt and X-­Bolt 2 share the same rotary magazine design, though one color-­matched mag is provided with the rifle. Capacity is three rounds for the 6.5 PRC. Browning.com offers spares. $75 (Photo by Mark Fingar)
That load’s average of five, five-­shot groups for G&A’s testing was 1.299 inches, a little over 1.25 MOA. Although Standard Deviation (SD) and Extreme Spread (ES) were higher than I like, that load shot with wonderful consistency, five-­shot groups running from 1.239 to 1.366. As I’d learned with the previous X-­Bolt 2 Speeds in .308 and 6.8 Western, the slender barrel heats quickly. Five-­shot groups are a big ask. Within my five-­shot groups, I found three-­shot clusters averaging .539-­inch. In hunting, we care most about the first shot from a cold barrel, so that’s all the accuracy I needed. Later I found Barnes Harvest Collection with tipped Sierra GameKing was accurate, 1.347 inches for five, five-­shot groups. It also produced an amazingly low SD — just 4.1 — from a handload with H4831SC and Berger’s 140-­grain BT Target. With that load, my five-­shot, five-­group average was a stunning .734-­inch. All five groups were under 1 inch, rarely seen from a production rifle in a big-­game cartridge. Unfortunately, that propellent can’t produce the velocity I wanted, and I don’t hunt with match bullets. Berger’s box for the 6.5mm 140-­grain BT Target is clearly marked “Recommended for Targets. Not Suitable for Hunting.” So, another compromise, this one between accuracy and bullet performance. I stuck with my load of 58 grains of H1000 behind the 143-­grain ELD-­X.
By the end of March, I’d put 150 rounds through the rifle. The one thing I hadn’t done was shoot for actual distance. On the first of April, I went to buddy Zack Aultman’s place in Georgia to hunt turkeys. Aultman has steel to 800 yards. The scope was a “Gen 2” CDS turret with tool-­less cap removal. I’d hoped to get a CDS turret cut for this load, but time was too short. After checking and rechecking velocity and zero, I marched the rifle out to 600 yards for four days, farther than I’m likely to shoot at game. It was 11/2-­inches high at 100 and dead center at 200 yards. The first partial stadia line was on at 250, the second at 300. Then, I marked the turret from 350 to 600 and took a great Eastern gobbler along the way. I went home and checked the rifle one last time. It was perfect.
The X-­Bolt 2 Speed Left Hand in 6.5 PRC was tested using handloads and Hornady Precision Hunter and Match ammo. Though Boddington knows how to operate a right-­hand bolt, he prefers the left-­hand actions, primarily for safety, not for convenience. (Photo by Craig Boddington)
Panic and Payoff
You can always be in better shape for a tough hunt. I was about as good as a guy my age can get. My knees were holding and the rifle was ready. I flew to Cabo San Lucas and was met by Klein and his Sierra Madre hunting team. We drove north to camp, a tidy ranchstead looking east to the Sea of Cortez. Time to settle in and check the rifle.
We put up a target and set up a folding table. Supremely confident, I broke out Spartan’s slick little tripod. The first shot was 6 inches left. So was the second. I was horrified and embarrassed. Less than 48 hours earlier, the rifle had been in perfect zero. I had no idea what the airline baggage smashers might have done. Naturally, I over-­corrected, third shot was off to the right. Several shots later and it was perfect again. That’s why we check zero in camp, but it’s a bad moment that works on your mind. Did screws come loose? No. Scope going out? Unlikely. Did they run a forklift over the gun case? I’ll never know. I couldn’t regain the confidence I’d just lost. I thought, Remember the 16 tight screws joining the scope to the X-­Bolt 2 and start hunting.

The South Baja desert sheep is a separate subspecies, smallest and darkest of the bighorns. It’s common for these rams to retain their tips and end with an outward flare. This is fine old ram for the area, taken in its bed with a single 143-­grain Hornady ELD-­X fired from the Browning X-­Bolt 2 Speed Left Hand in 6.5 PRC. (Photo by Craig Boddington)
The country was hot, dry, rough, and steep. Thankfully, no elevation, but the ancient crumbling rock of the American Southwest offers some of the world’s worst footing, and most plants are thorny cacti. Animal density is low, but you only need one — the right one. We weren’t far into the hunt when Klein’s team glassed him. He was a solitary ram, dark and heavy-­horned. Still a couple miles away on a rocky ridge, he was slowly climbing up and looking for a shady spot to bed. He found it at the base of a cliff, in perpetual shadow.
We crossed the valley, struggled up a near-­vertical ridge and came in above him on an adjacent outcropping. I spied a slanting rock, crawled forward, put a pack against it and snuggled prone. The distance to him was 167 yards, much closer than I’d dared dream. Just in front of him, a rare little tree swayed in slight breeze, but I had a clear window. I turned up a little more magnification, slipped off the safety, took a deep breath and let part of it out. I managed not to wonder if the rifle was back in zero. It was. This time, it was still in zero when I got it home.
