EAA Balikli BLK Rifle Review: Best New Budget Hunting Rifle?

How well will this Turkish-made Remington 700 clone stand up to our grueling ammo test?

 The new Balikli BLK bolt-action rifle from EAA proved its salt in this 30-load shootout. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

November 10, 2026

By Steve Gash

The .30-06 cartridge is a seasoned veteran that was officially adopted in 1906. It was the culmination of experimental work on a “new” cartridge for the U.S. military. The .30-03 of 1903 launched a 220-grain roundnose bullet, similar to the .30-40 Krag of 1892. But times they were a-changin’, and in 1906, a 150-grain Spitzer bullet was approved for the .30-06. The .30-06 went on to be one of the most popular cartridges for hunting and handloaders who experiment with as many component combinations as possible. In the ensuing decades, any riflemaker who did not offer models chambered for .30-06 started out behind the 8-ball, and stayed there. Now there’s a new, affordable, and serviceable bolt-action rifle chambered for the old warhorse round, imported by European American Armory (EAA). It’s made by Balikli in Turkey, and it’s called the BLK.
The new BLK bolt action from EAA features a matte black barrel and receiver and a Turkish walnut stock. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The BLK Features
EAA is a well-known importer of various firearms.
 These arms are known for quality and value and are priced competitively. The new BLK is specifically designed as a hunter’s gun, and it is proudly described as a “700-derived clone,” referring of course to the ubiquitous Remington Model 700. This makes it easy to modify, if desired, with aftermarket triggers, scope mounts, and other accessories. Made in Turkey by Balikli using modern technology, the BLK is loaded with features.
If you’re wondering what in the heck Balikli means in Turkish, well, I looked it up on the internet and found that it means “fish” or “fishy,” and it can be used as a noun or an adjective. However, in this rifle name, it refers to an area in the city of Istanbul located along the Marmara Sea and means “with fish,” or a place where there are fishes. Now you know all I know about this interesting and unique name. But there is nothing “fishy” about the Balikli BLK rifle at all.
In addition to the .30-06, the BLK is also offered in .308 Winchester, and the rifle is obviously designed to appeal to American hunters, unlike some European rifles that are made to their desires, not ours. If you like rifles with blued steel and nicely figured and finished walnut, you’re going to like the BLK. The stock is made of Turkish walnut, and my sample is nicely checkered and finished. There are solid streaks of color running through the wood, and the grain is properly oriented through the grip area for strength. Sling-swivel studs are installed fore and aft, and a nice, squishy soft 1-inch-thick recoil pad covers the end of the buttstock.

The stocks of some European guns have a schnabel so gaudy that it detracts from the overall aesthetics of the gun. Not so on the BLK, which has a graceful hint of a schnabel that is totally appropriate for its classic look.
The bolt release is on the left rear side of the receiver, and the two-position safety is at the right rear of the action. It does not lock the action, so a live round can be removed with the safety engaged. The receiver is topped with a 6.0-inch, multi-slotted Picatinny rail for easy mounting of a scope or a red-dot optic. If desired, the rail can be removed and replaced with regular scope bases.
The barrel is 20 inches long, spiral fluted, and threaded 5/8-24 at the muzzle. A thread protector is provided. I took a peek down the barrel with my Hawkeye borescope, and the rifling was smooth and well done. The rifling twist rate is one turn in 10 inches (1:10), standard for the .30-06.

The trigger is listed as adjustable, but the owner’s manual says it is set at the minimum pull weight of 3 pounds at the factory and that it should not be reduced. Could be, but the average pull weight of my rifle’s trigger as received was 5.31 pounds, and it varied from 3 to 6 pounds. I took the stock off (there are two synthetic bushings that guide each of the stock screw) and looked at the trigger and decided that I’d better leave well enough alone. Inveterate tinkerers will quickly realize that this trigger situation can be easily resolved with one phone call to Timney. Remember, the BLK is a Model 700 clone.
The BLK’s bolt has dual opposing locking lugs and a fluted body. Note the large bolt knob. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The rifle, as received, weighed 7.19 pounds. It came with two detachable box magazines. Both hold five rounds. The length of pull measures 13.75 inches, and the drop on the buttstock comb and heel fit me just right.
Range Results
I was anxious to get to the shooting range with the new gun and see how it shot. As the great, late Mike Venturino used to say, the only thing that matters are bullet holes in paper. So, I rounded up 15 factory loads with bullet weights ranging from 150 to 220 grains, including some new high-tech hunting bullets. I also loaded up several handloads.
I fired five-round groups from inside my shooting building from a Caldwell Lead Sled DFT rifle rest with two bags of shot on it for ballast. Velocities were measured with an Oehler Model 35P chronograph with the lighted Skyscreen IIIs four feet apart (midpoint was 10 feet from the gun’s muzzle). I cleaned the bore after every three groups, and the bore did not pick up a lot of bullet jacket fouling over the course of shooting many groups, a major bonus feature as far as I’m concerned.
The results of all loads tested are shown in the accompanying chart. The average group size for the 15 factory loads was 1.10 inches. Seven of the loads were at or under one inch. In addition, these loads were extremely uniform ballistically, with the coefficient of variation (COV) being only 0.63 percent. That, my friends, is quite good.

Steve’s thorough shootout with a total of 30 loads (factory ammo and handloads) proved the BLK is nicely accurate with the loads it liked the best. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The top factory load was Hornady’s Superformance ammo with the 180-grain SST bullet (average velocity was 2,683 fps). A close second was Federal’s 165-grain Trophy Copper at 2,723 fps, but many other loads shot just fine, too. The BLK wasn’t picky.
All those empty cases just begged to be reloaded, so to the bench I went. I ended up with 15 handloads, 12 of which are for big game. Here the BLK demonstrated its versatility with this classic cartridge. The overall group average size was a tidy 1.03 inches, again with a low COV of only 0.33 percent. Game from small deer to large elk or moose can be hunted with confidence with this rifle. Probably the most popular bullet weight for the .30-06 is 180 grains, and the four handloads with this bullet weight averaged 0.99 inch.
Sharp-eyed readers may scoff at the inclusion of the old-fashioned 220-grain roundnose bullets. But many readers will remember the Alaskan writer Jim Reardon. He once wrote about the proper rifle for brown bears, and he stated that the cartridge didn’t matter much, as long as you got the heaviest bullet you could get for it. For the .30-06, that’s 220 grains. I also tested Hornady’s 200-grain ELD-X, and it, too, shot well. Both the 220- and 200-grain bullets shot really well with Superformance and StaBALL 6.5 powders. However, the loads I used with these two powders with these heavy bullets looked like maximum loads for this rifle.
The BLK uses metal detachable box magazines. Our rifle came with two magazines that hold five rounds of .30-06 ammo each. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
I had a bright idea to further illustrate the BLK’s versatility. I had three component bullets in my cache that are made for the .30-30 Winchester with weights of 125, 150, and 170 grains. I picked IMR 4895 as the propellant because its origin was with the .30-06 in military ammo, and it was the first powder Bruce Hodgdon sold to handloaders in 1947.
I would like to proclaim success with this bright idea, but, alas, it was almost a total flop. While the groups were acceptable “minute-of-deer” with an average of 1.24 inches, their extreme spreads and standard deviations were huge.
In the course of all this shooting there was not a single malfunction of any kind. Every round chambered, fired, and ejected perfectly. And as noted, the accuracy was certainly up to par. The only factor that probably limited the group size was the rifle’s rather heavy trigger pull.
The .30-06 remains one of the premier cartridges for big-game hunting worldwide, and for good reasons. It is flexible and versatile, and it shows no signs of relinquishing its 120-year lead. And the new BLK is a great vehicle for it. I’d take the BLK hunting any day. In fact, I think a hunt for Texas exotics is in order.
