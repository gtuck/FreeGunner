## Article Text (extracted)

How Reliable Are Your Handguns Magazines? Here's How To Find Out.

Some reliability and endurance tests you can do at home.

 (Photo by Andy Grossman)

March 19, 2026

By James Tarr

The military often conducts durability and endurance tests on its rifle magazines, but you rarely see or hear of that on the civilian or commercial side regarding pistol magazines. The Glock design is currently the most popular and numerous in the U.S., and it occurred to me that a comparison of some of the magazines available for it might interest G&A readers — not just a review of the different features, but the ubiquitous drop test.
I tested versions of the original, standard 17-­round magazine meant for the G17 and other full-­size 9mms, including the G34, Shadow Systems DR920, TTI Combat Master, PSA Dagger, Lone Wolf Timberwolf 17, etc. For this, I sourced three different brands of magazines meant for Glock-­pattern pistols: 
Glock factory magazines
, 
Magpul GL9 PMags
 — the most popular Glock-­pattern magazine after the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) type — and the newest, metal-­bodied 18-­round 
flush-­fit magazines from Mec-Gar
. The latter have been nicknamed the “G-­Mags.”
A section of PVC pipe was used to ensure consistent magazine drop testing. A total of 180 drops were performed. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
First, a bit of pertinent history. Today’s Glock factory magazines are quite different from the first Gen 1 models introduced to the U.S. in 1985. Early mags were all polymer with no steel insert. Then, these pistols made their way into the holsters of cops, especially in America. Cops are often big guys, usually heavy before considering the body armor and 20 pounds of gear strapped around their waists. They sat on those guns while in their squad cars, many in hot climates. Heat and weight sometimes deformed the grip and the magazine inside, enough to induce malfunctions. Introduced in 1988, the Gen 2 models included magazines with a steel insert to address this issue.
Second. The early Glock magazines, even those with liners, were not meant to drop free from the pistol. Empty or loaded, sometimes they did but they regularly did not. Fully loaded mags tended to swell, also. This retention wasn’t considered a bug but rather a feature — to Europeans.

Glock considered the complaints from American law enforcement — they never truly listened to the commercial U.S. market — and provided drop-­free mags after 1988.
From the mid-1990s Gen 3 to the Gen 4 introduced in 2010,  which accommodated a reversible magazine catch, there weren’t significant changes to OEM factory magazines. Glock offered magazines in different capacities and colors following public interest in its FDE magazines issued with the G22s provided to the U.S. Army Special Forces Delta unit, but beyond that the reliable design hasn’t changed substantively.
Magpul PMag for Glock pistols don’t have a metal insert. The polymer used for those magazines is stiffer. A steel insert wasn’t deemed necessary. These magazines have been available since 2015 and have proven to be reliable and durable. I wish they had more witness holes for capacity status, though. There’s only one to show when the magazine is fully loaded, but that’s a small complaint.

The Mec-­Gar 18-­round flush-fit magazine for Glock pistols was introduced in 2024, receiving a lot of attention. It has a steel body, a bright-green polymer follower, and plus-one capacity within a flush-type profile designed to work with the newer polymer magazine releases, also. These mags ignited a firestorm online because — unlike both OEM and Magpul magazines — Mec-Gar’s doesn’t feature a built-­in overtravel stop. If you’re using a Mec-Gar mag in the G17 or clone, it’s not an issue. If you stuff it in a model with a shorter grip (i.e., G19 or G26), you could damage the magazine or the gun if you drive the magazine with a lot of force. Mec-­Gar advertises these mags are specifically for the G17, offering shorter mags for the G19. Many magazines don’t have overtravel stops, so complaints that a magazine doesn’t work right in a pistol for which it wasn’t specifically designed make the lawyers I know in the firearm industry laugh with liability-­free derision.
Before the drop tests, a caliper was used to measure each magazine’s feed lips. They were measured again after the tests. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
I have new magazines on hand from all three brands, including primaries and backups — just in case. An empty G17 Gen 5 OEM magazine weighed 2.73 ounces. For comparison, the all-­polymer Magpul PMag weighed 1.68 ounces and the Mec-­Gar steel mag weighed 3.15 ounces. The Magpul magazine has a noticeably stiffer spring than the other two. To remove the basepad on the Magpul or Mec-­Gar magazines, you’ll need a punch. Glock magazine bodies have tabs on the side that lock into the basepad, which can complicate disassembly. A tool is often needed to take apart an OEM mag. (Some users grind those side tabs off because they are so annoying, in fact.)
Just Drop It.
The drop test was simple. Each of the three magazines was released from a height of 6 feet onto bare concrete, on the basepads, empty, half-­full (nine rounds) and full, 10 times. Then I did it again onto the feed lips. That’s 180 drops total. I dropped them through a slightly tilted 6-­foot section of 3-­inch I.D. PVC pipe to keep them properly oriented, and half the drops were with the spine of the magazine against the pipe; half with the front of the magazine against the pipe.
When dropping the mags onto the basepads, all of the magazines were scuffed, but nothing cracked or broke. When half-­full, the Magpul GL9 magazines sometimes popped out a round on impact, but that was it for the lot. Dropping them onto the feed lips was something else entirely! 
I used calipers to measure the distance between the feed lips to track dimensional changes between repeated impacts. While the exterior of the GL9 magazine feed lips were scuffed by repeated impacts, the thick polymer ridges did not spread, narrow or crack. The front of the magazines at the top were bent outward slightly, but function wasn’t affected.

The steel feed lips of the Mec-­Gar magazines lost its finish halfway through the test. These survived the test without getting bent inward or outward. However, I noticed that near the end of the half-­full drops, my floor had tiny pieces of green plastic on it. Disassembling the Mec-­Gar magazines showed that small pieces had broken off the bottom front of the follower, and there was a small crack on one. I suspect the follower was damaged when I dropped a half-­full magazine onto the basepad and that cartridge column was slamming the follower down inside the magazine. The pieces only started to fall out where I could see them when I started dropping the magazine nose first. The follower suffered no other damage, and nothing affected reliability.
The OEM Glock magazine (above, right) failed after drop tests, while the Magpul GL9 and Meg-Gar metal mags survived. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
With the OEM magazine, the steel inserts in the feed lips were bent slightly — .01-inch closer together — but this didn’t affect function, even after 10 empty drops. After 10 drops with a half-­full magazine, the feed lips showed significantly more damage and were pushed together another .03 inch, holding the top cartridge at a visible angle to the left. The magazine still functioned in my test pistol, but I could feel the rounds hitting the chamber on the way in harder than usual. At this point, I considered the magazine to be too damaged to trust for carry but I would mark it and use it for range practice.
After 10 drops with a fully loaded magazine, the top-front of the OEM magazine — both the polymer and the steel liner — had bent outward, and the steel feed lips were crushed closer together another .02 inch. The total deformation measured .06 inch. That may not sound significant, but it was. The magazine ceased to function reliably and wouldn’t consistently feed rounds upward; cartridges were binding within.
This result, honestly, is the exact opposite of what I expected. When it comes to feed lips, apparently thick hard polymer (Magpul GL9) and tough tempered steel (Mec-­Gar) survive drop tests better than a combination of soft polymer around thin, soft steel liners. When new, all of these magazines worked, but use induces wear. While you might not abuse your magazines as I did for G&A’s test, you should inspect them regularly. With enough use and age, you’ll eventually see some problems.
