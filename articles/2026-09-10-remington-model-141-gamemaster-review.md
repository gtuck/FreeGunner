Remington Model 141 Gamemaster Review

A Pump-Action Rifle with Superior Vintage Craftsmanship 

 The vintage Remington Model 141’s craftsmanship is superior to most of the rifles, shotguns, and handguns on the market today. (Joseph von Benedik photo)

September 10, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

In 1913 Remington was concerned about the dominating popularity of Winchester’s lever-action Model 1894 rifle. World War I had not yet showed American hunters the ballistic superiority of the bolt action, and quick-handling, fast-functioning rifles were prized.
Five years earlier Remington had introduced the semiautomatic “self-loading” Model 8, a John Browning design that opened new frontiers as the first of its type designed specifically for hunters. It hadn’t garnered the popularity Remington had hoped for, though. Heavy and slightly less reliable than a rifle operated by muscle, it didn’t have widespread appeal.
John Pedersen’s pump-action design, Remington hoped, would be different. Quick-handling, light enough, and naturally familiar to America’s nation of pump-action shotgunners, the Model 14 had promise.
While it never achieved popularity enough to compete with Winchester’s 1894, the Model 14 and the later Model 141 reviewed here garnered a cult-like appreciation among those who used them. Somewhere around 200,000 total were made.

Most unique about the Models 14 and 141 is the spiral-indented tubular magazine designed to enable safe use of pointed bullets. By offsetting the cartridge noses so no bullet’s tip could contact the primer of the cartridge in front of it, Remington engineers eliminated the potential danger of in-magazine detonation.
Along with the Model 8 and Model 14/141, Remington introduced a series of cartridges intended to compete with Winchester’s .25-35, .30-30, and .32 Special. Of rimless design and featuring steeper shoulder angles, they incorporated modern characteristics. Named the .25, .30, .32, and .35 Remington, they were all good cartridges. Sadly, only the .35 Remington remains in production today. It’s worth noting that the .35 Remington owes its survival to Marlin, which chambered it in the ever-popular Model 336 series of lever actions.
Mechanicals
In addition to the spiral magazine, the Model 141 featured here has several unique mechanical characteristics. Most unusual is the fact that the steel magazine tube reciprocates back and forth with the pump. Also unique, said magazine is loaded via a gate housed in the mag tube itself, rather than through the side or bottom of the action.

The bolt is a tilting-breech design, and it locks up into the top of the receiver. The bolt lock release is a small button housed in the side of the bolt, activated with the tip of the thumb through the rear end of the ejection port.
Like many hunting rifles of the era, the Model 14s and 141s are takedowns. Unscrew a knurled bolt located just above the trigger guard on the left side of the action, and you can pull the front and rear halves of the rifle apart.
To load, thumb cartridges into the loading gate in the tubular magazine. Press the bolt catch and work the pump briskly to chamber a live round. To fire, press the crossbolt safety to the “Off” position, aim, and press the trigger. Work the pump and repeat.
Provenance
Thanks to a fascination with .35-caliber cartridges
, I’ve wanted a rifle chambered in .35 Remington for some time, although I always figured I’d find a self-loading Model 8 or a Marlin lever action. A friend and his wife are expecting, and he offered to sell me the Model 141 rifle pictured here. He stated he needs money for diapers more than he needs a vintage Remington pump action. Faced with such a good cause, who was I to argue? This one is worth between $500 and $700.
Nothing is known about the history of the rifle, aside from the fact that according to its letter code on the left rear of the barrel it was made in March 1942, right after the United States became embroiled in World War II.

Rangetime
My friend had warned me that the rifle was hitting left of point of aim. My first shot with the classic pump gun was offhand at my 100-yard steel gong, just to get a feel for the rifle. I missed off the left edge. Correcting my hold, I smacked the steel and broke the target off its stand. Gotta love a big .35-caliber bullet!
Adjusting the point of impact was easy, thanks to the fact that at some point the original rear sight had been replaced with a peep sight made by Skinner Sights.
The three loads I tested (
Hornady
, 
Remington
, and 
Winchester
) are topped with 200-grain bullets. Interestingly, the Winchester and Remington loads are advertised to produce 2,020 fps and 2,080 fps, respectively, while the Hornady LEVERevolution ammo is spec’d at 2,225 fps. At that speed, it generates the most energy, hammering the proverbial pendulum with 2,198 ft-lbs.
At 50 yards, Winchester’s ammo grouped well, averaging 1.14 inches for three-shot clusters. Hornady’s ammo averaged 1.22 inches. And Remington’s Core-Lokt ammo took top accuracy honors, averaging 0.92 inch. I was impressed.
Without a great big scope, without a free-floated, match-grade barrel and a crisp trigger measured in ounces, without a bipod or level bubble or muzzle brake or suppressor, my Model 141 is an honest 2-MOA rifle.
The vintage Remington 141 has a nice between-the-hands feel, and it shoulders smoothly and effortlessly. The balance is excellent, and pointability is reminiscent of the grand old Remington 870 shotgun—which is a great compliment.
Only one thing made the svelte pump action a bit challenging to shoot accurately, and that’s the 6-pound, 10-ounce trigger pull weight. It’s crisp enough, and I got used to it eventually, but it sure would be nice to have a lighter trigger, particularly for a rifle that’s so clearly perfect for fast shooting. That said, the more time I spent examining the Model 141, the more impressed I became with its ingenuity and quality. Its craftsmanship is clearly superior to many of the rifles, shotguns, and handguns on the market today.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
