Glenfield Model A by Ruger in .30-'06: Reviewed 

Revisiting the hardware store ERA with Ruger's revival of a classic brand and its Model A in .30-'06.

 (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)

July 13, 2026

By Craig Boddington

If you’re of my generation, you’ll remember the 
Glenfield
 brand made by Marlin from the 1960s for some of the early and iconic American big box retail and catalog outlets: JCPenney, Kmart, Montgomery Ward and Western Auto. Yes, they all sold firearms. Lots of firearms. If those names don’t ring bells, don’t fret! You’ll still appreciate the 
Glenfield Model A by Ruger
. It’s a practical, no-­frills hunting rifle that’s priced right, feels good, and does everything it’s supposed to do.
(Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
In 2020, Ruger acquired the Glenfield brand along with Marlin. We know what they’ve done with the Marlin brand, which now produces smooth-­operating Marlin Models 1895, 336, and 1894 lever-­action rifles. In November 2025, Ruger announced its first firearm under the Glenfield brand: the Model A, a centerfire bolt action. The Model A is not revolutionary. It is essentially a first-generation Ruger American with three-­lug bolt, detachable magazine and adjustable bladed trigger. To identify it with the Gen I American is not damning with faint praise. Since introduction in 2012, 2 million have been sold with a well-­earned reputation for ruggedness, reliability and accuracy.
The barrel features 5/8x24-inch threads underneath the provided thread cap, making the Glenfield Model A is an ideal host for a suppressor. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
I was present when Ruger’s then-­CEO, Mike Fifer, unveiled the Ruger American at Tim Fallon’s FTW ranch in Texas. The first Ruger American I shot was a .30-’06. Smooth and shockingly accurate, I took it out to 1,000 yards with no difficulty. The first Glenfield Model A I had in my hands was also a .30-’06, America’s most classic hunting cartridge, mated with a classic brand in a proven platform. I expected it to strut.
Blast From the Past
Fascinated by firearms from an early age, I remember admiring Glenfield firearms in store gun racks. Probably most famous was the Glenfield M30 series, essentially a Marlin 336. There were also slide-­action and bolt-­action shotguns. Back then, I didn’t have any experience with Glenfield firearms. Much later, I realized I missed a rare opportunity.

The three-lug bolt provides a 70-degree bolt throw, preventing the bolt handle from hitting a scope’s ocular housing when cycling. The bolt head has a sliding extractor and plunger-type ejector. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
When I was a pre-­teen, my cousin Carole Popham married Sidney McKnight Jr. His dad, “Big Sid” as we called him, was CEO of Montgomery Ward. Though already a gun nut, I wasn’t smart enough to realize I had one of America’s largest gun retailers as part of our extended family. Soon, visiting cousin Carole and Sid, I noticed that Sid, not surprisingly, had several Glenfields in his gun rack. I never thought about that again — until now, when Ruger resurrected that much-­loved American brand.
Regardless of which outlet sold it, the original Glenfield was the affordable Marlin. In those days, all gunstocks were wood. Glenfield reduced the cost by using birch and other hardwoods rather than walnut. The Glenfield Model A by Ruger doesn’t follow that model. Some versions of the Gen I American are still in production, with a retail price of the Glenfield Model A running about $100 less. Instead of less costly components, the Glenfield follows Henry Ford’s famous credo with his own Model T: “You can have it any way you want it …as long as it’s black.”
Glenfield’s Model A achieves its price point with no frills and few options. All metal is matte black. Stocks are synthetic in Moss Green Splatter, a textured finish that reduces abrasion and gives a good feel. All barrels are medium-­contour, all 20 inches, threaded with thread protector supplied. All have factory-­installed Picatinny rail strips, sling-swivel studs, and a good rubber recoil pad. Not fancy, but very complete.

The Glenfield Model A utilizes the patented Power Bedding system that appears in stocks on models such as the Ruger American and 10/22. Two stainless-steel blocks secure the action to the stock and help to free-float the barrel and improve accuracy potential. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Initially, seven cartridge options were announced: .243 Winchester, .270 Win., .308 Win, 6.5 Creedmoor, .30-’06, .350 Legend, and .450 Bushmaster. Obviously, this means two action lengths. The cartridge offerings also mean one magazine won’t fit all. The .243, 6.5 CM, .270, .308, and .30-’06 use the flush-­mounted polymer rotary magazine from the Ruger American. Capacity varies depending on cartridge. In .30-’06-­diameter cases (.243, 6.5 CM, .270, and .308), the compact magazine holds a surprising four cartridges. This magazine isn’t readily adaptable to the slimmer .350 Legend, nor the fatter .450 Bushmaster. The Legend uses an AR-­type magazine, while Bushmaster uses a single-­stack box magazine. Otherwise, that’s it; you can have your Glenfield Model A any way you want it.
The rubber recoil pad is soft, reducing perceived recoil while ensuring that the stock doesn’t slip off the shoulder. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Who Said A .30-’­06 Won't Shoot?
I suppose this rumor was started by .308 fans. I’m not one of them; I prefer a .30-’06 because of higher velocity and greater energy, but it’s almost an article of faith that the .308 is more accurate than its daddy. It’s true, though, the .308 is more efficient.
I actually received Model As in both .308 and .30-’06 about the same time, one directly from Ruger and the other from Guns & Ammo’s editorial office. We decided to focus on the .30-’06 for this article, but not necessarily because it’s my favorite. Perhaps because, with so many newer cartridges, the tired old — yet so effective — warhorse doesn’t have as much love.
Chambered in .30-’06, G&A’s Glenfield Model A included a flush-fit magazine with a four-round capacity. Additional mags are available from the Ruger website. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
At the Range
Somewhat defying the marching orders, I grouped both, using a mix of factory ammo and handloads. Both rifles were smooth-­cycling and flawless in functioning, and both were accurate. I’ll spare the suspense: Of these two rifles, the .30-’06 broke the rules and delivered the best average. A tidy 1.23-­inch aggregate of 15 five-­shot groups at 100 yards, five each with three different loads. Wow! For an inexpensive factory rifle with randomly selected loads, that’s strong! So was the .308. It averaged about 11/2 inches for its 15 groups. That’s also strong.
As a lifelong .30-’06 guy, I was delighted to see the ’06 come out on top. When I accepted the assignment, I didn’t have as much .30-’06 ammo on hand as I thought, and nothing that I expected to drive tacks with. I started with inexpensive Fox SWCR with 150-­grain TMEC, a tipped alloy bullet. Although extreme spread (ES) and standard deviation (SD) were disturbingly high, I’ve had good accuracy with this load. It did well, averaging 1.289-­inch for five, five-­shot groups.

I also had a couple boxes of an old Remington 75th anniversary load with 180-­grain Core-­Lokt round-­nose. Not fancy, but I’ve seen some rifles shoot their best groups with good old round-­nose bullets.
Lacking enough factory ammo to try anything else, I went to a time-­proven load recipe and whomped up a couple boxes with 56 grains of H4350 and some 180-­grain Swift A-­Frames. At this point, I was gathering ammo, hadn’t fired the rifle yet, and had no idea how it was going to shoot. If things went well, there was potential for an end-­of-­season whitetail hunt, so I had hunting ammo on my mind.
I greatly admire how the Swift A-­Frame performs on game, but, although accuracy is consistently adequate, I usually don’t find it among the most accurate bullets. You never know, though. In this small sampling, the 180-­grain A-­Frame not only produced the best average — 1.035-­inches — it was also the most consistent. Two of the five groups were under 1 MOA (.903 and .924 in.). Two were fractions more than, and the largest group of the five was 1.267 inches. Awesome, and that load also produced the lowest SD and ES!
The two-position tang safety is naturally positioned under the thumb. In the rear position, it conceals a red dot. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The barrel is stout enough that, even with a .30-­caliber hole, rapid heating wasn’t a problem. As usual, I cleaned the bore between loads, then fired a couple of fouling shots. With the growing popularity of suppressors, shorter barrels are all the rage. Love the handiness, especially with a suppressor, but there is velocity loss, about 100 fps per load in this .30-’06. No game animal will know the difference, but your trajectory curve is steeper. Shorter, more efficient cartridges such as the 6.5 Creedmoor and .308 tend to lose proportionately less than longer cartridges such as .270 and .30-­06.
As intended, the Glenfield Model A is a simple rifle and I like it. The stock is a pure American classic profile. For Joe Average me, it fits like a glove. Ribbing texture on the pistol grip area and both sides of the forend gives the Model A a good feel. No secrets to operation, except to remember that the two-­position tang safety does not lock the bolt when in the rear “safe” position.
Both on the bench and in the field, the Glenfield Model A shot exceptionally well with factory ammunition and a handload. The .30-­’06 is a powerful cartridge, so there was plenty of recoil on the bench. It was mitigated by the straight stock and thick recoil pad. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Deep in the Heart of Texas
First of the year is convention season, a busy time. In early 2026, I attended four conventions nearly back-­to-­back. The Glenfields arrived during this hectic time; I managed to get them to the range, take a deep breath, sit down and write this story. Except: A few days remained of the Texas private ranch deer season. My son-­in-­law Brad and daughter Brittany were behind their management goals on their Texas place. They couldn’t join me, but Brad offered the ranch if I could get there before the season ended. I’d had a tough whitetail season in Nebraska, and didn’t fill my Kansas tag. You don’t have to be crazy, but sometimes it helps.
Unable to resist, I scrambled and flew into San Antonio. With three days of season remaining, we whisked off to Hondo. This was purely a management hunt. Still, highly selective. The main goal was to remove some older does. I don’t like to shoot does, but it’s necessary. That time of year, it can be difficult. Some bucks have shed, and last year’s button bucks have grown as big as their moms.
When shooting groups to Guns & Ammo’s test protocol, the author let the barrel cool between groups and cleaned the bore between loads. The Glenfied has a fairly stiff barrel, which adds weight to the Model A but retards heating. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
With blizzards pummeling much of the country, it was 102 degrees in San Antonio during late February. Hot as blazes inside a box blind, I didn’t see much that evening. My friend Lee’s stand produced better, lots of deer coming and going, but nothing he felt certain he should take. Overnight, the worst of the hot spell passed. The next couple of days were still unseasonably warm but cooler with more activity. We did our due diligence and shot a few does, avoiding “buck o’clock” for this necessary business. The Glenfield felt as good in the field as on the range. As I’ve known for 50 years, the .30-’06 is a big gun on whitetails.

On the last evening, I went to a stand we call “Big Oak,” shaded by a massive, ancient live oak. It was a bit cooler, nice breeze coming in. Still, I saw nothing for the first couple of hours. I was starting to think this whole mission was folly, and perhaps I should have stayed home with Donna and our dog. Then, with about an hour of light — and season — remaining, the parade started. A young eight-­pointer came out, followed by another, then some does. A big, beautiful 10-­pointer. Then another big buck with broken points, part of the deal at the end of the season. No way to tell what he might have been before he started fighting.
Boddington’s Texas buck was a last-­minute miracle, a mature, heavy-­antlered eight-­pointer. He dropped in his tracks after being struck by a handloaded 180-­grain Swift A-­Frame delivered by the Glenfield Model A. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
At almost sunset, a dozen deer were in front of me. I was studying my options. Too big, too young. One modest eight-­pointer was likely 31/2 years old, and should be better at that age. I stroked the Glenfield, nestled in the corner of the blind and saw movement in thick oaks on the far side. I put the bino up and thought I could see antlers. Maybe it was a new buck! He stood in the trees for a couple of minutes, made up his mind, and advanced into the clearing. Right to left. Oh, yes. Big body, thick, gray neck. He was at least 5 years old, probably 6. Antlers tall and heavy, only three points were visible on his left side. My hands were shaking. Good Lord, it was the oldest buck and the biggest, cleanest eight-­pointer I’d seen on this ranch in years. It was my chance to blow this.
I lowered the binos and slowly, carefully, reached for the rifle and eased it up into the shadowed window. About 130 yards, broadside but not clear. I slipped the safety and waited, took a few breaths. The other deer separated and he quartered to me. I got the crosshairs on the point of his shoulder and pressed the trigger. The Glenfield, the great old .30-’06, and the devastating Swift A-­Frame did their work. The buck was down on the spot with no movement, just as the sun slipped below the horizon in the last half-­hour of the season.
