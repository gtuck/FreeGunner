Springfield Garrison Target 1911 Review [Tested]

Classic Design Meets Precision Up-grades: A Closer Look at the Garrison Target

 With the classically styled Garrison Target, Springfield gives demanding 1911 shooters the adjustable sight they want. (Mike Anschuetz photo)

September 01, 2026

By Keith Wood

I own 1911-style handguns from many makers, but when it comes to buying new ones, most of 
my recent acquisitions have come from Springfield Armory
. Springfield’s Garrison line in particular strikes a great balance between quality, affordability and features. With the release of the new adjustable-sight Garrison Target, this series just became even more attractive.
Garrisons are handguns that come out of the box with upgrades that used to require custom additions in the 1911 world. Flared ejection ports, Delta hammers, three-hole triggers and extended safeties are all standard equipment. I’ve used them in their stock configurations as well as foundations for custom builds and have been consistently impressed.
When I have tweaked Garrisons in the past, one of the things I addressed was the sights. In most cases, swapping fixed sights for compatible aftermarket sights is relatively simple since the parts use the same dovetail on the slide. Installing adjustable sights, though, generally requires a milling machine. And for most of us, that means a trip to a gunsmith. The new Garrison model offers adjustable sights as standard equipment.
The Garrison Target features a single-side thumb safety, Delta hammer and grip safety with bump. The thinline grips are done in classic double-diamond style. (Mike Anschuetz photo)
Before getting into the sights, though, let’s start with the basics. Like other pistols in the series, the adjustable-sight model begins with domestically forged steel frames and slides. Though some pistols in the Garrison lineup are available in stainless steel, the Target version is strictly a blued carbon steel proposition. The handgun is built on a full-size Government frame and is available in either .45 ACP or 9mm. I chose the classic .45 for the test gun.

The frame is machined in GI style, virtually unchanged in more than a century, with a smooth frontstrap and rounded trigger guard. However, this is where the upgrades begin. The pistol uses a beavertail-style grip safety with a large bump to ensure the safety disengages even with a high grip on the pistol.
The grip safety on this gun required about 75 percent of forward travel before it disengaged, allowing the trigger bow to move toward the sear. Below the grip safety, the flat steel mainspring housing is checkered at 30 lines per inch. The magazine well is beveled to eliminate sharp edges that could interrupt a fast magazine change.
The Garrison is fitted with a strong-side-only extended manual safety. The safety lever is large enough to engage without shifting one’s grip on the handgun but doesn’t protrude excessively. The thumb safety’s engagement and disengagement are crisp and positive.

The slide stop is a typical GI-style unit with a serrated pad. When engaged, the slide stop’s tab fit almost perfectly with its mating detent on the slide. I’ve seen makers of far pricier 1911s miss the mark here. This tiny detail is often overlooked but is evidence Springfield Armory isn’t cutting corners. The magazine catch is also standard, with vertical serrations.
The pistol comes with a three-hole aluminum trigger. The trigger pull on my example was very clean for a factory gun. At 5.1 pounds, it wasn’t super light, but it was crisp with a short, positive reset. Tweaking the sear spring tension would reduce the pull weight, but I left it as-is. The gun uses a simple and effective Series 70-style mechanism. Rounding out the controls is a Delta-style hammer.
The slide is machined from a steel forging. It is round-topped and cut with angled cocking serrations in the style first encountered on National Match Colts from the mid-20th century. Like the frame, the flats of the slide are polished while the other surfaces wear a satin finish. The ejection port is lowered and flared to allow positive ejection of spent cases without damaging brass on the way out.
The aspect of this pistol that separates it from the rest of the series is, of course, the sights. The front sight is a dovetailed white bead that is also drilled vertically at the front of the blade and roll-pinned into place. Experience has taught me that removing a sight from a Springfield Armory dovetail is next to impossible, even intentionally, but the added security provided by the pin is a nice touch.
The Garrison sports a classic Bo-Mar-style adjustable rear sight. Spring-loaded detents make for audible, positive windage and elevation clicks. (Mike Anschuetz photo)
The slide is milled to accept a Bo-Mar-style adjustable rear sight. Originally designed by George Elliason in the 1950s, the Bo-Mar became the standard adjustable sight for 1911s. It is one of those enduring products that simply works. The company folded in 2007, but the design remains popular.

Bo-Mar-style sights are mounted at different heights using different milling techniques. Springfield Armory’s approach is a simple and effective one that combines a large flat, a dovetail and a 90-degree relief cut for the windage mechanism and blade.
The sight has a large black blade with a serrated surface and a square notch. It is fully adjustable for both windage and elevation using a standard flat screwdriver. Thanks to specially designed screws and spring-loaded detents, the adjustments are accompanied by audible and tactile clicks.
The pistol retails for $121 more than the fixed-sight version. Considering that an aftermarket Bo-Mar-style sight can cost $80 or more—and paying a gunsmith to make the dovetail cut would easily exceed $100—this is a value-priced upgrade.
The five-inch, match-grade barrel is forged from stainless steel with a standard 1:16-inch twist. Other than a small window cut into the barrel hood that serves as a loaded-chamber indicator, the barrel is just as John Browning designed it. There are three top locking lug surfaces and two more at the barrel feet. The barrel bushing is also stainless steel, which contrasts nicely with the blued slide.
Both the barrel and bushing are stainless steel, which gives the Garrison Target a two-tone appearance from the front. (Mike Anschuetz photo)
The barrel on my sample locked up tightly both at the chamber and breech ends with no sign of barrel squat—vertical play at the back—thanks to good contact between the lower lugs and the slide stop. This fit became even more evident once I started sending rounds downrange.
The recoil system is the standard GI type with a checkered plug, recoil spring and spring guide. With this foolproof system, no tools or shenanigans are required to fieldstrip the gun. The magazine is a standard steel seven-rounder.
The pistol uses an internal extractor and a fixed, frame-mounted ejector. Gunmaker Jason Burton taught me a handy trick for evaluating extractor function that I used on this pistol. With the slide assembly removed, use the barrel to center a loaded cartridge on the breech face. Pull the barrel forward so it no longer supports the cartridge, and then roll the slide to ensure the extractor holds onto the case rim regardless of angle. The test gun passed this test easily.
The thinline grips are narrower than usual and use special bushing screws to maintain the slim profile. The wooden panels feature a double-diamond checkering pattern with Springfield Armory’s logo at the center. I consider most factory grips semi-disposable and easily replaced according to personal preference, but these look and feel fine.

Overall, the fit and finish on the test gun were very good. There was no traditional 1911 rattle when shaken. On today’s Springfields, the flats are flat and the rounds are round. The bluing and underlying surface preparation are excellent for a factory handgun. The gun ships in a handy zippered nylon case, ideal for long-term storage or a trip to the range.
I’ve been shooting 1911s since before puberty, so to say firing this pistol was a familiar experience would be a gross understatement. There’s just something satisfying about the familiar grip angle combined with the straight rearward trigger pull and the gratifying shove of .45 ACP recoil.
Accuracy was impressive across the board. It’s easy to slap words like “target” and “match” on a firearm, but it is another thing altogether when a gun actually performs at a level befitting those terms. This one did. While in my experience ammunition loaded with 
Hornady
’s XTP bullets is often the most accurate, this gun preferred a classic 230-grain hardball load. Those groups averaged right at an inch at 25 yards.
The magazine well is beveled slightly to remove the sharp edges and keep mags from hanging up during speed reloads. (Mike Anschuetz photo)
The internet loves to criticize the 1911’s reliability and, sure, it can be finicky compared to some modern handgun designs. This pistol, however, proved completely reliable with three different bullet profiles of varying weights, including two hollowpoint designs.
I ran it several times with no magazine inserted to ensure it would extract and eject without being supported by the cartridge below it. There were no problems. The slide locked back on an empty magazine without fail.

This performance mirrors my recent experiences with several Springfield Armory 1911s. I ran a pair of 9mm Garrisons through a high-round-count course in the Arizona desert last summer, with no problems whatsoever. Clean and lubricate this gun at appropriate intervals and there should be no issues.
The Garrison Target combines design elements faithful to the time-tested 1911/1911A1 while adding tasteful and functional upgrades where they matter. This is a classic-looking .45 that doesn’t suffer from the tiny sights and finicky grip safety of GI guns. I wouldn’t hesitate to use it as-is, but it would also serve as a fantastic foundation for a semi-custom handgun.
I’m often asked by less-experienced shooters to recommend the best gun at a given price point. If I were given a budget of $1,000 and asked to pick a 1911-style handgun, this would be my choice thanks to its accuracy, reliability, appearance and overall performance.
I’ve owned and/or tested 10 different Springfield Armory 1911s over the past few years. Every one of those handguns has impressed me straight out of the box. Aesthetically and functionally, though, this model is probably my favorite. To top this handgun, one would likely have to move into the custom or semi-custom market and dramatically increase the budget.
The Garrison Target is ready for general range use, home defense and even everyday carry for someone committed enough to pack a full-size 1911. It would also be ideal for single-stack competition, where it gives up nothing in terms of accuracy potential or dependability. For me, I’d simply place it in the “fun to shoot” category.

There are so many 1911 options on the market today that it would be easy to become overwhelmed by the choices. Many fine options are available, and no single brand has a monopoly on quality. That said, the Springfield Garrison lineup has really impressed me with its consistency and craftsmanship. With the addition of adjustable sights on the Target model, versatility can now be added to that list of strengths.
Springfield Armory’s Garrison Target is a well-built, accurate, reliable and attractive handgun available at a fair price. The addition of the adjustable rear sight represents a meaningful upgrade to a lineup of handguns that was already near the top of my list.
