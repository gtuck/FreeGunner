Ruger Harrier AR-15: The Ultimate Budget AR?

Performance on a Budget: Breaking Down the Ruger Harrier

 With an MSRP of just $699, the base model of the new Harrier AR-15 rifle from Ruger fits just about everybody’s budget. (Layne Simpson photo)

November 15, 2026

By Layne Simpson

Ruger jumped into the entry-level AR-15 market in 2014 by introducing the AR-556 rifle. I became so fond of the one I shot for a 
Shooting Times
 report that I bought it, and it has served quite well in 2-gun and 3-gun matches. The handy little rifle has also accounted for its share of feral hogs. Equipped with a Trijicon RMR sight, an INSIGHT light, and a Nosler SR-30ALTi suppressor, my AR-556 is the first gun I will reach for should things go really bad in my part of the world. But this report is about the reengineered Harrier rifle from Ruger. As of 2026, it has replaced the AR-556.
The Harrier shares its name with a bird of prey as well as a British jet fighter capable of vertical takeoff, and it is made in a large firearms manufacturing facility in Hebron, Kentucky, that had been owned by Anderson Manufacturing since 1983 and that Ruger had recently purchased. Rather than continuing production of guns made by Anderson, Ruger chose to build firearms of its own design, with the Harrier being the first to come off the production line there.
The Rifle
Two variations of the new Harrier are presently available. Model 28601 (MSRP: $699) has an M4-style stock and a full-length rail with 15 Picatinny slots at the top of its receiver and five slots out near the muzzle of the free-floating handguard. Model 28600 (MSRP: $749) has a Magpul DT collapsible stock with length-of-pull stops at 13.5, 12.56, 13.125, 11.5, and 10 inches. A thin, hard rubber pad with horizontal grooving is attached to the butt of the stock with a couple screws. The full-length rail of that model has close to 50 Picatinny slots over its entire surface. Otherwise, the two models are the same, with both having mil-spec buffer tubes and handguards with numerous M-LOK accessory attachment points. I chose the Model 28600 for this report, and according to my trusty digital scale, it weighs 6 pounds, 13 ounces.
The Harrier’s mil-spec trigger averaged 7.25 pounds of pull. (Layne Simpson photo)
The upper and lower receivers are machined to mil-spec dimensions from Type 7075 aluminum forgings and then given a Type III hardcoat anodized finish. A tension screw in the lower receiver is there to reduce looseness between it and the upper receiver. The lower receiver has all the standard AR-15 controls, including a two-position safety and a bolt release on its left-hand side and a magazine release on the opposite side. Nitriding hardens the exterior surfaces of the bolt and its carrier for wear and corrosion resistance. As specified decades ago for the original M16 military rifle, the two screws holding the gas key of the carrier in place are staked to prevent them from coming loose. The charging handle with its twin release tabs as well as the forward-assist and brass deflector are, shall we say, standard issue. I like the Magpul MOE-K2 grip, but those who don’t will find it to be easily replaced by a variety of aftermarket grips.

Slightly larger than the government contour, the 16.1-inch, carbon steel, six-groove barrel measures 0.730 inch at the muzzle where a “1:8” marking indicates its rifling twist rate. The barrel is threaded 1/2-28, and the rifle comes with an A2 birdcage-style flashhider attached. Two flats machined into its body make it easy to loosen and tighten with a small adjustable wrench.
The barrel is threaded 1/2-28, and the rifle comes with an A2 birdcage-style flashhider. (Layne Simpson photo)
When shooting the rifle, I removed the flashhider and used a 1/2-28 to 5/8-24 adapter from Silencer Central to attach a Nosler SR-30ALTi suppressor. Doing so virtually silenced the extremely loud muzzle blast from an AR-15 with a stubby barrel.
The barrel of the Harrier has a 
5.56mm NATO chamber
, and for the benefit of those who do not know how it differs from a .223 Remington chamber, I will add that the 5.56 chamber has a slightly longer throat. Equally important to know, the NATO version of the .223 Remington is commonly loaded to pressures in the neighborhood of 60,000 psi, whereas maximum for the .223 Remington is 55,000 psi. While .223 Remington ammo can be safely fired in a 5.56mm chamber, conversely is not recommended due to the difference in pressure along with the shorter throat of the .223 Remington chamber. Through the years I have fired thousands of rounds of .223 Remington ammo in 5.56mm rifles and never experienced a cycling or accuracy issue.

The Harrier has a mid-length gas system, and in my experience, that makes it more pleasant to shoot with a suppressor attached than an AR-15 with a carbine-length gas system due to considerably less gas backflow into my face. The trigger is mil-spec, so I was not surprised to feel a great deal of creep prior to the break. Average pull weight measured with a Lyman digital scale was 7.25 pounds. With the forearm of the Harrier snuggled firmly in a Lyman Match Bag, the rifle delivered what I consider to be excellent accuracy with a variety of 5.56 and .223 Remington ammo for an AR-15 in its price range. Accuracy was about the same with a Harris folding bipod attached to its handguard and the butt of the rifle resting on a sandbag.
Length-of-pull adjustment stops of the Magpul DT collapsible stock are 13.5, 12.56, 13.125, 11.5, and 10 inches. A thin, hard rubber pad with horizontal grooving is attached to the butt of the stock. (Layne Simpson photo)
The Ammo
Perhaps a bit of information on the test loads used would be of interest. Beginning with factory ammo, a number of companies offer both .223 Remington and 5.56mm loads. I chose 5.56mm ammunition from Black Hills, Federal, and Hornady with 75-grain and 77-grain bullets.
Perhaps the most interesting due to its history is the M855 “green tip” military loading of the 5.56mm I shot. Developed by Belgium during the 1970s, it was designated SS109 at the time, and it eventually became the standard loading for all members of NATO. The goal was to increase the penetrative power of the 5.56mm cartridge, and that was accomplished by making the core of the jacketed bullet a combination of lead and steel. When the U.S. military adopted the round, it was renamed M855, and giving the bullet a green tip helped troops differentiate it from the old M193 round with its all-lead core. A web search will turn up plenty of the ammo for sale, and while it can be legally owned under federal law, it is prohibited in some states.
I also tested .223 Remington ammo loaded by Barnes, Black Hills, Hornady, and HSM with bullets weighing from 50 to 69 grains.
The Results
Overall average accuracy at 100 yards was 1.67 inches for the 5.56 ammo and 1.74 inches for the .223 ammo. Obviously, there’s not much difference there.

When putting together handloads, I used Shooters World powders because that company has 5.56mm and .223 Remington data for a number of bullet weights. With a good supply of Berger bullets on hand, I chose those weighing 55, 60, 75, and 80 grains. In the hands of seasoned competitors, the AR-15 has proved to be accurate at 600 yards and beyond where extremely heavy, match-grade bullets are used. Berger recommends a rifling twist rate no slower than 1:8 for the 80-grain VLD Target, and since the barrel of the Harrier has that twist rate, I figured the rifle would send it downrange with acceptable accuracy—and it did just that. The bases of Starline 5.56mm cases I used are harder than the .223 Remington cases from that company and other companies as well. I prefer them when using .223 Remington data because case life is usually longer than for .223 Remington cases.
The author likes the Magpul MOE-K2 grip, but those who do not will find it to be easily replaced by a variety of aftermarket grips. (Layne Simpson photo)
During the slow-fire stage of across-the-course competition, competitors who load the heavier bullets seat them for an overall cartridge length as great as 2.550 inches, and that’s too long for the magazine of the AR-15 to handle. But just for the fun of it, I decided to try the extremely long Berger 90-grain VLD Target at an overall cartridge length of 2.540 inches. The Berger Bullets loading manual recommends a rifling twist rate no slower than 1:7, but I just had to see how the 1:8 twist of the Harrier rifle would handle it. Shooters World does not have data for a bullet of that weight, but the Berger manual lists 20.9 grains of VihtaVuori N540 for a velocity of 2,553 fps from a 24-inch barrel. Average velocity from the 16.1-inch barrel of the Harrier was 2,416 fps.
When single-loading cartridges in the AR-15, competitors replace its magazine with an Original Bob Sled (OBS) from Brownells. Here is how it works. When the bolt of the rifle is opened, a plunger in the device trips the bolt latch, holding the bolt open. A single round is then placed through the ejection port of the rifle and allowed to rest in the feed channel of the OBS. When the bolt release button of the rifle is pressed, the round is loaded. When that round is fired, the action cycles normally, and the OBS plunger trips the bolt catch to hold the bolt open and ready for the next round. I used one of the devices when shooting the Berger 90-grain bullet, and it worked perfectly. It is important to note that due to the floating firing pin of the AR-15 rifle, ammunition single-loaded in it should have mil-spec CCI No. 41 primers. The use of other Small Rifle primers with softer/thinner shot cups could result in a dangerous out-of-battery firing as the bolt slams forward.
So how did the Harrier with its 1:8 rifling twist handle the extremely long Berger 90-grain VLD Target? Holes in the target made by the passage of bullets indicated each was tumbling in flight. Even so, trying was fun.
After shooting the Harrier for accuracy, I rapid-fired several hundred rounds of 5.56 and .223 ammo at distant targets from the offhand position. The rifle was held firmly against my shoulder as well as in my hands. In addition to the PMAG 30-round magazine included with the rifle, I tried various other 30-round magazines, including those made of aluminum purchased many years ago from a military surplus store. A Surefire 100-round magazine completed the fun and games. Through it all, the Harrier did not malfunction in any way.
Final Thoughts
Those who are on a limited budget (and who is not these days?) might find this to be of interest. When introduced in 2014, the Ruger AR-556 was priced at $749. Adjusted for 12 years of inflation, that’s $1,057 in today’s dollars. The fact that the least expensive Harrier model sells for $699 is quite interesting, to say the least.
