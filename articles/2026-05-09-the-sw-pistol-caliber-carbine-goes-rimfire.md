The S&W Pistol-Caliber Carbine Goes Rimfire

Smith & Wesson's .22 LR M&P FPC 22 is perfect for a day on the shooting course, and it makes for an ideal training tool too.

 Shooting Smith & Wesson’s M&P FPC 22 was one of the best range days that I’d had in a while—and just what I needed to break the winter blues. (Frank Melloni photo)

May 09, 2026

By Frank Melloni

The pistol-caliber carbine has enjoyed tremendous success in recent years, with dozens of new designs hitting the market. Many of these have made their way through the doors at my company (Renaissance Firearms Instruction), with most students leaving thoroughly satisfied with their purchase and confident in their effectiveness. At the top of the list is the 
Smith & Wesson M&P FPC
, as it shares functionality with the company’s leading pistol. Shooting this light-recoiling long gun is indeed a thrilling experience, so much so that the cost of feeding it often enters the conversation. Sadly, as tight as things are, even the purchase of a few boxes of pistol ammo ought to be scrutinized. Invariably, this conversation always ends with adding a rimfire firearm to the collection.
Rimfire is Easier to Shoot
Being that we don’t sell guns to the general public, I never have a problem suggesting that a student purchase a companion firearm, particularly if it will aid training. If the shooter is receptive, I recommend nothing more often than a .22 LR. A rimfire gun solves the ammunition cost problem so well that one typically pays for itself in just a few thousand rounds. While collecting a bunch of differently styled guns is a hoot, it doesn’t help one stay proficient, particularly if the shooter isn’t really a “gun guy” and only wishes to stay practiced enough for the event of an emergency. In that increasingly more prevalent case, I almost always recommend the rimfire version of what they already own. Subbing in a rimfire has tremendous training application, much of which doesn’t meet the eye at first glance. One of my favorite aspects is that because the recoil is minimal, it doesn’t disguise shooting issues like flinching. This makes it exponentially easier to spot and correct a student who is suffering from this malady. Additionally, no firearm is quieter than a suppressed .22 LR shooting subsonic ammo, so most shooters find it comfortable enough to fire without earmuffs. That makes verbal instruction easier to give and receive. I’d like to think I’m unique in these discoveries, but the truth is that nearly every instructor worth their salt has the same mentality. To that end, Smith & Wesson recently released the latest incarnation of its very popular M&P FPC, now chambered in the world’s most popular rimfire cartridge.
When folded, Smith & Wesson’s rimfire M&P FPC 22 takes up little space, making for easy storage and transport. (Frank Melloni photo)
The Rimfire FPC (Folding Pistol Carbine)
Decoding the alphabet soup, M&P stands for Military & Police, while FPC is short for Folding Pistol Carbine. Upon first grasp, I noticed the familiar grip angle and texture, which can be tailored by swapping in one of the three included backstraps. While this feature carries more weight in a handgun, it plays a significant role in helping to keep the two feeling the same for the user who has both platforms. The magazine catch is also in the same location and, like others of the type, is reversible for left-handed operation. I appreciate considerations like this for the southpaw—I make up part of that demographic. However, learning that most righties have trouble converting a left-handed grip and stance to a right-handed one, I do all of my demonstrations right-handed. To that end, I appreciate ambidextrous controls even more. The FPC line delivers this functionality in both the charging handle and the bolt catch, making it attractive to either dexterity or folks who prefer to switch it up. We cannot say the same for the crossbolt safety, as lefties will have an easier time putting it on than taking it off, but the trigger bow safety doesn’t care how it’s engaged, so in practice, this is an ambi control as well.
While the controls are pretty standard, the folding latch is likely going to get more attention. As promised, the gun can be cracked in half, and it’s facilitated by a lever on the right side of the receiver. Intelligently designed, it needs to be lifted away from the firearm to be engaged. This staves off the possibility of hitting it under stress and creating a malfunction. Once swung open, the fore-end can be locked into the charging handle to hold it in this position, making it easier to stow and pull from a bag or carrying case. Nothing must be depressed to snap the two halves together, so deployment is swift and trouble-free.

The last control on the M&P FPC 22 is located in the stock, and it is designed to hold a pair of extra magazines in place. As this carbine uses the same mags as the 22X pistol, this puts an extra 40 rounds on deck. Pressing the right side releases the left mag, and depressing the left side sets the right one free. As the gun isn’t capable of firing in the folded position, you could theoretically keep a filled magazine in the grip for a total of 60 rounds “on the gun” in a safe manner.
Melloni found the S&W M&P FPC 22 to be sufficiently accurate for its purposes. (Frank Melloni photo)
Like the rest of the FPC family, the FPC 22 comes standard with an M-LOK fore-end with a full-length Picatinny section at the 12 o’clock position. Because the gun folds to the side, the rail can be utilized for nearly anything, but I think the gun pairs best with a red-dot optic or other similar-size aiming device. Running through it is a slim-profile 16.25-inch barrel, which keeps the weight at just a hair over four pounds. Looking at its twist rate reveals that S&W truly understands current industry rimfire trends; it’s cut with faster 1:15 rifling rather than the more standard 1:16. This undoubtedly is to help stabilize heavier bullets—the kind typically used in subsonic ammunition. And with its 1/2-28 threaded muzzle, the M&P FPC 22 makes an exceptional suppressor host for near-silent training sessions.
While I would like to see this gun come with iron sights (even a pair of cheap sights would do), I can accept that it’s “optic ready” instead. When choosing said optic, it’s important to realize the Picatinny section places an optic a bit farther from the eye than a more conventional long gun. This means that you’ll want an optic with extended or unlimited eye relief, or at the very least a mount to place the optic closer to your face.

.22 Rifle Shooting Results
All fingers point to using a red-dot optic on the FPC 22, but as proper firearms evaluation involves determining accuracy potential, a magnified option was going to be a better fit for my shooting session. Digging through my scopes, I found a Primary Arms SLx MicroPrism that offers 5X magnification. Although the bullet-drop-compensator reticle is intended for 5.56 ammo, the central chevron would be all I needed for this test. More importantly, the prism includes a cantilever mount that can be set in either direction, so I was able to place the ocular bell three inches closer to my face.
Being presented with a fast-twist barrel steered me toward some of the heavier ammo options on the market. Tipping the scale at 45 grains is 
CCI’s Suppressor Max
. Developed for rifle applications, this load delivers a segmented hollowpoint projectile about as fast as you can without cracking the sound barrier. This ammo was likely a response to Winchester’s Subsonic Max with its 42-grain bullet, which exhibits classic lead hollowpoint composition and was also on the menu. Last into my bag was SK’s Semi-Auto Rifle load, which is built with a lubricated 40-grain lead roundnose bullet. A revision of its revered match ammo, this loading is made to function reliably in semiautomatic guns.
Having 1/2-28 threading at my disposal, I decided to add a 
Dead Air Mask
 silencer to my test kit. Using a conventional baffle stack, this suppressor boasts little to no first-round pop and, although slightly heavier than some of the other ones out there, wouldn’t add much to the overall weight of the M&P FPC 22 package that I put together.
A unique magazine carrier in the buttstock houses a pair of spare 20-round magazines. The FPC 22 comes with three magazines. (Frank Melloni photo)
To be sure I was getting accuracy and reliability data from the carbine in its raw state, I fired the gun from a benchrest, shooting groups without the suppressor attached. Zeroing was relatively easy, but as with any other rifle with a pistol grip, I had to be creative to work around it. During the process, I fired a few rounds of each type of ammo and am pleased to report flawless feeding and ejection across the board. Going back to the days when I fired my first M&P 22-15, I was reminded that Smith & Wesson was always good at making reliable rimfire variants, and I was glad to see they hadn’t forgotten the recipe. Every load was capable of locking the action to the rear, and returning it to battery was equally effortless, whether it was via the bolt release or a slight tug on the charging handle.
Initial groups were acceptable, but we shouldn’t go into this expecting a match-grade rifle. The FPC 22 was built to emulate its centerfire brethren, and unfortunately, that does come at the cost of a bit of accuracy. However, on that front, it excelled. I had tested the 9mm Luger version previously, and the manual of arms was identical, sans recoil spring tension. The pencil-thin barrel wasn’t doing it any favors, but understanding intended use is critical before judging performance. Shaving weight here makes it more shootable, which is going to be attractive for new and smaller-statured shooters. In this light, things even out as they are better capable of putting lead on target than if they had something built with a cumbersome bull barrel.

To test the above, I filled all three magazines and took the carbine for a walk through my woodland target system. This part of the property houses over a dozen animal-shaped silhouettes from Caldwell and Birchwood Casey, placed at distances from 25 to 109 yards. The point of this course is to test a firearm from conventional field positions and, admittedly, help me learn how to judge shorter distances and trajectories. To ensure I would hear the report on steel, I canned up the FPC and enjoyed a quiet shooting experience. Shooting the gun from the standing position required next to no effort, as the balance point was much closer to my core, and even with the scope and suppressor, the package was barely more than five pounds with a full complement of ammunition on board. Running through the course, I was able to reload from the stock-mounted magazine carrier, which I enjoyed. I found it to be more than just a gimmick, and with a little practice, it can be just as fast as pulling a fresh mag from your belt.
When I was finished with the course, I folded the FPC and placed it neatly into the trunk of my UTV and proceeded back to the house. Despite the frigid weather, it was one of the best range days that I’d had in a while—and just what I needed to break the winter blues.
This brings me to my closing point, and that is .22 LR firearms are excellent for indoor ranges, and they just might be the ticket to training all year long if your area has restrictive seasons. Adding one of these to your collection is a great way to ensure that you get your reps in, and if you already have a centerfire FPC, it will only serve to further cement their manual of arms into habit. If nothing else, it’ll take a little of the sting out of your ammunition expenses, and these days I’ll take every penny I can get.
