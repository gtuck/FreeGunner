Wilson Combat Experior Elite Review: Best Double-Stack?

Does Wilson Combat's new double-stack 1911 live up to its elite name?

October 20, 2026

By Brad Fitzpatrick

Bill Wilson has had quite a career. The one-time watchmaker turned to making 1911 pistols and has won championships in various handgun sports around the world with his guns. Like all true 1911 fans, Wilson has a deep affection for John Browning’s legendary handgun, but Wilson—and the company that bears his name—has never been afraid to advance the design of the iconic 1911.
That’s certainly the case with Wilson Combat’s new eXperior Elite Commander double stack. Sure, it’s a 1911, but the eXperior Elite has advanced 
the original 1911 design 
in almost every way. It’s a 1911 for the next generation of shooters.
The eXperior Elite is chambered in 9mm, which is just one departure from the original design. It’s also one of the growing crop of double-stack 1911 pistols, and that means you can load 18 rounds in the included metal magazine. The gun also features an aluminum rail with integral light rail—which brings outstanding strength with light weight, along with the versatility provided by the rail—as well as an integrated aluminum magazine well.
The barrel on the eXperior Elite Commander is a 4.25-inch match-grade, fluted bull. It is flush fit and reverse crowned.

On the Wilson Combat website, you’ll see one of the two highlighted models features black cherry grips, but don’t be misled. Those panels aren’t actual wood but rather Wilson’s G-10 grips featuring its Starburst with Trak texture pattern. Black cherry is one of three color choices; the other two are gray/black and black. My sample had the latter, and the Trak texture on the front- and backstraps blends nicely with the grips. Regardless of panel color, pewter Wilson Combat medallions grace the left and right sides of the grip.
The ambidextrous Bullet Proof safety is plenty wide, but the gun lacks the original 1911 grip safety. Both the slide stop and mag release are perfectly fitted and easy to operate. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Safety
There are substantial differences between the original 1911 grip design and Wilson’s new eXperior Elite. For starters, the traditional grip profile has been modernized to include an aggressively curved mainspring housing that fills the hand and keeps the gun comfortably planted when firing long strings. This promotes a proper, high handhold on the pistol, and the upswept, well-designed beavertail locks the hand in place.
One standard 1911 feature missing on the eXperior Elite is the grip safety. Originally, the U.S. government wanted an additional, redundant safety on its battle sidearm, and that’s what prompted John Browning to incorporate the passive grip safety into the original design.

The addition of a grip safety became part of the 1911’s DNA, and so it stuck, but there are those who prefer their pistols without one. If you’ve spent much time around 1911 aficionados you’ve seen someone tape down that safety, and there are practical reasons to delete this feature.
First, in times of stress, you may not have the full-hand grip required to fire the gun. Despite the intuitive design, deactivating the grip safety can be challenging for some shooters based on hand structure, and I even saw one new shooter claim the 1911 they were shooting was “broken” only to rotate the pistol and have it fire unexpectedly when their adjusted handhold depressed the grip safety. The deletion of the grip safety also streamlines the grip.
The manual safety remains, of course, and it’s Wilson’s ambidextrous Bullet Proof part. CNC machined from billet steel, the safety offers ample surface area on the left and right sides to enable quick, efficient deactivation. And because it’s a Wilson Combat gun with components that are fitted by hand by a gunsmith, the safety operates with precision, snicking on and off positively and without any slop. With a width of just under 0.2 inch, the safety is easy to locate and operate on either side of the gun.
Precision machining virtually eliminates gaps, and the fluted bull barrel, slide and frame function together flawlessly. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Other Features
Other controls include a Bullet Proof magazine release and machined slide stop that are easy to operate. The trigger has a medium-size pad, and break weight on my sample averaging four pounds.
The eXperior frame sports a generous undercut below the trigger guard. It’s smooth and nicely rounded, so it doesn’t irritate the shooter’s finger. The back of the grip is also smooth just below the beavertail, and like the undercut it’s nicely rounded for a natural, comfortable grip.

The aluminum frame has a black anodized finish, and the light rail incorporates two mounting slots. The stainless steel slide features Wilson’s Tri-Top design with 30 lines-per-inch checkering on top and new, aggressive serrations front and rear. 1911 serrations have, historically, been rather thin and shallow, but the deep, angular cuts on the eXperior Elite slide offer ample purchase for operating this gun under duress.
Of course, Wilson adds special aesthetic touches everywhere, even to the serrations. There are narrow channels cut into the serrations that add an extra dash of sophistication. The slide is cut so it widens about 0.2 inch toward the rear, and this also helps with manipulation. The carry cuts and ball end mill cuts add an extra aesthetic touch to an already good-looking 1911, and each of these guns comes with Wilson Combat’s Concealment Bullet Proof hammer.
Underneath the optic cover lies Wilson’s excellent pin system for direct attachment of a red dot. Pins for RMR, DeltaPoint Pro and RMSc are provided. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Optics Ready
Wilson Combat’s ground-breaking Project 1 pistol hinted at some of the forward-facing concepts coming to the company’s 1911 pistols, and one of those was an easy-to-use pin design that eliminated the need for optic adapter plates and made mounting a reflex sight on the pistol straightforward. That same plate-less pin design carries over to the eXperior Elite.
Wilson Combat includes a removable slide cover plate with each pistol, and when this is removed a series of holes are visible in the top of the slide. The pins for these holes come in color-coded sealable bags: red for the Trijicon RMR footprint; blue for DeltaPoint Pro; and green for RMSc. Those footprints cover most of the optics on the market.
Simply arrange the pins in the appropriate cutouts in the top of the slide and place the optic in position. Eliminating plates not only simplifies the mounting process, it also allows the optic to ride lower on the slide. The pin/screw anchor mechanism works efficiently and prevents the optic from flying off under the stress of heavy shooting.
The black U-notch rear sight is part of the optic cover plate, and the 40-lpi checkering on its face extends down the back of the slide. (Brad Fitzpatrick photo)
Of course, if you’re a purist there’s nothing wrong with reliable iron sights. The Wilson’s cover plate includes a U-notch Battlesight that is dovetailed and screwed in place. The rear of the sight and sight plate have 40-lpi serrations, and the serrations extend down the rear of the slide. The front sight consists of a post with a snag-free fiber-optic bar.
Details
Wilson Combat is known for its custom 1911s, so while the eXperior Elite does have a set of standard components and specifications, you can still create a gun that is all your own. If you don’t like the all-black Armor-Tuff, you can choose to have your gun finished in any of the Armor-Tuff coatings the company offers. These include black/burnt bronze, black/coyote tan, black/FDE and black/gray.
You can also order the gun with a Trijicon RMR already installed, although the slide will be cut for Wilson’s pin system whether you go with an optic or not.

The fit and finish work on these guns is flawless, as it is with every other Wilson Combat pistol I’ve ever reviewed. That’s largely because there’s a gunsmith in Arkansas who fretted over every aspect of your gun’s build, creating a machine that was hand-tuned and ready to perform.
Small touches include the countersunk slide stop pin, and the optic cover plate fits perfectly with the slide. Similarly, there’s virtually no gap between the slide and the crowned muzzle.
But it’s only when the pistol is actually in your hand and you’re operating the slide that you can really appreciate why this gun costs four times what most factory 1911s do. That hand-honed slide/frame fit makes the eXperior operate like the precision machine that it is.
There’s also a much more practical, workaday side to this pistol’s design. While 1911 takedown is more complicated than modern polymer-frame guns, and that’s a hurdle for some gun buyers, the Wilson Combat is designed to be incredibly simple to disassemble.
For one, the barrel doesn’t have a bushing. I was once with Bill Wilson at his ranch when another writer asked why the original 1911 had a bushing, and Bill’s response was, “Ask John Browning.” The eXperior Elite doesn’t require tools to fieldstrip like some 1911 derivatives, and removing the slide stop allows you to disassemble the pistol. Reassembly is a breeze.

Performance
Now, it’s time to get down to business: How does this thing shoot? Maybe a better question for a gun costing four grand is best reframed as, “What does it do that my $800 1911 won’t?” Fair enough.
For starters, this gun holds 18 rounds in that metal magazine but doesn’t feel humongous. There’s ample room for very large hands, and for a Commander-style 1911 this gun stays remarkably planted in the hand—making this one of the easiest uncompensated 9mms to shoot.
That’s thanks in part to the weight and grip geometry and also to the Starburst/Trak G-10 grips and Trak frame texturing. The grip texture is pretty aggressive if you are shooting hundreds of rounds without gloves, though, so bear that in mind on extended range or training sessions.
The trigger pull is crisp and clean, and the controls are really easy to operate. The eXperior Elite will make you feel like a better shooter than you are, and its accuracy is where this gun really stands out. I fired several groups with the eXperior Elite at 25 yards that measured under an inch, and one five-shot, 25-yard cluster with Hornady’s American Gunner spanned just 0.65 inch.
It’s hard to measure the amount of time and work that goes into a custom 1911 pistol, but groups of that size—and there were others that nearly matched it—offer tangible evidence why these guns cost four grand.

Final Thoughts
The eXperior Elite is out of the price range of some shooters, and I get that. Also, although the Commander was 
the
 carry 1911 for years, I think the eXperior Commander is probably pushing the practical limits of everyday carry. It’s almost eight inches long and 1.4 inches wide and weighs almost 41 ounces loaded. That’s big for concealment by most measures.
But I’ll also tell you that if you can save the dough and get just one 9mm pistol, this one is very, very hard to beat. The 1911 DNA is still there, but the eXperior Elite is proof that Wilson Combat isn’t afraid to continue the work that John Browning started over a century ago.
