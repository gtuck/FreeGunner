Chiappa 1860 Spencer Carbine: Replica of a Civil War Legend

A Modern Recreation of the Historic Repeater That Changed American Warfare.

 Thanks to Chiappa Firearms, you can own a working reproduction of the world’s first metallic-cartridge-firing military repeating firearm. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

August 29, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

My compadres at our sister publication 
Firearms News
 once said the Spencer repeating rifle “truly revolutionized firearms.” The Spencer was patented by Christopher M. Spencer on March 6, 1860, and was of lever-action, falling-block design. According to one source I consulted, it was the world’s first metallic-cartridge-firing military repeating firearm. Uniquely, it had a magazine tube through the buttstock that held seven rounds of No. 56 rimfire (a.k.a. .56-56) cartridges. That cartridge contained a one-ounce ball and 42 grains of blackpowder. The rifle received the personal endorsement of President Abraham Lincoln and was eventually accepted by the U.S. military in 1863, halfway through the Civil War. On July 2, 1863, in the hands of the 2nd Michigan Cavalry Brigade, under the command of Brigadier General George Armstrong Custer, the Spencer carbine famously turned the tide during the Battle of Gettysburg. However, the rifle version, with a 30-inch barrel, was first to see action in the hands of Federal sailors, infantrymen, and cavalry soldiers (yes, cavalrymen fought on foot with the long-barreled rifles), and the U.S. Navy was first to purchase the Spencer rifle. Eventually, Spencer carbines with 22-inch barrels were produced and saw action mostly with the cavalry. Actually, in the end, more of the carbine version were produced than the rifle version, and after the Civil War, carbines generally had 20-inch barrels. According to Walter D. Mosher, who wrote about the original Spencer in the May 1973 issue of 
Shooting Times
, the “remarkable” Spencer design was revolutionary because it could dispense seven rounds in less than a minute. By comparison, a muzzleloading rifle musket, which was the standard military rifle of the day, could deliver two shots per minute, and that was in the hands of a highly trained rifleman. Plus, 10 extra Spencer tubes preloaded with seven rounds of ammo each could be carried in a special box, giving the soldier 70 rounds for rapid fire. Mosher said the Spencer won the day, and even more importantly, it was a transitional design that made better and more practical repeating firearms possible.
Chiappa’s reproduction Spencer rifle features a 30-inch, round, blued barrel and a walnut buttstock and full-length forearm. It weighs 10.6 pounds. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Knowing that 
Shooting Times
 readers appreciate historical firearms, we arranged to receive a reproduction Spencer rifle with 30-inch barrel from Chiappa Firearms for this report. And even better than the original rifles, per our request, our sample is chambered for a much more readily available chambering than the original and obsolete No. 56 rimfire. We ordered ours chambered for .45 Colt. Other chamberings listed on the Chiappa website include .56-50 Spencer (a centerfire cartridge developed by Springfield Armory in 1861 that carried a 350-grain bullet and 45 grains of blackpowder) and .44-40. Chiappa also offers the carbine version of the Spencer, and it comes with a 20-inch barrel and can be had in the same three chamberings as the full-length rifle.
Let’s take a good look at the sample rifle.
Features
Like I said, the rifle version comes with a 30-inch barrel. It’s rounded, and it features a nice, smooth, deep blued finish. The rifle has three barrel bands, with the middle one having a loop for a sling.

The long forearm (it ends 3.25 inches from the muzzle) and buttstock are walnut, and according to Chiappa, they are given a hand oiled finish. The finish looks glossy to my eyes, but it is well done. The buttstock has a sling swivel and a casecolored steel buttplate, and as I said earlier there is a magazine tube inside the buttstock. (I detail how to load it later in this article.)
The rifle’s receiver is casecolored, and to my eyes it is really attractive, with rich gold, gray, and blue hues. The action is a levered, falling-block type, and the steel lever and the steel trigger are casecolored.
The rifle’s hammer is blued steel, and so are the sights. Speaking of the sights, the rear has a shallow “V” and a folding ladder, and the front sight is a plain blade. And I have to say that front blade is quite narrow and fine!

The rifle’s rear sight has a shallow “V” and a folding ladder. The front sight is a very narrow and fine blade. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The trigger pull on our rifle was smooth and consistent, but it was extremely heavy. My old RCBS High Tension Trigger Scale measured it right at 9.5 pounds of pull over a series of five measurements. I think that’s the heaviest rifle trigger I’ve encountered during my entire 35 years here at 
Shooting Times
. I was unable to find any reports of how heavy original Spencer rifles were in terms of their trigger pulls, so I can’t say if this one is unusual in that regard, but this heavy trigger pull and the fine sights made shooting a real challenge for me and my less-than-stellar eyesight. However, I managed to turn in some decent accuracy nonetheless. (More about that later.)
The rifle’s length of pull is 13 inches. It is 47 inches long overall. And it weighs 10.6 pounds.
The Spencer loads in a pretty unique way. Before I detail how that is accomplished, I must point out that Chiappa stipulates that flatnose bullets are the only type to be used in the Spencer, stating that the use of pointed bullets is dangerous and could result in accidental primer ignition. With that in mind, point the rifle in a safe direction with the muzzle pointing down. Make sure the lever is in the upper position against the rifle. With the thumb, press the upper end of the magazine tube base located in the center of the buttplate 90 degrees downward to the right and withdraw the tube completely from the rifle. Holding the rifle at an angle so that the cartridges slide down the magazine instead of dropping in, load the ammo nose-first. After all rounds have been placed into the magazine, insert the magazine tube into the magazine well until the tube’s base is even and 90 degrees to the right. Rotate the magazine tube base 90 degrees to the left, back to its original position.
To fire the Spencer rifle, move the lever to its down-most position so that the breechblock picks up a round from the magazine. Bring the lever back up to the upmost position to chamber a round. Shoulder the rifle, aim at the target, and pull the hammer back two clicks. Place your finger on the trigger and squeeze when ready to fire. After the shot fires, push the lever down so that the empty case extracts and falls to the rear. Pull the lever up to chamber a fresh round. Working the lever smartly is required.
The Spencer’s innovative magazine tube is inside the buttstock. To load, remove the magazine tube, slide cartridges into the magazine nose-first, and then insert the magazine tube. Our rifle’s magazine tube holds seven rounds of .45 Colt ammo. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Loaded rounds can be emptied without firing if the shooter chooses by lowering the lever to its lowest position, pointing the muzzle down, removing the magazine tube, and elevating the muzzle upward enough to allow the cartridges to slide out into the hand or onto a soft surface.

Performance
So, how does the Spencer rifle in .45 Colt shoot? Well, let me just say shooting a reproduction of an innovative, historical gun design can be a whole lot of fun, and I certainly had fun shooting this one. I had four .45 Colt factory loads that met the flatnose bullet requirement, and I fired them for accuracy from a Caldwell Lead Sled rifle rest at a range of 50 yards. As I alluded to earlier, these sights and my poor eyes just don’t play well together. Still, by virtue of the 
Lead Sled
, which enabled me to maintain a very steady, consistent sight picture, and the extremely low felt recoil that came with firing the pistol cartridge in a 10.6-pound rifle, I was pleased to get five-shot group averages that did not exceed 3.75 inches. I also tracked the three-shot group averages, and those were much better (as expected), averaging 2.50 inches or less. The best three-shot group average was exactly 1.00 inch, and it came with the Winchester 250-grain Cowboy Action ammo. That load also had the best five-shot group average, coming in at 2.50 inches. By the way, all group averages are for three strings, and the results are listed in the accompanying chart.
The reproduction Spencer is offered chambered for .56-50, .44-40, and .45 Colt. Our sample was chambered for .45 Colt. Chiappa stipulates shooting lead flatnose ammo only. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
I did have a little trouble at the bench that I feel obligated to mention. With both the Ultramax 250-grain ammo and the PMC 250-grain load, I had repeated trouble with extraction. Every single one of those fired cases stuck in the chamber, and I simply could not get the single lane extractor to pull them out by working the lever. Luckily, I had a cleaning rod with me at my shooting range, and it easily poked out the fired cases lickety split.
The original Spencer rifle and carbine played an important part during the American Civil War and subsequent Indian Wars. According to 
Flayderman’s Guide to Antique American Firearms … and their Values
, 9th edition, approximately 144,500 were made by the Spencer Repeating Rifle Co. of Boston, Massachusetts, with the U.S. government purchasing 107,372 of them. Thanks to Chiappa Firearms, shooters today can have a working reproduction of this revolutionary design.
