Handgun Recoil 101: How much recoil do handgun calibers produce? 

Are you curious about how much recoil your handgun produces? You'll want to read this report.

 Recoil force is calculated based on five things—gun weight, bullet weight, bullet velocity, powder weight, and the velocity of the gas that exits the barrel. (Brad Miller PhD photo)

May 23, 2026

By Brad Miller PhD

One thing I often see in gun magazines, on the internet, and on YouTube are the comments, “It has no recoil,” and “It feels like shooting a .22.” I see this from amateurs and well-respected experts alike. Well, every cartridge and gun combination has recoil, even airguns. And as far as a gun’s recoil feeling like a .22, let’s look at the numbers before we decide.
Calculating Recoil
How much recoil do handgun calibers produce? I did some math to compare the recoil of various handgun cartridges. In order to standardize the comparison, all calculations are for a handgun that weighs two pounds. That way you can see how the recoil of different cartridges compares in a gun of the same weight.
Recoil force is calculated based on five things—gun weight, bullet weight, bullet velocity, powder weight, and the velocity of the gas that exits the barrel.
For these calculations, I used the ammunition manufacturers’ bullet weight and velocity as listed in their catalogs. For cartridges I had available to me, I pulled the bullets and weighed the powder charges. For cartridges not available to me, I used powder weights listed in handloading manuals. Those calibers are indicated in the chart at the end of the article.

The powder weight matters because it is part of the ejecta (everything that comes out of the barrel), so it must be included. And the speed of the gas produced by the burning powder also has to be included. When the bullet exits, the gas pressure, previously blocked by the bullet, blasts out. This gives it a jet effect. Newton’s third law of motion says that for every action there is an opposite and equal reaction, so as the gas blasts forward, it produces an equal force rearward. That’s why it has to be included in recoil calculations.
There is debate on the speed of the gas. The gas speed at bullet exit exceeds the bullet’s speed, but by how much? How do you assign a number to muzzle blast? I won’t go into the details here but will use the value that SAAMI suggests for calculations. For handguns, the value is 1.5X. That is, the speed of gas ejecta is calculated as 1.5 times the speed of the bullet. (The gas speed for rifles is 1.75X.)
Assigning a single value to gas speed is probably not right because gas pressure at the muzzle varies depending on the cartridge, powder, and barrel length, but since the gas velocity under all those conditions is not actually measured, a single value is applied.

America’s sweetheart, the .45 ACP, does not need much powder to launch its chubby 230-grain bullets. Service loads like this produce around 70 percent more recoil than the 9mm Luger. But light .45 target loads with 185-grain bullets produce no more recoil than the 9mm. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
The Effect of Powder Weight
The effect of powder weight can change the recoil even when looking at a single caliber. In fact, if you compare two different powders that push the same bullet to the same speed, the higher powder weight creates more recoil force.
Here’s an example based on actual chronographed speeds from a .45 ACP pistol using two different powders. When pushing a 230-grain bullet to a velocity of 850 fps, the Hodgdon Titegroup powder charge is 5.2 grains, and the Accurate No. 7 powder charge is 9.9 grains. Calculations with a 2-pound handgun say that Titegroup produces 6.48 ft-lbs of recoil, while Accurate No. 7 produces 6.87 ft-lbs of recoil for the same velocity. That’s about a 6 percent increase. The different recoil of different powders is real and can be felt and measured.
Powder weight can make a big contribution to recoil. Here’s an example. The rimfire .22 Magnum cartridge produces 0.78 ft-lbs of recoil. A good chunk of that is from the large powder charge, which weighed 5.8 grains from the one I pulled. If you drop the 5.8-grain powder charge from the calculation, the .22 Magnum produces only 0.52 ft-lbs of recoil. This means the powder itself is responsible for 33 percent of the recoil. For comparison, powder only adds 12 percent to the recoil of the .22 LR.
And you’ll find this with other rounds that use a large amount of powder. For the 
.357 Magnum with a 158-grain bullet
, the powder is responsible for 26 percent of the recoil. But the powder is only responsible for 7 percent of the recoil for the .38 Special with the same bullet weight. The powder alone can have a big role in “bringing the hurt” to magnum cartridges.
Let’s look at the data. First, what about .22 LR recoil? Using a standard-weight 40-grain bullet, CCI says it reaches 1,060 fps from a handgun. (The length of the handgun barrel is not indicated.) A powder charge of 1.5 grains produces 0.32 ft-lbs of recoil in a 2-pound gun. The .22 Short produces half that (0.15 ft-lbs). As I pointed out earlier, the .22 Magnum more than doubles the recoil to 0.78 ft-lbs.

What about small centerfire pistol rounds? The smallest, and weakest, centerfire is the .25 ACP. It shoots a 50-grain bullet at 748 fps and produces 0.24 ft-lbs of recoil. This is less than the .22 LR (0.32 ft-lbs), but the .25 ACP ballistics are from a short 2.5-inch barrel. To make the comparison fair, we have to know what speed the .22 LR runs at from a short barrel, too. I found a source that fired .22 LR from a Taurus 22 TUC pistol with a 2.5-inch barrel. Several 40-grain rounds produced an average of 800 fps. At that speed, the .22 LR makes only 0.18 ft-lbs of recoil. That’s just 75 percent of the .25 ACP’s recoil in the same barrel length. See, even the tiny .25 ACP has more recoil than the .22 LR.
The revolvers used for shooting the .357 Magnum Remington 125-grain jacketed loads listed in the chart include (front to back) an S&W Model 360, an S&W Model 60, an S&W Model 686, and an S&W Model 686 Plus. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
Recoil Velocity
I’ve included the calculation for recoil velocity because it helps to understand why some calibers have more bite than others. The faster the recoil velocity, the greater the pain. One place this really shows up is with gun weight. Lighter guns recoil faster, and for some guns this actually stings. A good example for the various gun weights is guns chambered in .357 Magnum. Some of these guns are small and very lightweight, and their recoil is painful.
For this example, I used the actual velocity of Remington .357 Magnum ammo with a 125-grain jacketed bullet in my guns, and this data is shown in the .357 Magnum chart. This ammo is rated at 1,450 fps from a 4.0-inch barrel. I fired it in four guns: an S&W Model 360 with a 1.88-inch barrel, an S&W Model 60 with a 2.12-inch barrel, an S&W Model 686 with a 4.0-inch barrel, and an S&W Model 686 Plus with a 7.0-inch barrel. Their weights are shown in the chart along with the chronographed velocities. As expected, velocity decreased with the shorter barrels.
The lightest gun weighed 14.9 ounces, and the heaviest weighed 48.2 ounces. Recoil with the 14.9-ounce gun was 11.42 ft-lbs, and with the 48.2-ounce gun it was 7.15 ft-lbs. The recoil was 1.6 times greater with the lighter gun. But this tells only part of the story. Recoil velocity increased even more. Recoil velocity with the 14.9-ounce gun was 28.09 fps, and with the 48.2-ounce gun it was 12.35 fps. The lighter gun’s recoil velocity was 2.3 times faster than the heavy gun’s. What the shooter notices is that the 14.9-ounce gun stings a lot. It’s like catching a 100-mph fastball barehanded. I’ve also fired this ammo in an S&W Model 340 PD with a titanium cylinder that weighs 11.8 ounces (0.74 pound). I haven’t chronographed it in this gun, but if it shoots the same speed as it does in the Model 360 with the same-length barrel, the recoil force increases to 14.42 ft-lbs (like a .41 Magnum in a 2-pound gun), and recoil velocity jumps to 35.47 fps (close to the speed of the .454 Casull in a 2-pound gun). It was horrible! I’ll never do it again.
It’s a Compelling Story
The .357 Magnum chart shows another interesting statistic. We already know that barrel length affects velocity, and longer barrels produce faster speeds. Longer barrels, and heavier frames, also add more weight, which helps to reduce recoil. That is apparent when comparing the recoil force from the snubnose guns to the 4.0-inch-barreled gun. The short-barrel handguns were lighter and had less velocity but more recoil force.
But the 7.0-inch-barreled gun turns this around a bit. Recoil from the 4.0-inch barrel was calculated as 6.82 ft-lbs. The 7.0-inch barrel had gained quite a bit of velocity (224 fps), and even though it was some 10 ounces heavier, it proved to produce more recoil force (7.15 ft-lbs) than the 4.0-inch barrel. So, you’d expect to feel that at the range, right? Nope. Recoil with the 7.0-inch gun feels much less than with the 4.0-inch gun. The 7.0-inch gun has a full underlug barrel, so there’s a lot of weight hanging out there on the nose. Instead of feeling like it has more recoil than the 4.0-inch gun, it feels like it has less recoil. It’s not just a difference in muzzle rise, it’s a difference in felt recoil. It’s an example of how weight distribution affects the feel of recoil, regardless of what the math says.
I don’t know that any of these calculated recoil values are unexpected, per se. We expect lighter rounds to have less recoil and the heavier rounds to have more, though the magnitude of the difference can be surprising. Certainly, the Magnum rounds, and especially the large-caliber Magnum rounds, produce recoil that make you question your sanity if you buy one.
The .22 Magnum (left) uses a lot more powder than the .22 LR (right) to produce about 34 percent more speed with a 40-grain bullet in a handgun. The result is 2.4 times more recoil. (Brad Miller PhD photo)
One thing that surprised me was the amount of powder in a .22 Magnum cartridge. That tiny case held 5.8 grains of powder, which is 3.9 times the powder of a .22 LR cartridge. Part of the reason is the longer .22 Magnum case means a longer cartridge overall length. This itself will reduce pressure and lower speed if using the same amount and kind of powder as the .22 LR. To make up for that, you have to increase the powder charge to get the same velocity, and since the Magnum pushes bullets much faster than the LR, it requires even more powder. But I didn’t expect it to be as much as it was. Live and learn.

The numbers can serve as a guide in some cases. The .38 Special is one such example. Nominal loads for the .38 Special are with a 158-grain bullet. These are not especially powerful, but recoil starts to go up in light revolvers, and I’ve seen shooters complain that standard .38 ammo is unpleasant in these guns. It discourages their use. In this case, the 130- and 148-grain loads can reduce recoil by around 24 percent, which is useful to know and can make a big difference to these shooters.
The feel of recoil of some bullet weights can be different in spite of them producing about the same recoil force. For instance, recoil force of the various 9mm loads with different bullet weights is not that different, as is their recoil velocity. But they might feel different when comparing 115-grain bullets to 147-grain bullets. The often-used explanation is that the lighter bullets have to accelerate faster to reach their speed, and that makes them feel snappy compared to the heavy bullets that don’t reach the same velocity.
The .500 S&W Magnum has 300 times the recoil of the .22 LR, and it feels like it! (Brad Miller PhD photo)
It should come as no surprise that the handgun recoil king is the .500 S&W Magnum, producing 97.81 ft-lbs of recoil in a 2-pound gun. It has 1.56 times the recoil of its closest competitor, the .460 S&W Magnum. The other big kickers are the .454 Casull and the .50 AE, which are nearly the same at close to 47 ft-lbs of recoil. Recoil from the big brutes is truly hard to imagine. And they stand out from the recoil of the .44 Magnum (17.71 ft-lbs), once the most powerful handgun cartridge in the world. The .500 S&W has 5.5 times the recoil of the .44 Magnum.
Thankfully, .500 S&W Magnum revolvers weigh much more than two pounds. An S&W gun that weighs 4.23 pounds (69.1 ounces) reduces the recoil to 45.3 ft-lbs. That’s still horribly too much for many people, especially me. My limit is the .44 Magnum. An S&W Model 29 in .44 Magnum with a 4.0-inch barrel weighs 2.74 pounds (43.9 ounces), and that weight brings the recoil force down to 12.91 ft-lbs and at a recoil speed of 17.40 fps. This is still too much for many folks, but I can tolerate it for a while. Oh, and S&W’s lightweight scandium-frame .44 Magnum weighs just 26.3 ounces, and that takes the recoil up to 21.54 ft-lbs with a recoil velocity of 29.04 fps. Ouch!
Felt recoil is a whole other topic and is subject to many factors. Gun weight, obviously, but also semiautomatic or revolver, operation (blowback versus locked breech), grip design and composition, height of bore axis, and the simple difference between people. You might look at the calculations in the chart and say, “That makes no sense because it sure doesn’t feel like that.” And for you, that would be right. But if you’re looking at just the mechanics of recoil force, the numbers in the chart tell a compelling story.

One last thing. If someone says their non-.22 gun feels like shooting a .22, the numbers show that no centerfire round has the same low recoil force as the .22 LR in an identical gun. If it’s a heavier gun, then, sure. But for the guns and calibers I’ve seen where this is claimed, they’re usually not even close to reality.
What about the statement, “It has no recoil?” Well, this is usually stated under very obviously false circumstances. Part of the hilarity of people saying a gun has no recoil is when they’re shooting a recoil-operated gun and the gun is functioning as it should—that is, the recoil makes the gun operate. It’s hard to support your claim when the thing you’re saying doesn’t exist obviously does. And it’s also obvious that their hand/arm/shoulder moves with every shot fired. It’s hard to translate that into “no recoil.”
Nitpicking? Pet peeve? Or using this as an excuse to write an informative article? I plead the fifth. Curious about your gun and ammo? You can run the numbers yourself if you go online. Visit 
kwk.us/recoil.html
