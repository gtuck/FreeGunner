.300 Rook vs .22LR: In Defense of a Rook and Rabbit Rifle

Beautiful and Practical vs. Mundane

 Rook rifles like the author’s Joseph Harkom rifle, built to a Holland & Holland patent and chambered in .300 Rook, have a rich history. (Terry Wieland photo)

By Terry Wieland

A few years ago, I wrote about developing loads for an old British rook rifle built by Joseph Harkom of Edinburgh—or at least it has Harkom’s name on it. Most likely, it was built by W&C Scott & Son of Birmingham, to a Holland & Holland patent, and the finishing touches were applied when it reached the Harkom shop.
I mention all this by way of establishing the rifle’s pedigree. In the British rifle world of the 1890s, names don’t get any better than that.
The Harkom is chambered for the .300 Rook, a rimmed cartridge about the same size as the later .30 Carbine. It’s not particularly powerful, but then it didn’t need to be. Its purpose in life was taking rooks (an edible blackbird) and rabbits for the pot, at 50 to 75 yards. Because rooks were shot off branches, no one wanted the bullet to travel far and do any unscheduled damage.
Essentially, this is a common or garden small-game rifle, comparable to America’s .32-20 of similar vintage. The British had half a dozen such cartridges, usually proprietary to the riflemaker. And like their American counterparts, all were killed off, or at least crippled, by the tidal wave of .22 LR guns that began in the 1890s and has not stopped to this day.

Better Than .22LR
I’m a great admirer of the .22 rimfire in its myriad forms and would be among the last to deny its usefulness. But I am also an admirer of rifles like the Harkom and cartridges like the .300 Rook, which will do a few things the .22 LR will not.
For one, 
it allows me to handload rounds tailored to a specific use
, such as dispatching, solidly and firmly, armadillos in the pea patch or raccoons in the stable. I can load them with soft lead bullets that will expand, kill, and not ricochet, or I can use some full metal jacketed bullets in the event I don’t want to damage the hide.
Obviously, a 90-grain lead bullet at 1,250 fps is going to outperform a 40-grain bullet at 1,050, although some .22 advocates insist that going to hollowpoints at higher velocities—say, 1,250 fps—will do it all, just as well. Possibly, but I’m not convinced.

There is also the question of the rifles themselves. Rook rifles (or rook and rabbit, as the Brits call them) are usually delightful pieces of work. Made as they were at the tail end of the Victorian era, they display the gunmaking ethos of that time. Victorians could, and did, turn the most prosaic of instruments into works of near-art—or at least finely made artifacts that displayed many artistic touches.
They did it with hairbrushes, shaving brushes, hand mirrors, ashtrays, and fountain pens. Rifles and shotguns, no matter how modest or inexpensive, were treated the same way.
Certainly, there are a few .22 rimfire rifles around that display the same characteristics, but not as many, and the commercial world being what it is, manufacturers were soon trying to pare every penny from the price of a .22. They quickly became mundane tools of everyday toil—kids’ guns or rough instruments to keep behind the henhouse door, rather than a possession to take pride in both owning and using.
The British shotgun writer Gough Thomas wrote about a quality in a mechanism he called “eumatic.” Related to ergonomic, eumatic went further and defined not just ease and convenience of use, but sensual pleasure in using anything from a pipe wrench to a shotgun. It’s a quality conspicuously lacking in most modern mass-produced firearms. Where you will find it, however, is in a custom (or customized) gun that has been worked on by a guy who is a shooter as well as a gunsmith.
It’s the break-action gun that opens promptly and effortlessly and closes the same way; the magazine in the semiautomatic that pops out into your waiting hand as if it’s alive; the cylinder on a revolver that wants to spin and keep on spinning. It’s the safety that doesn’t resist your thumb, but also doesn’t want to pop on and off on its own.

Show me a modern .22 that behaves like that.
On the other hand, you can find Winchester Low Walls, Stevens 44½s, and even Ballards that were made by skilled hands and escaped the barbaric 1920s intact, chambered for such delightful little rounds as the .32-20 and .25-20. Even the .32-40—probably the most commonly found—can be handloaded down to armadillo level or up, almost, to deer level. Not only are they a pleasure to use, but they afford a lot of leeway when it comes to making your own ammunition.
What’s more, they are neither terribly noisy nor inflict violent recoil—two qualities that grow on you as you get older.
