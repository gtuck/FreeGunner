The Famous Marlin Model 1893: Tested and Reviewed

Part 2 of our deep dive into centenarian hunting rifles.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

April 20, 2026

By Dave Emary

With the advent of smokeless propellant in the late 1880s, it was necessary to design stronger steels to handle the higher pressures that were produced by smokeless propellants. Winchester and Marlin both had several successful models of rifles in the Models 1881 and 1889 Marlin and the 1873, 1876, 1886 and 1892 Winchester. All were well-­designed, strong actions for blackpowder but the low-strength steels used in these rifles were not strong enough to handle cartridges designed around smokeless propellant that could produce two to three times the pressure of blackpowder. All the rifle manufacturers began work on stronger steels shortly after the introduction of smokeless propellant. At the time, metallurgists pursued alloying steel with nickel to achieve a higher strength. Marlin perfected its nickel steel a little sooner than Winchester and capitalized on this with the introduction of the Model 1893. The Winchester’s Model 1894 had not been announced yet, but Marlin likely knew it was coming.
Gunsmith Lewis Lobdell Hepburn invented a single-shot rifle, and was the lead designer of the Marlin Models 1888, 1889, 1891, 1893, 1894, 1895 and 1897. Guns & Ammo’s sample 1893 Marlin was a special-order equipped with a carbine-style buttplate, half magazine tube and factory sling swivels. Traces of its original case-color hardening are still visible under the lever. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Some might ask why this entry is not about the 1894 Winchester. Well, I felt that there was not much I could add to the ink that has been spilled on the Winchester ’94. It is the most successful commercial firearm in history with more than 7 million produced. For this entry in “Of Wood & Steel,” I thought it would be interesting to review a major competitor to Winchester. It also helped that I had a nice custom-­order 1893 Marlin in .30-­30.
The Marlin Model 1893 featured a solid-top receiver and square bolt that increased strength and safety for smokeless-powder ammunition. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 1893 Marlin
L. L. Hepburn had designed the successful 1889 Marlin. It was a classic lever-­action design that featured several improvements to strengthen the action, making it safer. It introduced Marlin’s closed-top receiver and side ejection, too, and featured a carrier that blocked the magazine tube when the lever was raised to eliminate any chance of a cartridge slipping out of the magazine tube and locking up the action. The 1889 was initially chambered in .32-­20 Winchester, .38-­40 Winchester, and .44-­40 Winchester.
In 1903, Marlin began drilling the receiver topstrap to accept a special Hepburn receiver sight. The top of the receiver is marked “MARLIN SAFETY”. Its .30-30 chambering is marked on the barrel. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Marlin needed a longer action than the 1889 to handle the .38-­55 Winchester class of cartridges in an updated lever-­action rifle. So, Hepburn stretched the action and made further refinements to the 1889 rifle. Marlin also used its new “Special Smokeless Steel” in the receiver and barrel. Hepburn received the patent for the Marlin Model 1893 on August 1, 1893.

Marlin offered the Model 1893 with three buttplates, including a curved crescent steel plate (above), a heavier stamped-steel carbine buttplate, and a flat hard-rubber buttplate. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The ’93 featured a rear locking bolt with a vertical locking lug actuated by the lever. Inside the closed-­top receiver, a square bolt covered the right side of the rear of the barrel, side ejection and a two-­piece firing pin. The firing pin has a unique separate rear piece that pivots into line with the front piece when the lever is completely closed, and the locking lug is raised into its full upward position. The design ensures the gun will not fire without the action completely locked. Marlin stated that side ejection was a safety and convenience feature for the shooter, as cartridges would not be ejected into the shooter’s face. After shooting an 1881 Marlin, I would agree with that claim. The very front of the bolt covers the very rear of the right side of the barrel. The bolt has two notches at the very front of the right side of the bolt that channels any gas from a blown primer or failed case away from the shooter. Marlin advertised that its 1893 barrels were the same barrels used on its prized Ballard target rifles. Hence, Marlin claimed the ’93 was the most accurate lever-­action rifle available. Based on Guns & Ammo’s test of the rifle in .30-­30 — and experience with my own 1893 in .32-­40 — I concur.
Lyman and Marbles tang sights were made for the Marlin Model 1893. G&A’s rifle was equipped with a period Lyman No. 1 tang sight without the stem-locking device of the No. 1A. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
When the Model 1893 was introduced, it was offered in .32-­40 and .38-­55. The .25-­36 Marlin and .30-­30 Winchester were added in the 1896 catalog. It’s interesting to note that the .25-­36 was made with a 1-in-10-­inch twist barrel as opposed to the 1-in-8-­inch twist of the .25-­35 Winchester. The heaviest bullet the .25-­36 could stabilize was a 100-­grain bullet. This probably explains why it was never very popular, as it was pretty marginal with the 100-­grain bullet as a deer cartridge. The .32 Special was added in 1902.
The 1893 bolt completely covers the ejection port when closed, but two small notches allow gas in the action to escape. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Marlin records are not complete prior to the 1930s. It is not possible to determine how many 1893s — or the virtually identical Marlin Model 93 and the 36 — were made. It is also not possible to exactly date many 1893 Marlin rifles due to incomplete records. It is possible to narrow it down based on the font on the markings on the barrel and receiver tang. Based on these markings, the best that can be said about the G&A’s test rifle is that it was made before ­1905. The only records we are aware of for the 1893 are that 69,100 rifles were made between 1893 and 1906. These records are also incomplete.

For perspective, about 330,000 1894 Winchesters were made in the same timeframe. It appears that Marlin sold one 1893 to every three to four Winchester Model 1894s. It’s fair to say that the 1893 Marlin line offered significant competition to the 1894, but it was a distant second. The 1893 lives on today in the Marlin 336 thanks to Ruger acquiring the Marlin brand in 2020 and restarting production in 2023.
According to Lt. Col. William Brophy’s book, “Marlin Firearms: A History of the Guns and the Company That Made Them” (1989), the 1893 was manufactured in rifle, carbine, musket and special lightweight configurations. Straight grips and pistol grips were available, as well as round, octagon and half-­octagon/half-round barrels. Takedown models were also available. The standard rifle had a 26-­inch barrel and full-­length magazine. Barrel lengths of 20, 24, 26, 28, 30 and 32 inches were produced. The standard carbine was available with either a 15-­ or 20-­inch barrel, straight grip stock, carbine buttplate, carbine sights and a saddle ring. For a special order, the carbine could be supplied without the saddle ring. Special lightweight models were made with an 18-­ or 20-­inch barrel, short forearm and short magazine. A semi-­deluxe model was available with a pistol grip stock and basic checkering on the pistol grip and forend. Special-­order rifles could be engraved by none other than L. D. Nimschke along with gold plating. The weight of a standard 26-­inch-barrel rifle with an octagon barrel was 7¾ pounds. A round barrel was 8¼ pounds. Carbines weighed between 65/8 to 6¾ pounds.
The loading port follows the Charles M. King’s May 1866 design patent. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Curiously, in 1905 Marlin offered rifles chambered in .32-­40 and .38-­55 with a Grade B barrel made of softer steel and marked “For Black Powder”. Standard Marlin 1893 barrels were marked “Special Smokeless Steel”.
Starting in 1903, Marlin manufactured a special Hepburn receiver sight that mounted to the top of the receiver. It was a sight that had wings on the side and a pivoting sight arm that elevated in notches. The sights were caliber specific. The top-rear of 1893 Marlin receivers made in 1903 and later were factory drilled and tapped for that sight. When you see an 1893 Marlin with two holes drilled on the top-rear of the receiver, it is original.
The Marlin Model 1893 spur hammer features a full-cock fire position (above) and a half-cock safety position (not shown). The hammer design was largely unchanged since 1881. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The Test Rifle
The 1893 Marlin used for the “Of Wood & Steel” series is a curious gun. It was a special-­order rifle with a 21 1/2-­inch round barrel, half magazine tube, carbine buttplate and factory installed sling swivels. The bore is bright and shiny. The barrel retains nearly 100 percent of its blue, and the receiver retains 10 to 15 percent of the original case color. The action is tight and smooth, too. It was fitted with a period Lyman tang sight, and the rifle has a crisp 5-­pound, 12-­ounce, trigger pull, and it weighs 7 pounds, 11 ounces.

Several types of front sights were available on the Marlin 1893. G&A’s test rifle has a Lyman bead front sight atop a serrated post, and a slot filler in the traditional rear sight slot on the barrel. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Period .30-­30 ammunition was not what it is today. The propellants available then were more limited. The 1922 Winchester catalog listed the .30-­30 with only a 170-­grain bullet rated at a specific “2,003.4” feet per second (fps). The 1929 Winchester catalog rated the same load at 2,200 fps. Apparently, a better propellant was introduced in that 7-­year period. The 1923 Remington catalog lists only a 170-­grain bullet at 2,020 fps. The Winchester catalog shows a round-nose flat-point projectile very much like the profile of the 
Hornady
 170-­grain flat point bullet. The Remington catalog shows a sleeker bullet that looks more like the profile of a Speer flat-point bullet. The reason for this is the Remington Model 14 pump-­action rifle did not function with the blunter-profile bullet such as the Hornady or Winchester bullet. Incidentally, the Remington catalog lists a 1,000-­round case of .30-­30 for $72.25. That would put a 20-­round box at approximately $1.50. That fact must be weighed against the average mid-­1920s yearly income of $1,368.
Designed in 1895, the .30-30 Winchester cartridge became the most common cartridge for the Model 1893 and 93 lever-action. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
In the 1920s, the most appropriate speed of propellant available was probably Hercules #308, which was equivalent to current IMR 4895. To try and match the factory loads of that period, I used Hornady’s 170-­grain round-nose flat-point bullet and consulted the Hodgdon Reloading Manual for a load of 28.7 grains of IMR 4895 that was listed at approximately 2,000 fps from a 24-­inch barrel.
Table 1 shows test results at 100 yards. Editor Eric Poole did all of the shooting for record. For this series, we fired three five-­shot groups at 100 yards and one five-­shot group at 200 yards. We had limited out-of-production ammunition for three of the rifles in this series, hence the number of groups with vintage ammo. I would have expected a velocity about 100 fps faster from the 211/2-­inch barrel, but the throat is probably a little long on this rifle.
The Model 1889 was the first with a solid top and side ejection. With the 1893, Marlin discontinued all top-ejecting rifles. A passive safety, the firing pin was two pieces and joined when the action was closed. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
We also wanted to explore what kind of ranges such guns with period sights would have been effective at. The mid-­1920s .30-­30 Winchester with a 170-­grain bullet produced effective ballistics to 150 to 175 yards. With iron sights, hunting ranges would have been limited by the eyesight of the shooter or the accuracy of the rifle and ammunition. We shot at a steel target with a 10-­inch circle to represent the kill zone of a deer. The 1893 Marlin was capable of accuracy far beyond the effective ballistic range of the .30-­30 cartridge. It easily held the 10-­inch kill zone on the 200-­yard target. We were very impressed with the accuracy of this little carbine. It shot right with the Winchester Model 54. In summary, the 1893 had a smooth action, a decent trigger, was light, balanced, and very accurate.
