The World's Most Powerful Handgun? Craig Boddington Relives the Model 29 Legend

From Hollywood fame to the hunting fields, an outdoor icon delivers the ultimate verdict on Smith & Wesson's defining .44 Magnum.

 There are bigger and more powerful handguns, but this is about all the fun the author needs to have. This is his M29 Classic Hunter with 6 ½-inch barrel, firing a fast Garrett 310-grain hard-cast load. (Photo courtesy of Craig Boddington)

July 23, 2026

By Craig Boddington

“The most powerful handgun in the world,” quoth Clint Eastwood, as Inspector Harry Callahan in 1971’s blockbuster 
Dirty Harry
. At the time, it was still true, at least among production handguns. The Smith & Wesson Model 29, Smith’s first big “N-frame” revolver, was introduced in 1956, along with the .44 Remington Magnum, usually shortened to “.44 Mag.”
Their tenure as the most powerful handgun was short. It was superseded by Dick Casull’s .454 Casull two years later. However, for years the Casull was a custom wildcat, not standardized by Sporting Arms and Ammunition Institute (SAAMI) until 1997. Since then, .44 Mag performance has been surpassed by .480 Ruger and .500 S&W Magnum (both in 2003), and by the big, super-fast .460 S&W Magnum in 2005. And there are plenty of other big wildcats and proprietaries for ham-handed pistoleros.
Today, most Smith & Wesson’s current M29 .44 Mag variants are stainless steel with “M629” designation. This is a current 629 with full underbarrel lug, and synthetic grip. (Photo courtesy of Smith & Wesson)
I’ve shot most of them, hunted with some. Don’t own any of them. Instead, I’ve had one or another S&W M29 for more than 40 years. Still one of my all-time favorite handguns. Fast, accurate, great single-action trigger, double-action smooth. Depending on barrel length, not too big to carry. With lighter loads, easy and fun to shoot. With heavy loads, not so easy, not so fun. Still manageable and accurate. With heavy loads it will do anything I might possibly ask a handgun to do, and a lot of stuff I’m not going to ask. Heavy loads in the .44 Magnum are about where my fun meter pegs out. I shoot my Model 29 well, and I enjoy shooting it. Everybody has different recoil (and pain) thresholds. For me, it’s a sane and sensible limit. More power than a Model 29 in .44 Mag offers, I neither need nor want in a handgun.
Ammo and Handloads
In the 1950s, gun writer Elmer Keith was in his prime. A staunch big revolver guy, he did a lot of 
handloading
 experimentation, trying to wring more power out of existing cartridges. He settled on the .44 Special, introduced by Smith & Wesson in 1907. At first blush, it might appear that the .44-40 and/or .45 Colt, with longer cases, had greater potential. Elmer Keith wasn’t the most literate of gun writers (as his last editor, I can attest to that!), but he knew his stuff. He settled on the .44 Special because both the .44-40 and .45 Colt were blackpowder cartridges dating back to 1873. Thin-walled brass, for blackpowder pressures. Designed for smokeless powder, the .44 Special had thicker brass, able to contain higher pressure.

Keith worked with Remington to develop the cartridge—with even stronger cases—and with S&W to produce a large-framed revolver to house it. External dimensions of the .44 Special were retained, the 1.16-inch case lengthened to 1.285 inches. Not to increase power—Keith’s handloads were already there—but to ensure a .44 Magnum cartridge could not be chambered in a .44 Special pistol. .44 Special ammo is loaded to a mild 15,500 psi; .44 Magnum is specified by SAAMI to maximum 36,000 psi.
One of the .44 Magnum’s charms is versatility from the variety of loads. Left to right, light-kicking .44 Special and lighter target loads with wadcutters; full up hunting loads like 
Hornady
’s 200-grain Monoflex and Remington 240-grain softpoint; all the way to maximum loads like Garrett’s with 310-grain hard-cast bullet at over 1300 fps. (Photo courtesy of Craig Boddington)
Versatility is part of the magic of the .44 Mag
. As .38 Special can be used in .357 Magnum pistols, .44 Special can be fired in .44 Magnum chambers. .44 Special ammo is hard to find but .44 Mag ammo has long been available in ascending grades. Common are milder 240-grain loads at 1180 fps, while the most common full-power load uses a 240-grain bullet rated at 1300 fps.
There’s a big difference in recoil between these loads. That ain’t all, folks. Specialty manufacturers, including Buffalo Bore, Cor-Bon, and Garrett Cartridges, have long made good business out of extra-heavy .44 Mag loads. Not all are suitable for all revolvers, and some are too long to cycle in repeating .44 Mag rifles. When I carry my M29 in bear country, the cylinder is loaded with Garrett’s 310-grain super-hard-cast Hammerhead bullet. At 1325 fps from a 7.5-inch barrel, it produces 1200 ft-lbs of energy and approaches the maximum one can safely cram into standard revolvers. Garrett’s description states: “Recommended for use in all 
steel
 .44 Mag revolvers except Taurus Tracker. This ammo is too long for rifles.” I can’t speak for efficacy on a big, bad bear, have no interest in learning, but it knocks the stuffing out of feral hogs.

Production
In 1956, about 3100 M29s were produced, price about $140 ($1700 in 2026 currency). Not everyone needs that big a revolver, but the M29 has been a big success. Currently, there are 15 M29 N-Frame options in .44 Mag, and the M29 is also chambered in 10mm Auto. In both chamberings, the majority are 629s, the “6” denoting stainless steel. Current prices start at $1139 and absent engraving, go up to almost $2000 to the Performance Center 629 Stealth Hunter. If you accept the “adjusted for inflation” figure, most are well below that $1700 figure.
Over the years, the 29 was factory-offered in seven barrel lengths: 4, 4 ½, 5, 6, 6 ½, 8 3/8, and 10 ½-inches, with others available on request. Elmer Keith always carried a four-inch M29, though he hunted with longer barrels. Inspector Callahan’s famous M29 had a 6 ½-inch barrel. My first M29 had an 8 3/8-inch barrel, while my current has a 6 ½-inch barrel.
Interesting factoid: When 
Dirty Harry 
was filmed, M29 was out of production. Sales had tanked because other .44 Mag revolvers were less expensive. 
Dirty Harry
 and its sequels changed that. Demand skyrocketed, production resumed, and M29s sold for ridiculous scalper prices. I was in college when 
Dirty Harry 
was released. I wanted one, couldn’t afford one, waited a full decade.
An exceptionally ugly feral hog taken with the M29 Classic Hunter. With the right loads, the .44 Mag is capable and proven up to bad bears and big bovines. (Photo courtesy of Craig Boddington)
Hunting with the Model 29
My first M29 was blued steel with original rosewood grips, came in a wooden presentation box (standard in that era). Its 8 3/8-inch barrel was ungainly for holster carry, but it gave me extra velocity. Which I imagined made a difference. For hunting, I liked the longer sighting radius. Of course, back then I could see better, and was steadier. I shot that revolver a lot and shot it well.
Once, I was hunting javelina in Texas, couldn’t find any. On the last morning, I spotted a small group feeding on a prickly pear flat. I stalked as close as I could, ran out of cover at something over 100 yards. A bit far, but I knew the gun. I held on the backline with a two-hand hold and flattened one. Not long after that, I lost the M29 (and most everything else) in a bad burglary. Gun-savvy burglars, they took the empty presentation case as well as the revolver.

Over time I bought more guns, but the loss affected me so deeply that I replaced almost none of them in kind. With one exception: I bought another M29 as soon as I could. The one I have now, long an old friend, is a 6 ½-inch M29 Classic Hunter with full underbarrel lug and Jim Hogue’s rubber grips.
Since hunting, not carry, is my primary reason for owning a .44 Mag, I probably would have gotten an 8 3/8-inch barrel if I could have found one. Glad I didn’t, because I like this one better. In fact, I like everything about it. The 6 ½-inch barrel is immeasurably handier for holster carry, and I like the added weight of the full lug. At 49.3 ounces, it’s a big revolver, but the weight allows it to hold steady. The single-action trigger is light and crisp, double-action clean. Grips are sort of a personal thing, and especially important on a big handgun. With medium-size hands, the 
Hogue grip
s on this one fit me perfectly.
Friend Kyler Hamman and I were stalking a group of pigs feeding on the edge of a barley field. A deep, sandy streambed gave us cover; all we needed to do was come up even with them and crawl up the bank, should be within 40 yards. Almost there when the wind changed.
Instead of running away, the pigs took the angle and started squirting across the streambed right in front of us. Close but moving fast. I got the 29 up. When a bigger hog appeared I swung with it, managed to get the shot off. Kyler was certain I’d missed. I wouldn’t have made any bets, glad I didn’t have to repeat it, but the shot felt good. We climbed up the opposite bank and there was the hog, down and center-punched behind the shoulder.
Final Thoughts
Well, that was a while ago. Last year, my eyes got so bad I was pretty much done with all iron sights. I got cataract surgery and lens replacement. Best money I ever spent, back in business. I hadn’t shot my 29 for a while. Thought I’d better reacquaint myself. Good groups with Hornady’s 200-grain California-legal unleaded Monoflex bullet, clocked at 1400 fps. Not as good with the hard-kicking 310-grain hard-cast bullet. Better than I expected. I need to take the Model 29 hog hunting again.
From a solid rest, the M29 is capable of exceptional accuracy. Left, his final 50-yard group with Hornady’s Handgun Hunter 200-grain Monoflex was excellent. Right, a three-shot group with hard-kicking Garrett 310-grain hard-cast bullets not so good. Both are plenty adequate for short-range hunting. (Photo courtesy of Craig Boddington)

Craig Boddington

 Craig Boddington is a retired US Marine Colonel and career outdoor journalist. He is the author of 31 books and more than 5000 articles on hunting, shooting, and conservation, with hundreds of appearances in films, outdoor television, and speaking engagements. Boddington's hunting experience spans six continents and 60 countries; his honors include the Weatherby Hunting and Conservation Award and Conklin Award. He and his wife Donna have three children and five grandchildren and divide their time between the California Central Coast and a small farm in his native Kansas that has lots of whitetails and never enough turkeys. He is most easily reached at www.craigboddington.com.
