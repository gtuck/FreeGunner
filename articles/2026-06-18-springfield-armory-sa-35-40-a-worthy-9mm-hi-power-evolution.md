## Article Text (extracted)

Springfield Armory SA-35 4.0: A Worthy 9mm Hi Power Evolution

The new Springfield Armory SA-35 4.0-inch pistol updates the iconic Browning Hi Power style for modern shooters.

 The new SA-35 4.0’s (bottom)main feature is the shorter 4.0-inch barrel. It has all the features of the full-size SA-35 (top). (Michael Anschuetz photo)

June 18, 2026

By Joel J. Hutchcroft

Springfield Armory doesn’t call their SA-35 pistol a “Hi Power” the way the original John M. Browning design came to be known, but there’s no denying that Springfield’s pistol is in fact a Hi Power. And there’s absolutely no shame in that at all. The Hi Power is a revered pistol, and even though it was originally introduced just shy of a century ago (1935), it has the fit, feel, and function that handgunners today can appreciate.
New for this year, Springfield has brought out a shorter version of the SA-35, and it might just be the best Hi Power yet. I own one of Springfield’s first SA-35s made in 2022, with the 4.7-inch barrel, and I like it a lot, but I have to admit the first time I picked up the new 4.0-inch-barreled SA-35, I liked the way it balanced in my hand even better.
The pistol’s trigger is smooth, and our sample’s pull averaged 4 pounds, 14 ounces, with 6 ounces of variation over 10 measurements. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Features
The SA-35 has all the significant features that the original Hi Power had which made it so different from John Browning’s other revered pistol design—the Model 1911—including no grip safety, no separate barrel bushing, a fixed cam on the barrel instead of a swinging barrel link, and the feedramp as an integral part of the barrel itself. Of course, the SA-35 has the wide-body, higher-capacity, double-stack magazine that really set the Hi Power apart from the classic single-stack Model 1911.
Springfield’s SA-35 has a cold-hammer-forged, carbon-steel barrel with an improved feedramp. And as I mentioned earlier this new variant has a 4.0-inch barrel instead of the 4.7-inch barrel of Springfield’s initial SA-35. However, like that earlier SA-35, the new pistol has a Tactical Rack combat-style rear sight with a finely striated, all-black face and a U-shaped notch; a white-dot front sight; and checkered walnut grips.

Also, the SA-35 has a matte blued finish; an external extractor; a smooth trigger; and a knurled, rowel-style hammerspur. The pistol’s forged frame is marked “SI Geneseo IL USA” on the right side just above the serial number. The slide is marked “Springfield Armory Model SA-35 Geneseo, IL” on the left side. The slide stop and a manual thumb safety are positioned on the left side. The thumb pads for both controls are striated, and the magazine release button, which is located in the traditional Model 1911 position, is lightly checkered.
The fit and finish of this sample SA-35 4.0-inch pistol are excellent. The trigger pull averaged 4 pounds, 14 ounces, according to 10 measurements with my RCBS trigger pull scale. There was some take-up, but when it broke, it did so crisply and consistently. There were 6 ounces of variance over those 10 measurements.
The pistol’s white-dot front sight is dovetailed into the slide, and it is 0.13 inch thick and 0.18 inch tall. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
The front sight and the rear sight are dovetailed into the slide, and the slide has grasping grooves at the rear only. There are 21 of them on each side. Of course, the slide has the distinctive Hi Power-style flute at the muzzle end, but on this new pistol it is noticeably shorter to go along with the shorter barrel. The slide’s top is smooth and rounded. The front sight is 0.13 inch thick and 0.18 inch tall. The rear sight’s U-shaped notch is 0.13 inch wide at the top. The sight radius is 5.63 inches.

The walnut grip panels have fine checkering. They are held in place by one screw each. The grip frame’s backstrap and frontstrap are smooth. As I wrote about Springfield’s full-size SA-35 when it first came out, the pistol’s grip is just about the most comfortable shape I have ever experienced—and that’s saying a lot because I’m a real fan of the Model 1911 grip shape and angle. The 4.0-inch SA-35 has the same comfortable grip as its longer-barreled littermate. The SA-35’s trigger guard is rounded and smooth, and it’s wide enough to accommodate my medium-sized trigger finger when I’m wearing gloves.
The new 4.0-inch-barreled SA-35 is 7.10 inches long, 1.30 inches thick, and 4.80 inches tall. It weighs 29.8 ounces, unloaded. The blued steel magazine has Springfield Armory’s crossed cannons and flame company logo on the left side and three witness holes on the right side, and it holds 15 rounds of 9mm ammunition. One magazine comes with the pistol.
John M. Browning’s Hi-Power design did away with the Model 1911-style grip safety, made the barrel bushing an integral part of the slide, replaced the swinging barrel link with a fixed cam, and made the feedramp an integral part of the barrel itself. Springfield’s SA-35 carries on that tradition. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
To help make inserting the magazine faster and smoother, the magazine well has been slightly beveled. Also worth noting is the pistol does not have a magazine disconnect safety, meaning it will fire without the magazine inserted.
Performance
As per my usual shooting protocol, I fired the new 4.0-inch-barreled SA-35 for accuracy from a sandbag benchrest at a distance of 25 yards. I managed to round up eight factory loads with bullet weights ranging from 115 grains to 147 grains and bullet styles ranging from solid FMJs through synthetic-coated TSJFNs and polymer-tipped FTXs to a number of different JHPs. All loads functioned reliably, and I didn’t experience a single malfunction. All bullet styles fed, fired, extracted, and ejected. In most cases, the empties were flung several feet to the right.
I won’t go into all the details, but as you can see from the accompanying chart, the 4.0-inch-barreled SA-35 averaged between 2.00 and 3.50 inches with the factory loads at 25 yards. Three, five-shot groups with each load were fired and averaged. Overall average accuracy was 2.78 inches. That’s certainly more than acceptable for self-defense and, in fact, is quite good compared to a lot of other 9mm personal-protection pistols that I’ve fired recently. Remember, the old standard for duty and personal-protection pistols is around 4.25 inches at 25 yards.

The top prize for accuracy went to the Nosler Match Grade 124-grain JHP loading. It averaged 2.00 inches. The load producing the highest velocity was the 
Hornady
 Critical Defense 115-grain FTX, which averaged a velocity of 1,135 fps for five rounds measured 12 feet from the muzzle with a Competition Electronics ProChrono LTD chronograph.
With shooting for accuracy completed, I put the 4.0-inch-barreled SA-35 through some action-shooting drills—the same ones I used for the full-size SA-35 four years ago. At seven yards I fired a modified El Presidente Drill, during which I performed a double tap on three targets, repeated the drill, and then fired a single round on each target (for a total of 15 rounds) as quickly as I could acquire the sights.
At five yards I fired an FBI Drill in which I fired two body shots and then one headshot on two silhouette targets. I repeated shooting like that until I emptied the fully loaded magazine.
At three yards I fired the pistol one-handed on three targets, three rounds at a time until I emptied the magazine.
And just for the heck of it, in a separate drill, I fired a fully loaded magazine with the pistol right side up, left side up, and upside down.
During all the shooting, including from the bench for accuracy and all the action drills, the 4.0-inch-barreled SA-35 functioned flawlessly. To me, shooting the new 4.0-inch SA-35 was every bit as much fun as shooting the 
full-size SA-35
 that I ended up buying after writing it up for 
Shooting Times
 four years ago. Nobody should be surprised if I add this new shorter SA-35 to my battery too.
