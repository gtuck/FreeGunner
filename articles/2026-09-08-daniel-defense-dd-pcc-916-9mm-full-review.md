Daniel Defense DD PCC 916 9mm: Full review

Daniel Defense has taken on the pistol-caliber carbine world in grand fashion. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Andy Grossman)

September 08, 2026

By Frank Melloni

Nearly every major manufacturer has rolled out an offering to sling 9mm Luger from an AR-style platform. Of them all, 
Georgia-based Daniel Defense
 was noticeably absent — until the company’s purchase and 
reintroduction of the H9 pistol
 seemed to explain why. 
What we didn’t realize at the time was that DD was concurrently working on a second project. As the company had much of the tooling to build a long gun chambered in 9mm, it was evident that 2024 would be the time to strike. Released nearly the same time as the H9, 
the DD PCC
 initially took a backseat in production, but we’re happy to report that availability is now more widespread, and I was able to procure a sample for testing and evaluation.
You might be wondering why I didn’t call out the new firearm by name in the introduction. Believe it or not, I did. Ever meet someone who named their dog simply, “Dog?” Well, that’s sort of what we have here. Daniel Defense’s pistol-caliber carbine’s official designation is the “DD PCC” — likely because they feel it gets straight to the point. However, after looking it over, I feel the name doesn’t tell enough of the story to do it justice. 
Straying from the path of typical PCCs, Daniel Defense used CZ Scorpion EVO 3 mags with double-stack and double-feed capabilities. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
Design and Features
For starters, the receiver set is shorter than that of a conventional AR-15. As there’s no reason to accommodate rifle cartridges and magazines, building it to that footprint creates unnecessary bulk and weight. Although this breaks the mold, it’s still compatible with aftermarket triggers, grips, forends, and buttstocks. Much of that list is certainly a target for customization, but I can’t see swapping the forend, as it contains DD’s signature DNA. Bolted to the receiver, it’s going to be the most rigid design out there, floating the barrel better than conventional tubes that merely lock onto a barrel nut. It also features seven sides of M-LOK and a full-length rail, so most shouldn’t have trouble adding the accessories they need for their particular use.

Avoiding the argument of Glock vs. Colt, Daniel Defense took an alternate route and built its PCC to take CZ Scorpion EVO 3 mags. The benefits in the PCC realm are staggering, and once you adopt them, you won’t look back. Being double-stack and double-feed, they are remarkably faster to fill than Glock magazines. As for Colt mags, they retain ammunition far better if they hit the ground, and their intricate nature locks into the magwell more positively. The gun ships with one Magpul 35-rounder, but spares can be sourced online for less than $20 at most retailers. The gun can also take the 50-round drum if you want to keep the action going longer.
Daniel Defense’s own AR furniture comes standard on the PCC. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
Controls and Ergonomics
The lock-up of the Scorpion mag made it easier for DD to design ambidextrous controls, and as a lefty, I appreciate how they were identical on each side. If you aren’t part of the chosen 17 percent, you’ll still reap the benefit of dropping the bolt with your trigger finger, giving your support hand one less step to get you back into the fight. As implied, the bolt locks open after firing the last round, another feature made easier when a gun is designed to use Evo 3 mags. 
The DD PCC is a good mix of fancy and simplistic, which shows itself when we look at the action. Direct blowback isn’t sexy in the slightest, but it works well and isn’t picky with ammunition. The one drawback has always been its recoil pulse, but that is tamed using a hydraulic buffer. This advanced device swaps a rubber bumper with a fluid regulated plunger that soaks up the blow of the reciprocating bolt carrier group. It does this in a gradual manner, which takes the thump off the shooter’s shoulder without redistributing it through the gun in the form of shock. It’s paired with what Daniel Defense bills as an A2 flash hider, but the physics of closing the 6 o’clock slot gives it some sort of compensation properties, so not only does it save your night vision, but it will also direct the muzzle back down to some degree under recoil.

Range Setup and Testing
Of all the features the DD PCC is froth with, sights are not one of them. So, to get it ready for the range, I mounted one of Trijicon’s latest Miniature Reflex Optics, the MRO SD. This single-dot variant offers a clean 2-MOA reticle framed inside a nearly indestructible aluminum housing. The use of high-definition glass and a shortened tube makes shooting with both eyes open more natural and more obtainable for newcomers. I don’t typically put magnified optics on PCCs, but decided to pair the MRO with Trijicon’s 3X magnifier to observe the capability of the firearm.
Chances are, if ammo runs well in a handgun, it’ll work well in a carbine, too. There are a few exceptions to the rule, but generally speaking, compatibility is shared. Remington’s Range Clean is at the forefront of this idea and is built with a bullet profile created to function in all action types. This projectile also shields its lead base to keep airborne toxins to a minimum. Ball ammo is fun, but as this gun is likely going to find its way into the hands of law enforcement and homeowners, I made sure to include some self-defense options as well. Federal’s Hydra-Shok has served both camps for many years, so it easily made the list, as it’s hard to argue with its proven center-post hollowpoint design. As the carbine manages recoil better than a handgun, I also tested DoubleTap’s hard-hitting +P bonded defense load. The easiest cure to solve bullet/jacket separation is to chemically make them one and the same. Striking them to a 147-grain profile ensures they will achieve maximum penetration, too. 
(Photo by Andy Grossman)
Accuracy and Handling
Initial testing was conducted from the bench to gauge accuracy and establish a zero. This was comical, as working around the magazine and the forward grip was a chore. Needless to say, this gun isn’t designed for this style of shooting, so if that is your ultimate goal, just know that you’ll need to invest in a shorter magazine and remove some furniture. Although awkward, this was the optimal position to reveal that the gun was one heck of a shooter. Not a single group strayed past the 2-inch mark, and overall, all three types of ammo seemed to be neck and neck in the consistency department. 
Stepping away from the crutch, I ran the gun harder, as I engaged steel with my feet planted and on the move. I found the gun to be extraordinarily agile, which is the result of its compact receiver coupled with lightweight construction. The gun didn’t miss a beat. Moreover, the recoil was barely noticeable even with the +P fodder. If I had one gripe, it’s that it comes with a mil-spec bang switch, but I understand why. Many of DD’s clients are used to that pull weight, and some departments even require it. There are plenty of good AR triggers out there, so if that’s a sticking point, just know it’s an easy and affordable fix. 
Final Thoughts
Combining Daniel Defense’s hit forend geometry with a vastly reengineered receiver made the DD PCC one of the best out there — and the Scorpion mags mark significant progress on this front. Overall, it’s a sharp-shooting carbine that is equally at home in the trunk of a cruiser as it is sitting in a competitor’s gear cart, and thanks to the affordable nature of 9mm Luger, it even makes a great range plinker for casual shooting. Daniel Defense may have been late to the PCC market, but like most delayed introductions, it was certainly worth the wait.
