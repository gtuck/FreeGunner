An FRT May be the Greatest MP5 Upgrade You Can Get

The quest to create the ultimate Heckler & Koch MP5K PDW stumbles towards perfection.

 With the Rare Breed FRT installed Tarr’s SP5K PDW ran at 900 RPM unsuppressed, and 950 suppressed. (Photo by Andy Grossman)

August 21, 2026

By James Tarr

Having built up, basically, a 
semi-auto HK MP5K PDW out of an SP5K PDW pistol
, I was almost perfectly happy. I would have been happier having a true select-fire gun, but financially that wasn’t feasible.
There are ways to increase rate of fire, and I’ve got a lot of experience with binary triggers. But I’ve tried binary triggers, and they left me wanting.
The Rare Breet FRT-RD3 is meant to be a drop-in unit that uses your standard lower and doesn’t require any parts to be added to your carrier.
Binary triggers, all things being equal, allow you to shoot twice as fast as you normally would. In “binary” position, the gun fires when you pull the trigger, and when you release it. The faster you can work the trigger, the faster your gun fires.
Years ago, I put a binary trigger in my first MP5, a Zenith MP5K clone. I’m not that fast on the trigger, but still I could get that gun going fast enough people at the range thought it was a (slow) full-auto. It takes a bit of practice and skill to get the gun running smoothly, and yet still the best use of a binary trigger for me seems to be the “double-tap”, just firing and releasing, for two quick shots. It’s a way to fire two quick shots basically as fast as you could normally fire one. It’s quick and controllable.

But…binary triggers seem very complicated, I never liked the selectors on that unit, if you are really fast on the trigger you can literally outrun it and cause a dead trigger, and it never really felt or sounded like a true select-fire gun. But I didn’t have any better options.
Then capitalism, and the disdain for government overreach that is part of the American DNA answered, and the answer was spelled F-R-T.
Forced reset triggers, or FRTs, use the action of the bolt/carrier to reset the trigger, physically push it forward, and you have to pull it again. But you can do this easily and amazingly fast. It’s quicker and easier than running a binary trigger. Technically they are semi-automatic in function, even if they often allow you to shoot at speeds that simulate full-auto.

Installation is almost toolless. You’ll need to remove this screw to pop up the transfer bar, which gives you room to install your ejector assembly. But that’s it.
FRT options for HK-pattern guns started popping up everywhere, but I’m picky. I didn’t want to replace my entire lower trigger housing. A lot of FRTs meant for HK-pattern guns use AR-style trigger groups in new lowers, and I didn’t want that. I wanted to keep a traditional trigger housing, and a traditional selector, not a push-button one like with the Hoffman Super Safety. And many of those brands require additional parts, or modifying your factory hammer. I didn’t want to do that either, so I was out of luck. Until 
Rare Breed Triggers
 introduced 
their FRT-RD3
.
In case you haven’t been following the story, Rare Breed came out with an FRT for AR-15s quite a few years ago. Mechanically, it was not a machine gun. The BATF basically told them that it was a machine gun, and to cease and desist. Rare Breed, in a display of testicular fortitude, told them no. What followed was years of legal action, harassment, debanking, intimidation, and much more by the government. Everyone assumed Rare Breed would lose, and they saw almost no support in the industry. But, finally, Rare Breed won… kind of. The government agreed to leave them alone, and to not go after any forced reset triggers, but mandated Rare Breed vigorously defend their patent. A lot of other companies jumped into the FRT business once Rare Breed cleared the way, and presumably the government did this to make Rare Breed police the industry.
Whenever you change anything that can affect function on a gun, test fire it. Tarr put 100 rounds through his K at the range when switching to the UMP-style stock, then cleaned it…and stuck the FRT in the lower.
And they have. Arguably with some of the worst optics possible, serving Cease and Desist orders on all sorts of companies making FRTs, almost getting thrown out of SHOT Show while confronting competitors, etc. The public perception on all this legal in-fighting is not good, and has earned Rare Breed some unsavory nicknames. All of these legal battles over patents, I know, will get settled eventually. I suspect many of them will end up not going anywhere, and Rare Breed knows this—but they will have done what they were required to do under the agreement with the DOJ/ATF.
Look, if you dislike what Rare Breed has done defending their patents, and refuse to buy their products, I fully support your freedom of choice. Vote with your wallet. But claiming RB is “anti-2A”, when they are only in this position because they—suffering harassment, debanking, risking fines, bankruptcy, and jail time—fought the DOJ/ATF in court, and against all odds eked out a narrow victory, 
and are now back to selling FRTs
, is crazy.
Tarr has played with binary triggers in the past. This is the unit inside an SP5, and not only has the trigger system seemed finicky, Tarr always hated the selector.
Back to the Point
Rare Breed introduced their FRT-RD3. This is a 3-position trigger, with Safe, Semi-Auto, and Forced Reset. It works with HK-pattern guns, and simply drops into your trigger housing, no modifications necessary. It uses an HK-pattern selector, and uses your stock ejector/pin/spring. At $615 for the single-sided safety version, or $620 for the ambi model, it’s not cheap…but it is very inexpensive compared to the cost of an actual registered HK machinegun ($50-60K). Provided it actually worked.

They were out of stock far more than they were in, but after months I was finally able to get one, and I bought it with my own money, so expect an unbiased review.
Upon inspection, the Rare Breed RD3 seems to be the simplest, most robust HK forced reset trigger on the market. You will need to swap your ejector/pin/spring from your trigger pack to the RB, but that’s it—and that’s simple.
The Rare Breed selector looks like a factory HK piece. The levers are aluminum, and they ride on a steel axle.
Having installed it once, I could do it again in five minutes, but that first time installation took me about fifteen minutes, because I couldn’t remember how to disassemble the HK selector, and didn’t understand the locking screw on the RB selector and thought it was more complicated than it was. MP5s like the HK SP5/SP5K with an ambi selector disassemble a bit differently than the single-sided ones. Google “how to disassemble an ambi MP5 selector” for that. You won’t need tools, just fingers. And Rare Breed has an install video for this trigger on their website, go to their FAQ page. Once you’ve got the trigger in, check function in all three positions. In forced reset, as you work the bolt, you can feel the trigger moving against your finger.
You can’t engage the safety if the hammer isn’t cocked. Trigger pull on the Rare Breed is 3.5-4.0 lbs, which is a huge improvement when compared to the stock, long, 6-7-lb HK factory trigger pull. The trigger looks like an actual HK trigger, which is my only complaint about the otherwise excellent Timney trigger for the HK guns.
I love the Rare Breed selector. The levers are machined aluminum, with serrations for texture. The axle is steel, it clicks into each position with authority, and does not go anywhere else. All of that is as it should be, and completely not my experience with binary triggers.
At the time of writing, I have 200 rounds through the Rare Breed on semi-auto. Among other things, I used it to shoot a 2-gun match, where I appreciated the lighter crisper trigger pull over the factory model. The handiness and low recoil allowed me to keep up with everyone running competition rifles…until it was time to shoot a plate rack at 90 yards, where my 5.5-inch barrel and the 9 MOA dot on my RMR were the wrong choice for the job.
In forced reset mode, I have now roughly 500 rounds through the gun. It is everything I wanted it to be.
Armament USA offers two services for Rare Breed owners—they’ll take your SP5 lower and laser-etch and color fill full-auto pictograph markings on it, and do a “clip and pin” where it looks like your lower has a forward pin, like a true full-auto gun. (Photo courtesy Armament.)
Unsuppressed, gauging it with my ear, the Rare Breed ran the K PDW at 850-900 rounds per minute (RPM). And that's roughly the same speed as an actual full-auto HK MP5K PDW. Which is exactly what the designers at Rare Breed were going for. I talked to one of the engineers and he said that not only did they want to keep the aesthetic of the original guns, which meant their grip housings, they wanted to provide the user with the same rate of fire as what they would get with actual select-fire guns. This trigger pack in a full-size MP5 reportedly will get you roughly 800 RPM, which his roughly the speed of a full-auto full-size MP5.

You can feel the trigger moving under your finger while shooting, but it is not distracting. If you are very light on the trigger, with the selector on forced reset, you can fire just one shot at a time. But pull with any authority, and the race is on. There’s no manual of arms to learn, just flip the selector down to the third position and ride the lightning.
When I attached my SilencerCo Omega 9K, the RPM went up to about 950 or so. The Omega 9K is not a low-back-pressure/flow-through can, but it is very short, so I’m guessing it provides less back pressure than some other cans. And those might increase your fire rate more than mine did. But whether you're talking 850-950 RPM I found it very controllable, especially with the vertical foregrip.
Watching the dot, the gun doesn’t climb, it just bounces. And not a lot, at least compared to a 9mm AR-15. 950 RPM sounds fast, and it is, but I didn’t have any problem doing two-round bursts.
At 10 yards I had no problem keeping 2 to 3-round busts in the center zone of IDPA and USPSA targets. At 10 yards, full mag dumps produced groups slightly larger than my palm. Your experience, as they say, may vary. I’ve got a lot of experience shooting, but don’t have nearly as much mass behind the gun as some people. But mag dumps aren’t really useful for anything other than having fun and spending a lot of money really fast, as ammo ain’t cheap.
In forced reset mode the K PDW was very controllable. Tarr could do two-round bursts, and with longer bursts the gun doesn’t climb, it just bounces. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
Where guns like this shine is in firing 2-4-round bursts. That’s also the best way to employ a binary trigger. But binaries still pale in comparison to what you get with the true forced reset trigger. The Rare Breed HK FRT truly gives you submachine gun looks and an equivalent rate of fire, but at a much lower price.

Advertisement

My K runs every type of JHP I tried in semi-auto, and every brand of FMJ in forced reset mode at 900-950 RPM. FYI you’ll see a little velocity increase over advertised numbers out of the PDW’s 5.5-inch tube, but not much. Likely about 50 FPS.
While the MP5/K/PDW has been improved to handle modern JHP ammunition, at high RPMs you will quickly find out whether or not your gun likes a certain hollowpoint bullet profile, even if it ran it in semi-auto just fine. Spend the extra money for good magazines (HKs or steel mags from Turkey), so you know they aren’t the problem if you have one.
So far Tarr has 200 rounds through the Rare Breed FRT in semi-auto, and 500 rounds in forced reset mode. It has run flawlessly so far. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
In forced reset mode, the wider mouth of 
Hornady
’s Subsonic 147-grain XTP JHPs were enough to cause a feeding issue about once a magazine. But the narrower nose of their 115- and 124-grain XTP bullets (found in all sorts of ammo from them and other manufacturers) ran without a problem. Federal’s 124-grain HSTs ran smoothly. Black Hills’ 100-grain +P Honey Badger load ran without a problem. I want to find subsonic JHP ammo that is completely reliable at 950 RPM in my gun, and I know I will, but I haven’t yet.
For discreet transport I bought an actual backpack at an office supply place, not one of those discreet tactical backpacks that screams “GRAY MAN!”. It is sized to fit a 15” laptop, and my HK SP5K PDW fits inside it with the suppressor attached and 30-round magazine inserted. Inside the backpack is a Lynx Defense (www.lynxdefense.com) Policy, which is a padded insert that protects your SBR/PDW and keeps it properly oriented. It also has a Velcro exterior, and I attached one of their magazine holsters to the exterior as well.
While not technically full-auto, the forced reset trigger from Rare Breed allows you to shoot semi-auto at equivalent speeds, giving Tarr 99.9% of the MP5K PDW SMG he always wanted. At a fraction of the price. (Photo by Andy Grossman)
If you really want your SP5K PDW with the Rare Breed to look like the real full-auto model, Armament (www.armamentusa.com) is offering full-auto bullet pictograms laser etched and color filled onto your SP5 grip housing. They will also do a “clip and pin” that makes your lower look like an authentic full-auto lower with its additional push pin. I’m thinking about it….

Advertisement

Rifles are general-purpose firearms. Submachine guns and PDWs/pistol caliber carbines with FRTs are special-purpose firearms. Where PDWs shine is proving rapid, accurate hits from point blank range out to 100 yards or so, far in excess of what you can do with a handgun. While you will often get more velocity out of longer barrels, the value of pistol-caliber submachine guns is in their ability to deliver multiple hits very rapidly, and an FRT gives you that same capability. Extensive testing has shown that, no matter your skill level, inside 25 yards you will get more hits on target per second in full-auto/forced reset than you will in semi-auto. Which means that even though I wanted an MP5K PDW simply because of how iconic it was, its defensive performance, whether in semi-auto or forced reset, isn’t wasted.
I now have 99.9% of the HK MP5K PDW that I’ve always wanted, and that’s good enough for me.
