Is an Ultralight Hunting Rifle a Burden? The Recoil Factor

The Hidden Trade-offs of Counting Ounces on the Mountain

 The author at the range with the Savage 110 Ultralight Pro. (Photo courtesy of Alice Jones Webb)

September 26, 2026

By Alice Jones Webb

I squeezed the trigger halfway through a long exhale and then held my breath. It seemed to take several heartbeats for that whisper-quiet “ping” to come back across the desert to my waiting ears.
I’m a Southern girl from the coastal plain who’s spent most of her life shooting whitetails in thick river bottoms. To me, 200 yards feels like a long shot. I regularly hunt with iron sights and a lever-action .30-30. Which is weird considering I’d just sent a lead projectile from a 7mm PRC across a portion of Carbon County, Utah, toward cowboy-shaped steel I couldn’t even see without an optic.
When that lead finally connected, I was hooked. I started high-fiving everyone within a 10-yard radius.
That experience stuck with me, and a couple years later, I found myself holding a 
Savage 110 Ultralite Pro
 chambered in 7mm PRC. I was preparing for an Idaho elk hunt. With its often wide-open ridge-to-ridge terrain, Idaho felt like the kind of place where that reach would matter. I also knew I’d be covering a lot of rough ground, and I didn’t want a heavy rifle weighing down my middle-aged flatlander body.

The rifle was gorgeous and turned heads that first day at the range. It shouldered well and felt super light in my hands. The adjustable LOP and comb height let me dial in the fit until it felt like the rifle was made just for me.
Then I shot it.
My job that day was simple. I needed to get a solid zero at 100 yards. But that first shot slammed into my shoulder hard enough to make me rethink most of my life choices, including whether I really wanted to carry a rifle this light.

The Cost of Going Light
With a carbon-fiber barrel, skeletonized receiver, and glass-filled nylon stock, my rifle weighed just under 7 pounds naked. Once I added a Leupold Mark V scope, the ready-to-hunt weight of my new rig was only 8 pounds per my bathroom scale. There wasn’t much mass to soak up recoil. My shoulder got the rest. It took only a dozen or so shots to feel confident in my zero, but by that time, all I wanted was an ice pack and Tylenol.
The week before my hunt, I pulled it out again to practice shooting distances beyond my typical whitetail ranges. I easily hit steel at 300 yards. Then stretched it out to 660 yards, and eventually felt confident that I could put a Barnes all-copper LRX through bull-sized vitals. But I found myself bracing, tension settling into my arms and shoulders just before I squeezed the trigger.
I was starting to understand what Savage had sacrificed to shave weight. I just hadn’t realized what all those missing ounces would be worth once my boots hit Idaho.
I woke before sunrise on the first day of the hunt, full of hope and optimism. My Table Mountain Outfitters guide, Dylan Downs, chugged an energy drink and talked about a bull caught on a remote trail camera the day before. “It’s a pretty easy hike in,” he said.
Dylan lies.

A half mile up a relatively gentle rise by Idaho standards, I was sucking wind like an 80-year-old asthmatic smoker. The mountains and the altitude didn’t care about any of the hours I’d spent on the gym stair stepper back home counting imaginary miles. I didn’t have much time to think about that, though. Bugles were echoing through the tamaracks, and Dylan and I were moving fast toward them. 
Then suddenly we were pinned in place by forty cow elk, just as the sun peeked over the mountains. A bull was bugling his way up the ridge behind them. I was shaking in my boots, my rifle pointed at a gap in the trees, willing my hands to keep the rifle steady.
But that bull didn’t get the playbook. He crested the ridge to our left, blowing through the timber at 35 yards, screaming so loud it vibrated my bones. I didn’t dare move a muscle. Cow eyes were staring a hole in me, and all I could do was try hard not to pee my pants.
I didn’t get a shot. There is such a thing as too close. I did, however, get a clear welcome message from Idaho. Everything there would push me to my limits, from the altitude to the terrain to the volume of eyeballs watching my every twitch.
Even ounces feel heavy after hiking five miles uphill. (Photo courtesy of Alice Jones Webb)
Every Ounce Matters
Over the next three days, 
I gained a serious appreciation of what Savage managed to leave out of my rifle
. We were in the woods before dawn, climbing ridges, dropping into draws, sometimes sidestepping our way across a brutal and breathtaking landscape. It was the kind of country that made my usual whitetail woods look tame enough to be a city park.
I kept apologizing to Dylan for slowing him down. He kept pretending not to notice. He may lie about easy hikes, but he’s a polite guy.
I collapsed into bed each night with muscles that felt like wet sand, sore in places that probably don’t have names. I wished I could just kill an elk already so I wouldn’t have to hike anymore. My lungs were too irritated with the whole altitude situation for me to care much about recoil. I was just bone-weary grateful that I wasn’t hauling a heavier rifle.
That’s the part of the ultralight rifle equation that’s easy to miss from a flatland shooting bench. A pound doesn’t feel like much on a showroom floor. It feels a heckuva lot different after you’ve carried it uphill for five miles. Do that for a string of days and every ounce starts to matter.

But carrying a rifle is only part of hunting. When things go right, you have to shoot it. I got my chance on the morning of day four.
We were back on the bull from the first morning. Again, he was bugling hard behind the same massive herd of cows. Suddenly, the whole herd split like someone had kicked an anthill. Dylan tracked a string of cows filtering through a power line, trying to slip into big timber on the other side. A lone bull was bringing up the rear.
Everything about the setup felt right. Clean shooting lane. Steady rest. The bull was trotting fast, trying to catch the cows. Dylan purred a few soft mewing cow calls, and the bull hesitated broadside for just a second at 200 yards.
And I missed.
My stomach dropped straight through the pine needles. I wanted the ground to swallow me. It was all the miles, the thin air, my aching calves, and the sick feeling that I’d wasted all the work Dylan had poured into this hunt. I felt like I’d let him down.

And then it hit me that the miss meant I’d be hiking again that afternoon. That was almost enough to bring tears. Dylan never reacted. He just shifted into problem-solving mode.
We set up in an open field to glass a series of parallel ridges. A lone 6x6 eased through the tamaracks with a single cow. We watched him for almost an hour at 2,000 yards before he bedded.
“That’s your bull,” Dylan said. “I know exactly where we’re going this afternoon.”
Waiting for my bull to get up from his afternoon bed. (Photo courtesy of Alice Jones Webb)
After lunch, we parked low and hiked to a spot with a clean view of the ridgeline a few hundred yards from where the bull bedded that morning. The bull finally stood late in the day. I watched him through the scope as he fed along, totally unaware he was being watched by a starstruck woman with more adrenaline than sense.
The bull turned broadside at 340 yards. 

Advertisement

“Whenever you’re ready,” Dylan whispered.
I was focusing hard on controlling my breathing, but I was on the verge of hyperventilating. I sent the first shot clean over his back. The bull took a few steps and stopped to look around as Dylan tried to soothe him with cow calls. I settled again, took a long breath, and put the next round straight through the vitals. His back legs wobbled. His rack swayed. I sent another round. He folded into a heap.
It took a minute for what happened to really sink in. “I just shot an elk,” I said out loud, trying to convince myself I wasn’t dreaming. Then it turned into hollering and hugs. Dylan and I had both worked hard for that bull. After four days of miles, missed opportunities, and one bruised shoulder, we finally had a bull on the ground.
A tired but successful hunt with the Savage 110 chambered in 7mm PRC. (Photo courtesy of Alice Jones Webb)
When I boarded the plane to Idaho, I thought the biggest advantage of that 7mm PRC was its ability to stretch a shot across a canyon. By the time I checked a cooler full of backstraps at the Boise airport, I realized the most important thing wasn’t whether I could print a tight group at 600 yards. If I couldn’t carry that rifle into rough country and shoot it well enough to make a solid shot once I got there, I might as well have left it at camp.
There are sacrifices to be made with an ultralight rifle like the Savage 110 I carried into elk country. My shoulder can testify to that. And for most of my hunts, which involve less than a half-mile hike from my truck to my treestand, I don’t get tired enough to appreciate the weight reduction. From a treestand, I’m not convinced that the weight sacrifice is worth the recoil. But when a hunt involves miles of steep country, every ounce matters.

In that country, recoil be damned.
