Ruger American Gen 2 Varmint Target Review: Budget Precision

A heavy-contoured barrel and redesigned stock make this a precision shooting machine.

 The Ruger American Gen2 Varmint Target is ready for the hunt or competition. (Rodger Holscher photo)

November 16, 2026

By Rodger Holscher 

If you’ve been looking for a factory tactical target or varmint rifle, you’ll notice many models labeled 'target.' Most of these rifles feature barrels that fall into one of two categories: a heavy-contour 24- to 26-inch barrel or a medium-contour 20-inch barrel. With the recently released Ruger American Gen 2 Varmint Target, we now have a 20-inch heavy-contour option.
Initial Thoughts
After receiving the rifle, I took the rifle apart to inspect its craftsmanship and the build quality of the components. Removing the stock requires a 3/16-inch Allen wrench that’s at least 4-inches long. The rear action screw is directly under the magazine release lever so getting to it can be a pain. 
The V-shaped Power Bedding Blocks of the target stock. (Rodger Holscher photo)
Once I removed the stock, I found the V-shaped Power Bedding blocks that match up with the V grooves on the front and rear of the action screws. This free floats the barrel and gives the receiver a positive lock up in the stock. The stock is an injection molded polymer. It’s light weight but feels flimsy. The rifle I evaluated was chambered in 6.5 Creedmoor and outfitted with an interchangeable magazine well that accepts AICS magazines. I’m guessing Ruger plans accommodate smaller short-action magazines like the 223 Rem and 6 ARC in the future. I had my doubts about the way it would perform with that stock because I could grab the forend and barrel and make the two touch with little effort. 
I checked the pull weight of the Ruger Marksman Adjustable trigger, it was set from the factory at 3 lbs., 14 oz. It’s not the heaviest trigger I have felt on a factory rifle, but I noted some trigger creep prior to the break. After firing several groups, I adjusted the trigger down to 3 lbs. 2 oz.

The Varmint Tactical stock is unique among the American Gen II line
. It is a target stock and includes features found on tactical-type stocks. The forend is flat and square, perfect for resting solidly on a bag. The buttstock is blockish, with a flat recoil pad, long, horizontal bag rider, vertical grip, adjustable cheekpiece and length of pull.
The Varmint Target's stock uses spacers to adjust the length of pull. Pressing a button allows the shooter to raise or lower the cheek piece. (Rodger Holscher photo)
Three 0.50-inch length-of-pull spacers are included to extend the stock’s length of pull from 13 to 14.5 inches. I did not use the spacers because I prefer a shorter length of pull. The push-button cheek riser is a nice touch; a series of notches allows you to fine-tune its height and get a solid adjustment. The forend has built-in M-LOK slots at the 6 o'clock position for adding ARCA or Picatinny rails or other compatible accessories.
When I took off the included thread protector, I found a small ding in the muzzle. It was on the thread side of the muzzle so I don’t believe it would affect the recessed crown. Looking into the chamber with a bore scope, I noted some tooling marks in the chamber but for a factory rifle, it was a decent chamber job. Prior to reinstalling and torquing the stock, I gave the barrel a good cleaning.

Accuracy Testing
For this test, I set up the rifle with a GPOTAC 4.5-27x50i FFP scope in a set of 1.26” high Rubion Precision Manufacturing (RPM) rings. I added a 9.5-inch ARCA rail to attach my T3 Optics Dauntless M bipod. To round off the build, I added an Unknown Suppressors Reaper can with a Salmon River Solutions Grim brake. With this setup and an empty 5-round magazine (included), the rifle weighed 14 lbs., 10.5 oz.
For accuracy testing, I shot a 10-round group with fast follow up shots to simulate a rifle match or varmint shooting.
After zeroing the rifle, I shot three ten-shot groups with zero barrel break-in. From my experience, if you clean the barrel well with an abrasive like JB Bore Paste, KG2, or Iosso prior to shooting, you don’t need to do the old-school break-in methods. The first nine shots of Hornady TAP 147 ELD-M ammo resulted in a 0.58-inch group with a 0.22-inch mean radius. The tenth shot opened the group to 0.98 inches with a 0.28-inch mean radius.
That was a great start, but I was struggling with that heavier trigger its creep. I would not say I am a trigger snob, but I use a lot of TriggerTech, Timney and Bix’n Andy triggers set from 1lb to 1.5lbs. The factory trigger was not ideal for me. A Timney 2-stage trigger would greatly improve this setup. The factory Hornady Match 147 ELD-M ammo opened up at the end of the string of fire due to the suppressor break coming loose and most likely affecting the group size. 
An impressive .98-inch, 10-shot group. The first 9 rounds clumped together in a .59-inch group. (Rodger Holscher photo)
I was very happy with the way this rifle shot, especially the 147 and 140 ELD-M bullets. I have no doubts that with a better trigger, these groups would have been even better. I also think the stock undermined consistency. The forend has little to no structure and a lot of flex. This causes the rifle to bounce while recoiling, especially with the heavy contoured barrel and suppressor with a brake. That brake drives the muzzle down which causes the system to flex. There is not enough rigidity in the forend to tame this. I have seen it happen with other rifles in the past. If I were to retest the rifle, I would not install the Grim brake.

Also of note, the 135 Berger classic hunter was not a good performer. The point of impact shifted up almost three inches and right almost 0.75 inch. I only shot five rounds with that ammo because I could tell right away that the rifle did not like that bullet. 
I continued to shoot this rifle from various barricades and props at the range. The recoil impulse was pleasant, and the rifle tracked well with a front bag on a prop. 
Conclusion
This rifle is a solid buy for someone looking to get into tactical target or varmint shooting. The 20-inch heavy barrel gives you a great platform to host a suppressor and not be overly long. The accuracy was really good, and I could see it getting even better with a better trigger and maybe even the stock. I did have some feeding issues with a couple different AICS magazines but was able to find a metal AICS magazine that I could tune for good feeding. 
I would recommend this rifle. You could compete in an NRL Hunter in Open or Teams division using factory ammunition. This rifle has a lot of pros and very little cons, especially at the suggested retail price.
