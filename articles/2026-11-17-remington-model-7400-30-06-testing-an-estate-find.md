Remington Model 7400 .30-06: Testing an Estate Find

For fast-paced action afield, the classic semiautomatic Model 7400 hunting rifle can't be beat.

 Remington’s classic Model 7400 was a well-built semiautomatic hunting rifle chambered in hard-hitting cartridges. (Joseph von Benedikt)

November 17, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

Although the story of the Remington Model 7400 semiautomatic hunting rifle officially began with the Model 740 in 1952, its legacy began much farther back, in 1908. Remington’s Model 8, introduced that year, was the first truly successful “self-loading” hunting rifle, and it kicked off a long tradition of Remington semiautomatic deer rifles.
Glorious though it was, the Model 8 had issues. So did the Model 740. It took 29 years, but eventually most of the bugs got worked out of the 740, and new life was breathed into the revised version along with a brand-new zero in the model number. Enter the Model 7400.
Chambered for popular high-powered cartridges like the .30-06 (shown here), as well as the .243 Winchester, .270 Winchester, .308 Winchester, and .280 Remington, the 7400 also was chambered for intriguing cartridges like 6mm Remington and .35 Whelen. The design featured a rotating bolthead that locked into battery much like a bolt-action rifle, providing the strength necessary to safely fire these high-octane centerfire hunting cartridges.
Model 7400s were fed by detachable box magazines, which typically contained four rounds. Bolt reciprocation was powered by a gas-operated system housed inside the beavertail forearm, which had ergonomics reminiscent of many pump-action 12-gauge shotguns.

Two primary versions were available. One was fitted with an 18-inch barrel and filled the need for a quick-handling carbine. The other iteration—like the rifle pictured and tested here—had a 22-inch barrel.
Both versions had walnut stocks with high-gloss finishes, hard black plastic buttplates with white spacers, and the pressed-in “checkering” common to the era. A black grip cap and nose cap featured matching white-line spacers.
Synthetic versions with a fiberglass-reinforced stock were introduced in 1996, and in 2003, near the very end of its run, a 7400 Weathermaster version featured a matte nickel finish.

Mechanicals
I’m no expert on Model 7400s, so I won’t profess to know much about how the gas-operated action is driven. It features a reciprocating charging handle on the right side, much like most semiautomatic shotguns. To load, stoke the detachable box magazine with fresh cartridges, insert it into the bottom of the action, haul back on the charging handle, and let go. The bolt will leap forward, propelled by the return spring, stripping a cartridge from the magazine and chambering it.
The safety is a crossbolt type housed in the rear of the trigger guard. Pushed to the right is “Safe.” Press it through to the left, aim, and squeeze the trigger to fire. When the magazine is empty the bolt will lock rearward. The magazine is next to impossible to remove while the bolt is locked back because the rear of the cartridge follower serves as the bolt hold-open. Uniquely, there’s a round, knurl-headed lever in the left bottom edge of the magazine; press it forward to drop the bolt, then press the mag release button housed at the front right side of the trigger bow to drop the magazine.
Provenance
I purchased the rifle shown at an estate sale where the collection of an older gentleman with good taste in rifles but poor taste in scopes was being sold after his passing. The photograph does not do justice to how excellent the condition is. I can’t find a sign of wear or a scratch on it anywhere. It came with the Tasco scope shown mounted in decent Redfield bases.
Rangetime
Gathering up three .30-06 factory loads, I headed to the range. Hoping the Model 7400’s scope was at least roughly sighted in, I fired at a big target at 100 yards. No bullet hole appeared. Shifting my aim to 25 yards, I fired at a similarly large target. Still no impact.
One can’t very well bore-sight a Model 7400, so I walked up to 10 yards, and finally caught a bullet. It was far to one side. Back at the bench I went to adjust the side screws in the base to shift the scope’s windage alignment—and discovered that both screws were loose.

Snugging them down, I hunkered behind the rifle to fire again—and found that the image in the scope had gone extremely blurry. I reached up to turn the magnification ring down, thinking that perhaps by zooming out I’d be able to see better. The entire magnification ring and housing made a slight scraping sound and suddenly spun freely. Plus it now had about a quarter-inch of movement forward and back. Folks, don’t buy cheap scopes.
Clearly, although it appeared brand new, the Tasco was toast. Replacing the old Tasco with a Trijicon 3-9X 40mm AccuPoint I had on my shelf, I quickly and easily got it sighted in and went to work.
First, I tested Hornady’s 178-grain ELD-X Precision Hunter load. At more than 3 MOA accuracy wasn’t inspiring, but the rifle ran beautifully. Next was Barnes’s new Harvest ammo loaded with Sierra 165-grain Tipped Game-King bullets. This load shot much better, averaging 1.68 inches, but most of the case rims slipped out from beneath the little C-clip type extractor (similar to those in Model 700 bolt-action Remingtons) and failed to extract. Headstamps showed that Barnes had used PPU brass when loading that ammo, and the Model 7400 did not like it one bit.
Last, I tested Nosler’s new 180-grain Whitetail Country load. It averaged 2.47 inches, and it ran flawlessly.
A quick internet search suggested that most Model 7400 rifles shoot between 1.5 and 3 MOA at 100 yards. The design is fairly picky, but testing several different brands of ammo is likely to turn up a load that shoots well. I’ll have to keep looking until I find a load that combines the accuracy of the Barnes ammo with the reliability of the others.
Recoil throughout my shooting was mild and—for lack of a better term—ka-chunky. The bolt reciprocated at low speed (for a semiautomatic) and ate up a lot of the kick. It’s a pleasant gun to shoot.
Final Thoughts
In the field, Model 7400 rifles are most suitable to whitetails and wild hogs in areas where 200 yards is a long shot. They are particularly well-suited for driven hunts, where a couple of fast follow-up shots can be a significant advantage.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
