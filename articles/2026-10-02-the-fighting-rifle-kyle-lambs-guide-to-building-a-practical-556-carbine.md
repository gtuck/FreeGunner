The Fighting Rifle: Kyle Lamb's Guide to Building a Practical 5.56 Carbine

Kyle Lamb breaks down the essential components of a dependable 5.56 carbine, from optics and lights to magazines, ammunition, slings and the training that matters most.

 (Photo by Lukas Lamb)

October 02, 2026

By Kyle Lamb

“Common sense isn’t so common.” Voltaire
I never thought the day would come that a quote from an Enlightenment writer such as Voltaire would grace the opening of a former Army Special Operations soldiers article, but there it is.
Common sense truly isn’t common to many who start building a so called “Fighting Rifle.” The legions of advertisements push you to believe that with just one more widget you can step into the land of milk and honey, and never look back with your amazingly scientifically enhanced, truly revolutionary and might I add, exquisite Fighting Rifle. The fact is, all you are really fighting is poverty if you continually go after the latest and greatest gadgetry just for the sake of it.
The full build entails a red dot sight, light, vertical grip with battery storage, and sling. Some shooters may be inclined to swap triggers, too. (Photo by Lukas Lamb)
I intend to lay out “Thee Rifle” that I use daily, carried for years, and would stake my life and that of my family on. Don’t be alarmed if it isn’t what you already have, as you can eventually get to the same end game with your current set up.

Starting with the rifle (or better termed carbine) of choice, here is what I would list as my must haves for this rig. It will absolutely be chambered in 5.56mm NATO or .223 Wylde, allowing for easy availability of quality ammunition. Remember, this is my list, not yours; so, if you have chosen to go with a caliber “cooler” than mine, knock yourself out. But in the end don’t be disappointed with poor performance and difficulty acquiring the Dracula Killing, dipped in Virgin Blood ammunition, only available in quantities of 5 rounds per box. That is why I prefer 5.56mm, it simply works, and works well.
The carbine I choose has a 16-inch barrel, because it’s legal and provides enough room for 5.56mm NATO bullets to get the speed they need to perform not just at 7 yards but at 200 plus yards as well. In most states the 16-inch barrel won’t require any special paperwork either. If you do choose a shorter barrel beware of the distance boundaries you are placing on your so-called Fighting Rifle. Terminal Ballistics are influenced heavily by bullet speed. End of story.
A no-frills, 16-inch barreled AR-15 is a great do-it-all gun. (Photo by Lukas Lamb)
Although this carbine can be very basic, it should also have a collapsible stock, so it can fit other family members, and be adjusted for your stature. I also prefer a carbine that can shoot at a minimum, 3 MOA. Most quality carbines can achieve this easily. For those of you not aware of what 3 MOA translates to, it literally means it will shoot 3 inches at 100 yards, give or take a decimal point here and there. I shoot 5 round groups and all rounds count.

The most basic accessories are then added. I prefer a red dot sight with a small, intense dot, like the 1 MOA dot you will find in the 
Leupold LCO Pro F2
. It’s small and bright and has repeatable adjustments. It also uses a CR 123 battery which is available at almost every store I frequent.
The next accessory is a light, there are many lights, but this 
Surefire Turbo
 is mine. The Turbo also comes with a mount that will attach to Picatinny as well as MLOK rails. More on that in a minute. The Surefire can also be equipped with a pressure pad, or you can use the provided tail cap with momentary and hands-free on capability. It also uses CR 123 batteries, but can also be equipped with rechargeable batteries.
Surefire's mounting system can adapt to both Picatinny or MLOK rails. (Photo by Lukas Lamb)
At this point the only other must have is reliable 
Magpul magazines
 and good ammo. I prefer 
Hornady
 for gassing up the Fighting Rifle, as they make several flavors that lend themselves nicely to home defense and urban combat. I have found that their 
55 grainers
 are awesome as well as the 
53 grainers
. I generally use heavier bullets though, something in the range from 73-75 grains. Overall, ammunition is heavily user-preference-based, so I’ll leave that up to you. I use .223 Remington and 5.56mm NATO ammunition interchangeably.
Hornady's 5.56 and .223 Rem will get the job done. (Photo by Lukas Lamb)
Now for the not required but nice to have portion of the Fighting Carbine. Due to their nature, free float hand guards enhance accuracy. In this day and age, I always use those equipped with MLOK capability, and most tactical versions also have a full length top Picatinny rail. On this rail you can mount your light and for me, I always try to attach a short vertical grip. I use the 
Viking Tactics G-10 vertical grip
, it’s just enough length to accomplish its tasks and also has battery storage for those CR123’s used in my Surefire Light and Leupold Optic.
Last but not least is the 
Viking Tactics sling
, I am obviously partial to this sling, but it truly gives you all you need and allows for more adjustability than any of the closed loop versions on the market. You may have to attach a mount on your free-floating hand guard to attach a heavy-duty quick attach sling swivel.

So, with all these basic accessories in place, the Fighting Rifle is ready for action. On some of my carbines I replace the trigger system as well. But this isn’t a must since some carbines are built with decent triggers. When I do change my trigger I generally use a 
Trigger Tech Diamond single stage trigger
. The weight is easily adjustable and allows you to tune to your ability and comfort level. Trigger Tech also makes Duty Triggers but at the time of writing I haven’t used that particular version of their trigger.
Triggertech's Diamond single stage trigger is a simple swap to make, as the whole thing can be dropped into the rifle. (Photo by Lukas Lamb)
Now that you have that Fighting Carbine ready for action, it’s time to get on the range and train. Spend your hard-earned money on a decent rifle, then spend the rest of your remaining cash on training. You will never regret good training.
