The Ultimate Uberti 1873 Guide: History, Rifle & Carbine Review

 Uberti’s replica Winchester Model 1873 Special Sporting is a classy, accurate lever gun. (Layne Simpson photo)

October 01, 2019

By Layne Simpson

Since 1959 the Italian firm Uberti has specialized in bringing famous rifles and revolvers of yesteryear back to life. The company’s high-quality reproduction of the Winchester Model 1873 is one of my favorites. Before taking a close look at the Special Sporting version, let’s hop aboard my trusty time-travel machine and visit the beginning of a rifle that many believe won the West.
Horace Smith and Daniel Wesson would later make their fortunes manufacturing handguns with revolving cylinders, but in 1854 they owned the Volcanic Repeating Arms Company. Their product was a lever-action pistol with an exposed hammer and a tubular magazine. Called the Volcanic, its ammunition was described in patents as “loaded ball,” and it consisted of a conical lead bullet with a hollow base containing a charge of black powder.
Using some of the profits from his clothing factory, Oliver F. Winchester invested in what would turn out to be a short-lived venture. The Volcanic pistol proved to be a slow seller, and during bankruptcy proceedings in 1856, Winchester became the sole owner of the company and renamed it New Haven Arms Company.
1873 Origins
Benjamin Tyler Henry was hired as company superintendent in 1858, and his first assignment from Winchester was to improve the Volcanic pistol and develop a metallic cartridge for it. By 1860 Henry had come up with a .44 caliber rimfire cartridge, but far more important to the future of Oliver Winchester, he had transformed the pistol into a rifle with a magazine capacity of 16 rounds.

Unwilling to invest money in new tooling required for producing the rifle, Winchester signed an agreement that gave Henry exclusive rights to its manufacture. Upon expiration of the contract in 1864, Winchester regained all rights to what had become the Model 1860 Henry rifle. A highly desired, although rather scarce, firearm among select Union troops during the American Civil War, the Henry is easily recognized by a number of features, including a brass frame and front loading of its tubular magazine.
While the Henry was far superior in firepower to the Spencer repeater used in greater numbers during the war, it fell short of perfection. The exposed follower thumb-tab used when loading the magazine required a full-length slot in the bottom of the magazine that, under battlefield conditions, could allow mud and debris to enter and muck up the works.
The Henry was improved in 1866 by the addition of a hinged magazine loading gate in the side of its receiver and a wooden fore-end. Renamed the Winchester Model 66, it had a brass receiver and was chambered for the .44 Henry Rimfire.

Additional improvements resulted in the Model 1873. The receiver was first machined of forged iron and then later of steel. A sliding cover kept dust, dirt and water from entering the action. With the cover in its closed position, pushing the finger lever forward slides the cover to the rear where it remains until manually pushed forward to close off the top of the receiver.
The addition of a trigger block prevented the rifle from firing unless the lever was in the full-lock position. A latch called a lever hook held the lever close against the grip of the rifle. Rotating the latch to its disengaged position released the lever for cycling.
The Winchester 1873 was eventually chambered for a number of cartridges, including the .22 Short and .22 Long rimfires, but the .44-40 Win. proved to be the most popular. During its 46 years of production, numerous variations and barrel lengths were offered. Options included a straight- or curved-grip stock, a jointed steel cleaning rod stowed in the stock, a tang-mounted aperture sight and a set trigger.
Perhaps the most famous were the “One of a Thousand” series with barrels hand-selected for accuracy. The plot of the 1950 film “Winchester ’73” starring James Stewart as Lin McAdam was built around one of those rifles. And speaking of movies, I recall watching “High Noon” as a kid and being puzzled when town marshal Will Kane (played by Gary Cooper) passed on a pair of ’73 Winchesters stowed in the office of his jail and carried only his single-action Colt revolver when going against four bad guys.
The color case-hardened finish on the receiver, hammer, trigger and finger lever contrasts nicely with the blued finish on the buttplate, fore-end nose cap, barrel and magazine tube. (Layne Simpson photo)
Uberti Rifle and Carbine 1873 Features
Uberti offers a dozen or so variations of Winchester 1873 rifles and carbines in .357 Mag., .44-40, .44 Mag. and .45 Colt. Just my opinion, but I don’t believe the breech lockup of the 1873 is sturdy enough to handle a steady diet of full-power .44 Mag. loads.

The company would not offer the rifle in that chambering if it were unsafe, but a steady diet will accelerate wear and cause looseness in a 145-year-old breech locking design, which is rather weak when compared to later Winchester rifles. Chamber pressure for the .45 Colt should not exceed 14,000 p.s.i., which is also maximum for Colt’s Single Action Army revolver in serviceable condition. It’s far kinder to the action.
The receiver of the Model 1873 is quite strong, but its lockup is not as strong as later Winchester actions such as the Model 1886 and Model 1892. Here is how it works.
The Winchester 1873, Winchester 1866 and the 1860 Henry share the same toggle-joint breech bolt locking system. The tail of the rear section of a steel toggle is attached to the receiver while the nose of the front section is attached to the breech bolt, the body of which is only 0.355 inch in diameter.
The swiveling midpoint of the toggle is pinned to the top end of the finger lever. Pushing the lever forward collapses the toggle at its midsection, releasing its locking pressure on the breech bolt.
Continued movement of the finger lever fully withdraws the bolt, and as it reaches the limit of its travel, the hammer is cocked and a brass carrier block elevates a cartridge into a horizontal position and in alignment with the chamber.
Pulling the lever fully to the rear brings the breech bolt forward to push the cartridge into the chamber and lowers the carrier block to pick up the next cartridge in the magazine. Just as the bolt makes contact with the breech, the lever pushes the toggle into its rigid or locked position.
The toggle works like a finger on your hand. When the finger is outstretched, it can resist considerable force applied against its tip by the palm of your other hand. Allow the finger to bend at its joint and its ability to resist applied force virtually disappears.
Swinging the lever forward elevates the brass carrier block to position a cartridge in perfect alignment with the chamber. The rifle can be topped off by pushing a round into the receiver. (Layne Simpson photo)
The cartridge feeding system is actually superior to more modern lever-action designs in which a cartridge has to travel at an angle on its way to the chamber. To access the inside of the 1873 receiver for cleaning, use a gunsmith-grade, parallel-tipped screwdriver of the proper size to remove a sideplate.

Fit and Finish
The quality and workmanship of the Special Sporting rifle I shot were flawless. Wood-to-metal fit was custom rifle excellent. It is one of the most handsome rifles of any type built by any company at any time.
The finish on the A-grade walnut stock has the same dark reddish hue as on rifles built in the old days by the Winchester custom shop. The stock and fore-end have good coverage of nicely executed 20-line cut checkering.
The nose of the fore-end is capped with blued steel. A steel crescent buttplate on the stock prevents the gun from slipping off the shoulder when quickly slinging lead downrange, and while I would not want it on a light rifle chambered for a hard-kicking cartridge, due to the eight-pound weight of the Uberti rifle along with the fairly light .45 Colt loads I shot, it proved to be ideal.
The receiver, finger lever, hammer and trigger have an attractive color case-hardened finish. The buttplate, lever latch, loading gate, barrel and magazine are blued.
The full-octagon barrel is 23.25 inches long and measures 0.750 inch across the flats. Rifling twist is 1:16, and my Lyman Digital Bore Cam revealed extremely smooth lands and grooves.

Advertisement

Sights consist of a ladder-adjustable buckhorn at the rear and a 0.120-inch-wide plain black blade up front. Both sights are dovetailed to the barrel. As many traditionalists prefer, the rifle has a half-cock notch on the hammer; no manual safeties have been added to the original Winchester design.
According to specifications included with the rifle, its magazine holds 13 rounds of .45 Colt, but the magazine of the test rifle accepted 14 with room to spare—making it a 15-shooter when you have one round in the chamber. Switching to the shorter .45 Schofield cartridge increased firepower to 16 shots.
The 1873 is quite 
popular among Cowboy Action shooters
, and many use the .45 Schofield for reasons other than the extra round. Its shorter case is more suitable for use with small charges of smokeless powders, and in stages that require another round beyond magazine capacity to complete, it is more fumble-free to single load than the longer .45 Colt.
With the lever fully open, place a cartridge atop the carrier block, press it down into the receiver, close the lever and the round is in the chamber. The rifle I shot fed both cartridges and ejected spent cases without a hitch as long as the lever was operated aggressively.
Lever-action rifles designed during the 19th century and built on modern computerized machinery can be surprisingly accurate. For quite some time I have had a Henry in .44-40 built by today’s Henry Repeating Arms. It consistently shoots five cast or swaged-lead bullets inside two inches at 50 yards with no small number of individual groups measuring less than 1.5 inches.

Performance
The Uberti 1873 proved to be as accurate. There is no law against shooting jacketed bullets, but those without jackets not only are more traditional, but also are less expensive and more fun to shoot. 
Hornady
, HSM, Black Hills and others offer .45 Colt ammo loaded with that type of bullet.
As for handloads, the 
Cast Bullet Handbook
 from Lyman is the very best source of information on the subject, and it contains plenty of load data for many rifle and handgun cartridges. The .45 Colt load with the Blackhorn 209 black powder substitute I shot came from Western Powders’ 
Handloading Guide.
 Maximum overall length for the .45 Colt is 1.600 inches with most factory Cowboy Action ammo running 1.560 to 1.570 inches.
As a rule, Model 1873 rifles won’t reliably feed cartridges loaded with semi-wadcutter cast bullets. The best bullet shape is a roundnose with a fairly large meplat. The Lyman No. 452664 is an excellent example. For those who would rather not cast their own bullets, Rim Rock Bullets and Missouri Bullet Company offer them.
After punching paper with 200 rounds from a rest, I rapid-fired another 150 rounds offhand at steel bad guys. I kicked myself for not bringing more ammo. The 1873 can become seriously addictive.
Closing Thoughts
Shooting the Uberti 1873 reminded me of a time long ago. The art of still-hunting was practiced by many, and their rifle of choice was a lever action chambered for one of the old classics.

Advertisement

My father took most of his deer with a Winchester Model 92 in .44-40 and Remington factory ammunition loaded with a 200-grain bullet at 1,200 fps. A woods hunter, he took most of his bucks inside 50 yards and considered anything farther than 100 long paces to be a risky shot. Even after I gave him a Remington Model 600 in .308 his heart was still with that little Winchester.
If Dad were here today, he would likely describe the Uberti 1873 Special Sporting replica Winchester as a fine-looking deer rifle that holds steady and shoots true.
