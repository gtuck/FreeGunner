## Article Text (extracted)

Smith & Wesson Reintroduces the Night Guard Revolvers.

The Cult-Classic Scandium/Aluminum-Alloy Frame Wheelguns Are Finally Back.

 Both of the 2026 Night Guards are of the L-frame persuasion, and they’re offered as exclusives for Lipsey’s distributors. (J. Scott Rupp)

June 25, 2026

By J. Scott Rupp

The new Night Guard revolvers from Smith & Wesson might seem familiar to longtime S&W fans. That’s because both models—the Model 386 in .357 Magnum and the Model 396 in .44 Special—are in fact returnees to the company’s wheelgun lineup.
The quick backstory is the models 386 and 396 were introduced in 2000—the former as the .357 Magnum AirLite Sc Mountain Lite and the latter as the .44 Special Ti AirLite Mountain Lite. Both were L-frame guns. Then, in 2009, Smith & Wesson trotted out the Night Guards. This family of scandium-frame revolvers included two Ns (.44 Magnum, eight-shot .357 Magnum), two Ls (seven-shot .357 and .44 Special) and one K (.38 Special).
Both of the 2026 Night Guards are of the L-frame persuasion, and they’re offered as exclusives for Lipsey’s distributors—meaning they’re not standard-catalog S&W models. The two revolvers share a lot of the same characteristics. They’re lightweight, scandium/aluminum alloy-frame guns with three-inch stainless steel barrels, Hogue Bantam rubber grips, XS day/night front sights and an all-black PVD finish on frame, barrel shroud and stainless-steel cylinder. Something that’s sure to please a lot of S&W revolver purists is the lack an internal lock. Both have a suggested retail price of $1,269.
The 386 Night Guard (l.) holds seven rounds of .357 Magnum while the 396 Night Guard has a five-shot .44 Special cylinder. (J. Scott Rupp)
.357 Magnum Night Guard
While I’m enamored of the .44 Special, I suspect the .357 Magnum will be the more popular of the two, so I’ll cover it first. Why does it win the popularity contest? One, its cylinder holds seven rounds, which isn’t your everyday .357; and two, the ability to choose from a wide spectrum of .357 Magnum and .38 Special loads is an incredible advantage.

When you combine this kind of capacity and power potential with a scandium frame that results in a weight of just 24 ounces, it gives the Model 386 Night Guard a leg up as a carry gun. Sure, it doesn’t match the amount of ammo you can cram into, say, a 9mm semiautomatic weighing about the same, but it’s utterly reliable, and the energy advantage of the .357 Magnum over typical pistol cartridges is no small consideration.
The new Model 386 Night Guard is not quite a doppelganger for the previous Night Guard. At three inches, the barrel is half an inch longer. I’m a big fan of this barrel length because I think it provides the right balance between concealability and shootability.
The front sight is also different. It is an XS Sights day/night model with a luminescent green ring surrounding a tritium lamp, one of the most visible and useful sights on the market. While the first Night Guards had a Cylinder & Slide Extreme Duty non-adjustable rear sight, the new Night Guard 386 features a traditional S&W adjustable plain black blade.

I have .357s in both J- and K-frame configurations, and while the latter is a particular favorite of mine, it’s easy to see why Smith & Wesson developed the L frame that’s used in the 386 Night Guard. The K frame suffered when subjected to a heavy diet of full-power .357 loads, and the N frame was just too damn big for a .357, even though its durability was beyond question.
To develop the L frame, Smith & Wesson beefed up the K and added a full-length underlug for a bit more weight out front. And in a nod to longevity, on the new Night Guards S&W has installed a stainless steel blast shield in the topstrap forward of the cylinder to prevent gas cutting. The shield is present on both models, although I would think it’s a little more critical on the .357 Magnum, which operates at a much higher pressure.
You could definitely put the Model 386 Night Guard on a heavy full-time diet of .357s if you were so inclined. While the Model 386 Night Guard definitely falls on the light end of the spectrum for a .357, I actually found it to be slightly more controllable than the .44 Special version—although to be fair the .357s I had on hand were the lighter, more modern 125-grain loads as opposed to the old standard 158s.
The gun wasn’t unpleasant to shoot from the bench during accuracy testing, and in drills it proved quite capable of accurate and fast double-action fire. And again, with the 386 you’ve got seven rounds at your disposal instead of the five or six you get with typical .357 Magnum revolvers.
And .38 Specials? Piece of cake. The +Ps I tested were 100-grain HoneyBadgers from Black Hills, but any +P or standard .38 Special is going to be easy for just about anyone to shoot well—and quickly if necessary.

Aside from the different chambering, capacity and a slight variance in weight, the Model 396 Night Guard is identical to its 386 stablemate. To me, it’s the cooler one.
.44 Special Night Guard
I’ve gone on and on about the 
.44 Special in Handguns
 before, so I won’t do that here other than to say it is one of my favorite handgun cartridges. I have a few revolvers chambered to the round, and a Ruger GP100 .44 Special loaded with cast bullets has been one of my go-to trail guns. However, I have to admit I find the Ruger to be on the heavy side when I want to go minimalist in the backcountry.
The Model 396 Night Guard, a full 12 ounces lighter than my GP100, solves the problem while still providing five rounds of those fat, relatively hard-hitting .44 caliber bullets. Some time back I was doing penetration testing in synthetic gel blocks, and just for fun I threw in the Oregon Trails Laser Cast 240-grain Keith-style bullets I load in my Ruger for trail use. Three rounds zipped right through the 16-inch gel block, so despite the relatively low power level, it doesn’t lack penetration.
Now I wouldn’t dream of counting on this ammo to stop a brown bear, but a problem black bear, wolf or mountain lion I might be faced with here in Colorado would be another matter. Despite the gun’s light weight, it’s easy to hit with in double action while being way more pleasant to carry on long hikes. Those same traits, along with the .44’s effectiveness when loaded with today’s great choices for defensive ammo, make it a good choice for concealed carry around town as well.
Both Night Guards incorporate an excellent green luminescent/tritium day/night XS front sight. (J. Scott Rupp)
Common Features
Whether you choose the .357 or the .44, the Night Guards have you covered for defensive uses. The front sight is impossible to miss in daylight and easy to pick up in low or even no light.
Although I’m a sucker for the old white-outline rear sight, which I could easily swap out if I was so inclined, the plain black serrated rear is perfectly fine and allows you to adjust for windage and elevation. That’s especially key for the .357, which has such a wide range of defense loads you can shoot.
The triggers are great. Both had smooth double-action pulls, with a typical amount of stacking. The Model 386 Night Guard had a lighter DA pull than the 396, and because I love to tinker, it would be an easy matter to get a spring kit and tune the 396’s pull more to my liking.
The hammer spur is aggressively checkered for easy cocking when you want to shoot single action, and the single-action pulls were nice and crisp.

The earlier Night Guards had straight rubber grips, while the current models feature finger-grooved Hogue Bantams. I’m of two minds here. Straight grips tend to be faster and more consistent on the draw for me. While the grooves do direct my fingers where to go, sometimes it take a little longer to get that proper initial grip—most likely because the grooves don’t exactly match my hands.
Grips are Hogue Bantam overmolded rubber with finger grooves and a Cobblestone texture. They provide good control and help to reduce felt recoil. (J. Scott Rupp)
On the flip side, I think finger grooves provide more recoil control, and the Cobblestone texturing on the sides helps. So it’s a personal decision. And like the springs, there are plenty of aftermarket options for L-frame grips. But I really think most buyers will find the Hogue Bantams to be just fine.
In fact, I think the wheelgun-minded among us will find both these guns to be fine in all regards. In today’s parlance, they check every box: practical calibers, light weight, versatile barrel length, low-key finish.
Summary
When I had the chance to run a cylinder’s worth of ammo through each at this year’s SHOT Show in January, I’d already made up my mind that not only was I going to test both, I was going to buy one of them when I was done. Of course, I already pretty much knew which one it was probably going to be, and my credit card is all the poorer for the .44 Special Model 396 Night Guard I now own.
But I’m all the richer for owning it, and it’s going to be a constant companion on the trail. For that I’m going to need a holster. Searching the internet turns up a lot of “night guard” holsters, but these are usually for guns other than the new L-frame Night Guards.

Advertisement

Galco, however, offers a Combat Master and a Masterbilt Revenger specifically for them, and Kirkpatrick Leather has holsters for three-inch L-frame revolvers. I’m sure if you put in the time, you can find other rigs that would work.
I think these new Night Guard revolvers are worth considering by anyone looking for a do-everything carry wheelgun. While they’re currently Lipsey’s exclusives, I would think they could become everyday production guns because of how much they have to offer to today’s revolver fans.
