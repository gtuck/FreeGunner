Which 100-Year-Old Hunting Rifle Performed Best?

Taking aim with any of these classics is highly satisfying, but how did they compare?

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

September 07, 2026

By Dave Emary

We have reached the end of our series, “Of Wood & Steel,” featuring popular hunting rifles offered 100 years ago. The mid-­1920s marked the beginning of the trend away from the lever-­action’s long-­established market dominance, but American sportsmens’ transition to bolt actions was interrupted by the Great Depression in 1929 and then again by World War II. By the mid-­1950s, though, there is no question that lever-­action hunting rifles were sliding in popularity.
Following Guns & Ammo’s evaluations and studies of hunters in the 1920s, I’d like to offer my own ratings of these rifles based on our impressions of shooting them, the features and performance. I would also like to rank these rifles from the perspective of a 20th-­century American hunter.
Performance, in terms of accuracy potential, is a little subjective due to the lack of optical sights on these rifles. Sighting would have been improved with the five rifles equipped with peep sights. The Remington Model 14 had a traditional slide-­adjustable open-­barrel mounted sight. The Remington Model 8 already had a 1950s-­era Weaver K3 scope mounted to it when it was purchased. As an experiment, we retested the Model 8 with the K3 scope after testing it with its peep sight. The improvement in our accuracy results was dramatic, and that would have been the case with scoping any of these rifles. Table 1 shows a summary of each rifle’s characteristics.
6. Winchester Model 1907
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 
Winchester Model 1907
 was a well-­balanced, fast-­handling and compact rifle, albeit heavy due to the blowback semiautomatic design. The gun came to the shoulder easily and pointed well, but that’s where the good attributes end. It was the heaviest gun in our group at 8 pounds, 10 ounces. G&A’s M1907 also possessed a horrible trigger at 9 pounds, 8 ounces, with noticeable creep. Its accuracy results were near the bottom of the list and it was the only gun in the group that could not hold a 10-­inch kill zone on a deer at 200 yards. The .351 WSL would have been a good short-­range cartridge, but its capability fell off rapidly after 100 yards. I would view this gun as only a woods or treestand hunting rifle. Unfortunately, the Model 1907 was a distant last in our six-­gun shoot-­off.

5. Remington Model 8
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 
Remington Model 8
 has many attractive attributes. It has a reasonable weight at 7 pounds, 8 ounces, and presented a good trigger at a crisp 3 pounds, 6 ounces. It is also rather compact and fast handling, and was chambered in a series of what are now classic and effective deer cartridges. G&A’s particular test rifle had a larger front sight bead, which definitely hampered accuracy results on paper. It was the most inaccurate of the rifles tested at 100 yards, printing an aggregate group of 4.9 inches. However, the Model 8 still held a group within a deer’s 10-­inch kill zone at 200 yards!
When equipped with the Weaver K3 scope, the accuracy results for the Model 8 were reduced to a stunning 1.4-­inch average at 100 yards. It would certainly be worthwhile to evaluate the Model 8 with a smaller front sight bead. With finer sights, the Model 8 would have been an effective and reliable semiautomatic deer rifle. The semiautomatic action also allowed us to stay on the sights for quick follow-­up shots.
4. Remington Model 14
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
At this point, the remaining rifles are essentially a toss-­up. They all served as effective deer rifles. 
The Remington Model 14
 was nearly the lightest gun in the group, though, at 7 pounds, 2 ounces. It had a fairly crisp trigger that measured a little heavy at 6 pounds, 10 ounces. It is slim, well balanced, easy to carry and a fast-­pointing rifle. Loading the first round in the loading gate required a little finessing, but after that feeding it was easy. The action is fast and allowed us to easily stay on the sights for follow-­up shots, also.

As with the Model 8, the same effective cartridges were also chambered in the Model 14. The Model 14 produced reasonable accuracy with an aggregate group at 100 yards of 3.8 inches. It easily held our deer target’s 10-­inch kill zone at 200 yards. Peep sights were and are available for the Model 14. Peeps would probably provide accuracy improvements to bring it near the best of the group.
3. Savage Model 1899 Takedown
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 
Savage Model 1899
 was a light rifle for the time. It was the lightest in our test group weighing just 7 pounds. It had a crisp trigger for a lever action, too, at 4 pounds, 15 ounces. If you can get past the somewhat non-­typical appearance of the rifle, you’ll notice that it was beautifully made and chambered in several powerful and long-­range cartridges for the era. The top-­loading rotary magazine was easy to load, also. The receiver felt a little bulky in the hand, but the light weight made up for this and helped it carry and point easily. The smooth operation of the lever action made quick follow-­up shots a breeze, never requiring us to come off the sights.
The 1899 was near the top of the group’s accuracy chart, producing a 100-­yard group aggregate of 3.4 inches. It easily held the 10-­inch deer kill zone at 200 yards. With the .300 Savage chambering, its effective range was well beyond 200 yards, merely dependent on a shooter’s eyesight.
2. Marlin 1893
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The 
1893 Marlin
 was a classic lever-­action rifle, even for the mid-­1920s. It carried and balanced wonderfully in the hand, and was fast pointing with a nice trigger. Ours tested at a crisp 5 pounds, 12 ounces. The side-­loading-­gate magazine is timeless, too, facilitating easy loading.
The ’93 was available in the classic lever-­action deer cartridges of the time, and ours was equipped with a tang peep sight and fine bead front sight. The sight picture was quite good with this rifle. The action was smooth and allowed for quick follow-­up shots without needing to come off the sights. We were surprised by the good accuracy of 2.8 inches at 100 yards. Essentially, its accuracy potential rivaled the Model 54 Winchester. Younger eyes could probably extract even more accuracy from this ’93. The Marlin easily held the 10-­inch kill zone of our deer target at 200 yards. The only limitations to harnessing the Marlin’s effectiveness would have been the user’s eyesight and the ballistics of the cartridges it was chambered for.

1. Winchester Model 54
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
Winchester’s Model 54
 features all of the attributes of a good bolt-­action rifle that we are familiar with today. G&A’s sample was well made and had a target-­quality trigger with a pull of only 1 pound, 8 ounces. The weight of the rifle — 7 pounds, 14 ounces — made it one of the heavier rifles of the group, though. The gun loads from the top, has a fixed magazine floorplate and can be loaded from stripper clips. The gun balanced well when carried near the front of the magazine floorplate, and the bolt was buttery smooth. The Model 54 was also the longest at 44.4-­inches long.
Our 54 was equipped with a receiver-­mounted peep sight, enhancing the sight picture. As with all bolt actions, follow-­up shots were more difficult than the levers and pump-­actions. A bolt action requires the shooter to roll his head to the side to cycle the bolt, causing the shooter to lose sight picture.
On paper, the Model 54 in .270 Winchester proved to be the most accurate rifle of the group, printing a 2.6-­inch aggregate group at 100 yards. It consistently threw a flyer, likely attributable to the non-­free-­float barrel configuration. Going in its favor, the Model 54 was chambered in cartridges that are still considered powerful today. It easily held the 10-­inch kill zone on our 200-­yard deer target, too. Chambered in .270 Winchester, it’s effective performance would have only been limited by the shooter’s eyesight.
A Hunter’s Rifle in 1925
Following World War I, Americans experienced 80 percent inflation between 1916 and 1920. From 1921 to 1924, prices went down 25 percent before slowly increasing 2 percent by 1925. I offer this information to give perspective regarding gun prices at that time; They were much higher than the prices of rifles made 10 years prior. In 1925, our Marlin Model 1893 retailed for $32.50. A Remington Model 8 would have been $60.50, while the Model 14 was $40.50. The Savage 1899 was $43.50, while the Winchester Model 54 was $49.50 and Model 1907 started at $50.40. Putting these prices in perspective, the average salary in 1925 was only $3,000 per year and the average work week about 50 hours. People didn’t get a lot of vacation either, so the average wage was $1.15 a day.
If I were in the market for one of these guns in 1925, as a typical hourly factory worker, and knew anything at all about the performance characteristics of some of these guns, I’d have a hard time looking past the 1893 Marlin or it’s competing Winchester Model 1894. I would also give a serious look at the Model 14 Remington and Savage 1899. I’d have a harder time justifying the Winchester 1907 from a cost and performance standpoint or the Model 8 Remington simply due to its cost. I would not eagerly buy a Winchester Model 54, either. It was a new and unknown bolt-­action and would have had to convince me of its advantages for the price.
That’s my take. On price alone, it’s easy to see why lever-­actions remained popular.
Parting Shots
Handloading for these rifles was fun, and dies are still available. Except for the Winchester Model 1907, I would recommend any of these rifles that is in good condition to anyone for recreational shooting and hunting. Generally, there was little difference in the performance of the other five rifles. The Remington Model 8 certainly needs an optic or a finer, more precise front sight to put it on par for accuracy. An interesting thing about both Remington rifles is that a shooter can use pointed bullets in both rifles because of the box magazine in the Model 8 and the fluted magazine tube on the Model 14. You might have to shorten the case a bit, but the potential is there.
The Marlin 1893 and Savage 1899/99 are well known rifles that still offer good hunting performance. The Winchester Model 54 is, as you would expect, the father of many bolt-­actions offered today — including the Winchester Model 70 — so it is familiar to use and chambers powerful, effective cartridges.

I would encourage any of you that have an interest in these older guns to pick up a 100-­year-­old hunting rifle if you can find one at a reasonable price. Taking game with any of these classics is highly satisfying, too. 

Dave Emary
