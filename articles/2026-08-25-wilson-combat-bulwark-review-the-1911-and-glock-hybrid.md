Wilson Combat Bulwark Review: The 1911 and Glock Hybrid?

The Best of Both Worlds and Then Some

 Wilson Combat’s newest pistol has a cutting-edge design that delivers for shooters. (Keith Wood photo)

August 25, 2026

By Keith Wood

It is not often that we see something that is truly new in the handgun world. Save for a few examples, most “new” handguns are evolutions of previous designs. The new Bulwark from Wilson Combat is not one of those guns. This all-new service pistol is not an evolution of anything but rather a completely fresh look at the combat handgun, incorporating some of the more attractive elements of proven pistols.
Two of the most successful handguns of all time are the 1911 and the Glock 19. The 
1911 is revered for its shootability
 thanks to its trigger and grip angle. The 
Glock is desirable thanks to its “just right” size, reliability and capacity
. Both handguns have innovative safety features. What if a design team like the one at Wilson Combat sat down and combined the most desirable elements of both guns? Well, they did—and landed on the Bulwark.
Appearance-wise, the Bulwark is a serious departure from anything Wilson Combat has built in the past. There are none of the graceful lines of the 1911 or Browning Hi Power. This pistol’s appearance is harsh and functional. It reminds me of the first time I laid eyes on a Glock.
The gripping surfaces of the machined aluminum grip module feature Wilson’s Brick pattern, which provides plenty of purchase. (Keith Wood photo)
Handgun Features
At its core, the Bulwark is a hammer-fired single-action 9mm with a steel chassis and a black anodized aluminum grip frame. The machined steel chassis is the serialized component, which means grip modules can be traded out without legal consequences, though doing so requires some serious skill in aligning the fire-control parts for reassembly. So that will probably be a gunsmith-only proposition.

The frame rails are part of the chassis so, regardless of the grip module’s material, the slide is riding on steel. Those frame rails are made with cuts to allow the pistol to continue to run even when dirty.
The grip module is machined from aluminum, so it’s lighter than an all-steel gun but more substantial than a polymer-frame pistol. The G-10 grip panels themselves are removable and secured with Torx screws.
The gripping surfaces of the module are machined with the company’s Brick pattern, which provides plenty of purchase. The upper portions of the grip, where the thumb and trigger finger need to slide during the draw stroke, are far smoother with just a hint of texture.

Part of the 1911’s magic is the 18-degree grip angle. The Bulwark’s angle comes close to that, which means it points naturally. The grip module has a generous beavertail at the rear and a light rail on the dustcover.
In addition to a trigger-tab safety, the pistol features a grip safety. The latter is easy to deactivate regardless of how you grip the gun. (Keith Wood photo)
Safeties
One of the most outwardly noticeable features of the Bulwark is the grip safety. The shape reminds me of the one used on the Springfield Armory XD series of handguns rather than anything you’d see on a 1911.
Given the current debate around “uncommanded discharges” and the popularity of appendix carry, Wilson Combat’s designers put a great deal of focus on the safety features of the Bulwark. The grip safety not only blocks the rearward movement of the trigger the same way it does on a 1911, it also blocks the sear as well as preventing the firing pin from moving unless it is disengaged.
This makes the Bulwark drop safe and is pretty ingenious when you pull the gun apart and see how it works. It is an innovative way to increase the safety of a single-action handgun without negatively affecting the trigger pull the way the Colt Series 80 system does.
I found the grip safety to be easier to disengage than any 1911 safety I’ve used, and I can’t envision a grip style that would prevent this gun from firing. One thing to note is that, even when the grip safety is engaged, it doesn’t fit completely flush with the backstrap of the grip module. I didn’t find this to be uncomfortable despite putting several hundred rounds through the gun.

1911 Trigger Style
The 1911’s trigger is the envy of nearly all other semiautomatic handguns. Not only is it capable of being short, light and crisp, it pulls straight to the rear rather than hinging from the top. The single rearward plane of the trigger’s movement seems to disrupt the hand less than other types.
The Bulwark maintains the 1911’s trigger style, and the characteristics of the trigger pull and break are similar, which is to say outstanding. I’ve never encountered a striker-fired handgun at any price point with a trigger this good. Under the hood, the Bulwark uses a sear and disconnector that are oversize versions of those found on the 1911 system.
My test sample’s trigger broke cleanly and consistently at 3.4 pounds after a well-defined wall. A safety tab inset into the trigger ensures the trigger cannot be moved to the rear unless the shooter’s finger is engaging the tab. This is about as safe as it gets. The reset was short and positive.
Slide Features
The Bulwark uses a frame-mounted slide stop and a reversible mag catch that is easily to manipulate without shifting your grip on the handgun. There is a slot in the back of the slide, just above the beavertail, that serves as a cocking indicator. The internal hammer is coated red, and when the hammer is cocked it is visible through the slot.
The Bulwark’s slide is machined from a billet of 416 stainless steel and given a black nitride treatment. There are generously cut front and rear angled cocking serrations and a flat top with longitudinally milled slots. A large engraved “Bulwark” graces the left flat.
The slide uses a separate breech block that is also nitrided to create a rock-hard surface that will stand up to significant wear. An oversize external extractor is pinned into the slide from beneath.
The Bulwark is optic-ready, and Wilson includes three sets of steel pins for optics on the DeltaPoint Pro, RMR and RMSc footprints. (Keith Wood photo)
Sights
There are front and rear sight dovetails cut into the slide to secure the red fiber-optic front and serrated U-notch rear. The Bulwark is optic-ready, of course, and uses a rigid direct-to-slide mounting system. Optics are fastened directly to the slide using two fasteners, and a set of vertical pins in color-coded bags is included to match the various footprints the design will accommodate. Those footprints include Trijicon RMR, Leupold DeltaPoint Pro and Shield RMSc. This setup is simple, durable and results in a low-mounted optic with no plates or adapters.
Barrel
The Bulwark uses a stainless steel barrel that is four inches long. The barrel is ramped with a fully supported chamber. A slot milled into the barrel hood serves as a loaded-chamber indicator. The crown sits flush with the face of the slide and tapers inward toward the bore.

The barrel itself is unremarkable, but its lock-up method is like nothing else I’ve ever seen. The bottom lug of the barrel locks into a steel locking block that is effectively an extension of the recoil spring guide assembly, which in turn is secured into the frame via the slide stop.
This locking system is one of the reasons that the Bulwark has an extremely low bore axis, which paid dividends when I got it to the range. The Bulwark uses a flat-wire recoil spring, which will give it an extremely long service life. The spring rides on a full-length stainless steel guide rod. As mentioned, the guide rod is secured to the locking block using a roll pin.
The Bulwark uses a unique locking block that is part of the recoil spring assembly. The slide stop retains the block in the frame. (Keith Wood photo)
Magazines
The Bulwark uses a proprietary magazine that is adapted from the mag used on the Wilson Combat EDC X9. These magazines have steel bodies with polymer base pads and high visibility orange followers. The magazines taper to a single column so, combined with the frame’s beveled magazine well, reloads should be trouble-free. Ten- and 15-round magazines are currently available, and extended capacity versions are in the works.
Handling and Performance
I’m going to be honest: I didn’t love the Bulwark’s looks at first. Then I picked it up. To handle this gun is to love it, and to shoot it is something special indeed. There’s just something unquantifiably “right” about the way the Bulwark fits into the hand.
Recoil is very subjective and difficult to quantify without sophisticated equipment, but the Bulwark is one of the softest shooting 9mm handguns I’m familiar with. Even though it is a more or less compact design, it feels as though you are shooting a full-size, compensated handgun.

This is a combination of a few factors. For starters, the Bulwark weighs 41.6 ounces unloaded. Beyond sheer mass, the low bore axis and optimal grip angle really help the shooter control muzzle rise with minimal effort. Even out to 30 or more yards, I could put hits on an A-zone-size steel target nearly as fast as I could pull the trigger.
Bench accuracy ranged from excellent with Bill Wilson’s Signature 155-grain target load to average with the full-metal-jacket loads I used.
Unlike other Wilson Combat handguns that are hand-fitted by its gunsmiths, the Bulwark was designed to be a production handgun with interchangeable components. When receiving pre-production samples of a new firearm, it is not uncommon for a few bugs to show up along the way.
I ran into a problem a handful of times where the trigger was not resetting properly. Wilson asked me to return the gun for examination, and the team discovered that the trigger return springs needed a bit more tension. That will be addressed and should not be an issue on production guns.
Most holsters designed for the Glock 19 will be compatible with the Bulwark. I used an outside-the-waistband holster from BlackPoint Tactical, and both fit and retention were excellent. Drawing from the holster was when I really came to appreciate how well the Bulwark pointed for me. I’ve owned a pile of Glocks and have put many tens of thousands of rounds through them, but none of them can touch the Bulwark in terms of finding the target quickly.

Versatile Handgun
So what niche does the Bulwark fill? Based on its size, capacity and capability, that question has a broad answer. It is certainly a great choice for everyday carry, especially appendix carry where those extra safety features would provide peace of mind. This would be an ideal duty pistol for those with the means, and I can see it becoming dominant in IDPA-style competition. Basically, the Bulwark is at home anywhere that a serious midsize 9mm handgun will do the job.
Some may balk at the Bulwark’s $1,900 price tag, but it’s important to keep that in perspective. This handgun is built from high-quality components machined from forgings and billets. Comparing such a handgun to one made using MIM, cast and polymer parts is completely unfair. Just because it looks like a mass-production striker-fired gun doesn’t mean it is one.
Final Thoughts
Wilson Combat has come a long way from its humble roots in the back of a jewelry store. With the introduction of the Bulwark, the company has evolved into serious firearm manufacturer. The Bulwark is an audacious service pistol built from the ground up with both safety and shootability in mind. This handgun is unique and well thought out, and its on-range performance was excellent. This is a gun built by real shooters, for real shooting.
