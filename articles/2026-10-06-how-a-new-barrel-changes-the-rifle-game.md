How A New Barrel Changes the Rifle Game

Tested on Proof Research's new Elevation MTR FDX Rifle, the PXT barrel is a force multiplier.

October 06, 2026

By Tom Beckstrand

I’ve never had a rifle shoot this accurately across three different test loads. The points of impact for each didn’t move that much, which was also unusual. All three groups, if superimposed, would have yielded nine-­shots in an approximately .6-­inch group. Those were my first thoughts when I quickly examined my disintegrating target in a torrential downpour.
(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The test rifle was 
Proof Research’s new Elevation MTR FDX.
 The rifle features a new receiver in one of the brand’s most popular stocks. While the rifle is new and interesting, it’s the rifling inside the also-new barrel that steals the show. It’s called “Proof Exponential Twist,” or “PXT.” While the technology isn’t new to medium- and large-caliber barrels, it is absolutely new to small-­caliber munitions, meaning anything .50-­caliber or less.
The target may be wet, but the groups spoke for themselves. Combined, they averaged barely more than half an inch. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Faster & Faster
Proof Research started as a barrel-­making company in 2012, primarily focused on carbon-­fiber-­wrapped stainless-­steel barrels. It quickly grew into a large barrel-­making company that also built a small number of rifles annually. Business increased rapidly to where, in 2018, Proof Research began developing military solutions for bore diameters greater than .50 caliber and smaller than 75mm (about 3 inches).
Medium-­caliber munitions often make use of gain twist technology, far more advanced than what we find in small-­caliber cartridges. The projectiles can weigh a few pounds, and taking an object of that weight and in milli-­second trying to spin it at a few hundred thousand revolutions per minute (RPM) yields poor results. Such projectiles rely on narrow driving bands that engages with the rifling grooves, and spinning them too fast too quickly just strips the driving band off the projectile.

After a few years of working with aggressive gain-­twist in medium-­caliber barrels, Proof Research took what it learned and considered what would happen if it applied their new superpowers to small-­arms calibers. The process began in 2023 with barrels being selectively fielded in 2024. Proof Research has a few employees who are serious precision rifle competitors, so it put gain-­twist barrels to work. It also sent barrels to industry partners to do independent testing. One of those partners was 
Hornady
.
Proof Research’s internal tests during a few years taught them that there are significant gains to be had with aggressive gain-­twist use. PXT starts out with the rifling almost running straight down the bore. If you calculated it with the traditional method, it would probably be around one twist in about 400 inches of barrel. As the bullet moves down the bore, the twist rate gets tighter and tighter until the last few inches of the barrel, where it is the 1-in-7-, or -8-, or -9-­inch twist that we’re used to seeing. The goal is to make life as easy as possible on the bullet and the bore as the projectile engages the rifling. Once happily on its way, the bullet starts spinning faster as the rifling twist rate increases.
A carbon-fiber bedding block is used to secure the action. It minimizes potential movement of the action, enhancing the accuracy potential in combination with the gain-twist rifling of the barrel. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Tough Tooling
The creation of such rifling in a small rifle bore is difficult. Proof Research invested heavily in the machines capable of making such cuts and then worked through the issues in programming to impart the rifling. What it learned in a few years of testing was that, in addition to easing the bullet into its rotational speed, it was also helpful to reduce the rifling’s sharp edges. Looking at a cross-­section of the bore would reveal rifling that looks like hills and valleys instead of the sharp angles we’re used to seeing.

The advantages of PXT rifling are substantial. The biggest advantage — and the one for which I’ve seen the most evidence — is prolonged barrel life. Barrels die when the rifling closest to the chamber erodes, hence a smooth first few inches of the bore. A bullet traveling too far before it engages rifling (since it is getting pushed from behind) has a good chance of engraving in the rifling when not perfectly aligned, resulting in poor accuracy. Gas also escapes around the sides of the projectile, so velocity drops along with accuracy.
A rifle of this character naturally pairs will with a lightweight suppressor. The muzzle is threaded, and Proof Research includes a thread protector with the Elevation MTR FDX. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The reason the rifling close to the chamber erodes is the bullet goes from no rotation when the round is chambered to a few hundred thousand RPMs almost instantly. The rifling closest to the chamber has to endure all of that shear force every time a cartridge fires, as well as the heat and pressure. PXT moves the transition from no bullet rotation to full speed later down the barrel, preserving the throat, the rifling closest to the chamber. Testing from numerous sources indicated barrel life was increased by between 30 and 100 ­percent. For example, a 6mm Creedmoor shot in rifle matches may have the throat that erodes by .1 ­inch in 1,000 rounds. PXT rifling in the same scenario moved .01-inch in more than 1,200 rounds.
The Package
The rifle sent to Guns & Ammo was the Elevation MTR FDX, available exclusively with PXT rifling. The barrel and action combination was used by Proof Research in the 2 years of testing with high-­pressure ammunition, including 6.5mm Creedmoor +Peak currently available from Federal Premium. PXT rifling was an ideal match for use with high-­pressure ammunition even though the benefits of the rifling apply equally to all types of ammunition.
Considered an upgrade to any bolt-action rifle is a TriggerTech Field trigger. It is adjustable from 2 to 5 pounds, and it’s favored for its smooth pull and reliability. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The receiver was made by 
Falkor Defense
 for Proof Research from the LW7 receiver. The Model 700-­footprint receiver features an integral recoil lug and bolt with a removeable head. The removeable head contains the extractor and the ejector, two of the most problematic parts on any bolt-­action rifle. What I love about bolts of this type is the ease with which they can be maintained. If I planned an expensive hunt that involved travel far from home, I would always leave home with a spare bolt head. If a problem arises in the field, a few minutes is all that’s required to replace the bolt head with the new extractor and ejector already installed. The easily removed bolt head also allows the shooter to change from standard .473-­inch case head cartridges to the .532-­inch case head of the short magnum rounds. Since the receiver accepts pre-­fit barrels, all that’s needed to make the swap is a vise and some hand tools. There’s no need to send anything to a gunsmith.
Combined with that excellent receiver is a Proof Research carbon-­fiber rifle stock. Proof places carbon fiber in the mold and then pressurizes an air bladder inside the mold to force the carbon fiber shell into place. The stock isn’t made from two halves that are molded together; it is one uniform piece of carbon fiber. Even more impressive than its rigidity is the way the carbon-fiber bedding block was incorporated to support the receiver.

Most carbon-fiber stocks use simple aluminum pillars and foam (or even an aluminum mini-­chassis) to support the receiver. Proof Research has a bedding block made from carbon fiber. It’s about a quarter-­inch thick, and it’s integral to the stock’s carbon-fiber shell. Aluminum pillars sit between the bedding block and bottom metal to ensure that distance is correct for reliable feeding. The advantage of the integral carbon-fiber bedding block is that it will never shift or work loose. Also, if the barrel sits straight in the barrel channel, that’s the way it’s going to stay until the stock is destroyed.
Comb height is fixed, not adjustable. However, it is elevated to better locate the cheek and eyes behind the scope objective. Sling-swivel attachment points are on the sides and underneath the toe. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Astounding Accuracy
I referenced the accuracy potenetial I witnessed during testing. I’m fortunate that I get to see and shoot a lot of rifles, and some of them are incredibly accurate. While I’ve seen other barrels with conventional rifling shoot groups this small, I’ve never seen any conventionally rifled barrel — carbon fiber-­wrapped or steel — shoot all three test loads into groups this small. I think that’s going to be the big take­away concerning accuracy and PXT rifling. Anyone purchasing this barrel should expect the same accuracy performance as what’s currently found in a high-­end steel match barrel, but I’m betting the PXT barrel won’t be as picky about what load it likes.
Atop the receiver, sections of rail are secured with screws for mounting optics. Unusual for many modern actions, the receiver port is open nearly 180 degress for maximum clearance. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
What I saw at the range was impressive by any measure. I’d taken this rifle to the range previously just because I wanted to shoot it and because I had some new 6.5mm Creedmoor +Peak ammunition to test. I cleaned the barrel when it arrived and then put about 40 rounds through it before looking at accuracy results. The first round of three-­shot groups (because this is a hunting rifle) yielded a .18-­inch group from Federal, a .28-­inch group from SAKO, and a .48-­inch group from Hornady. Later rounds of testing would shrink the Hornady best three-­shot group to .35 ­inch.
All three groups from the initial rounds landed at the same point of impact, also a first for me. Most of the time groups move anywhere from half- to 1-­inch when the load and manufacturer changes. For this test, I had three different bullet weights — all match bullets with thin copper jackets — land in the same spot. It makes me wonder if the trauma from conventional rifling is more responsible for point of impact shifts than changing ammunition manufacturers!
The forend offers multiple attachment methods for bipods or other accessories. There is a four-slot rail at the front-most portion, followed by a QD slot, and a section of Arca rail. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Like opening Pandora’s Box, it’s hard to say what’ll happen next in the rifleman’s world. The increase in accuracy will be appreciated, but it’s hard for most of us to prove. PXT rifling certainly offers an increase in barrel life. The potential for more so-called “barrel burning” cartridges is a feasible future. I suggest that a 120-­grain Hornady A-­Tip be loaded for the 6mm Creedmoor at 3,100 feet per second (fps). Let’s see if the PXT barrel lasts 3,000 rounds. Hornady and Proof, if you’re reading, I volunteer as tribute! 

Tom Beckstrand
