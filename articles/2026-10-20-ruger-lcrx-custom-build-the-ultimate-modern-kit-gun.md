Ruger LCRx Custom Build: The Ultimate Modern Kit Gun

Tailoring a lightweight wheelgun for wilderness carry and backwoods reliability

 The resin grip is highly effective and grippy. (Mitch Eckler photo)

October 20, 2026

By Mitch Eckler

Some shooters will always see revolvers as backup guns, hunting tools, or relics that occasionally get dragged into the modern world. Put an optic and an unusual grip on one, and they might dismiss the whole thing as a spectacle. That is too bad. They are missing out on some seriously enjoyable shooting.
I spotted 
the 3-inch Ruger LCRx in .22 LR
 while cruising Ruger’s website for projects. Eight shot capacity, an exposed hammer, adjustable sights, and a lightweight frame sounded like an excellent starting point. Once I began talking with the people making accessories for these little revolvers, the project took shape. I wanted to build my ultimate kit gun.
An Old Idea, Updated
A kit gun is a small, portable handgun carried as part of an outdoorsman’s equipment. Traditionally, that often meant a compact .22 revolver for small game, pests, camp chores, and recreational shooting. Smith & Wesson gets the credit for popularizing the name, but the idea belongs to anyone who appreciates a useful handgun that is easy to bring along.
The kit gun is more than just looks. (Mitch Eckler photo)
Ruger’s LCR, or Lightweight Compact Revolver, arrived in January 2009. The original five-shot .38 Special +P combined a polymer fire-control housing, aluminum frame, and heavily fluted stainless-steel cylinder in a 13.5-ounce package. Its friction-reducing cam system helped deliver a smooth double-action pull. Ruger had developed a platform with room to grow, and the exposed-hammer LCRx eventually added single-action capability.

In April 2017, Ruger introduced the eight-shot, 3-inch LCRx in .22 LR. Its aluminum frame and polymer fire-control housing keep weight manageable, while the stainless-steel barrel and black PVD-finished stainless cylinder suit a working outdoor revolver. I appreciate weather-resistant materials on something intended to accompany me beyond the range.
The LCR family also permits dry firing without damage to the firing pin or internal components, according to Ruger’s manual. I mention that frequently because it is particularly useful in a rimfire revolver. Practicing the trigger pull without burning ammunition makes getting acquainted with this gun easier.
The optics plate mounts to the rear sight's dovetail. (Mitch Eckler photo)
A Dot and a Clever Mount
My optic choice was the Cyelee CAT0, an uncomplicated open-emitter red dot using the RMSc footprint. It runs on a CR1632 battery, with advertised battery life reaching 50,000 hours, and weighs about an ounce. I have used the CAT0 on several guns and have been pleased with its performance and it’s $150 price tag. For this project, modest weight and dependable operation were more appealing than a long list of features.

The mount deserves its own applause. Matt at Checkerboard Creek Customs makes the Merlon LCRx mount, a solution for a revolver that does not enjoy the same accessory selection as a typical optics-ready pistol. The LCRx’s small adjustable rear sight limits the usefulness of mounts intended for other Ruger revolvers.
The Merlon uses the existing rear-sight attachment locations, including the threaded elevation-screw hole and cross-pin holes. On my gun, installation was straightforward and required no permanent modification. The finished arrangement held zero throughout my shooting. I love finding small manufacturers solving specific problems like this. Their work makes projects possible that would otherwise stall at the parts-search stage.
I also added a HiViz fiber-optic front sight. Its job is to provide a bright visual reference as I orient the revolver and bring it toward eye level. With ambient light reaching the fiber, it catches my eye readily. It does not generate its own light in darkness, but I appreciate the reference it provides. It also looks awfully good out front.
A fiber optic front sight. (Mitch Eckler photo)
A Grip From Another Universe
The Neon Forest Industries grip might suggest that I am preparing to hunt replicants in the Blade Runner universe. Pick it up, however, and the appeal becomes practical. This resin grip is comfortable, feels warm in the hand, and remained surprisingly secure with wet hands during my use.
The enlarged upper portion gives my thumb a comfortable resting place. On this mild little .22, the shape helps the revolver settle into my hand and stay there. I would evaluate that thumb position differently on a harder-recoiling gun, but here it feels natural.

Neon Forest offers its LCR/LCRx grip in multiple colors and configurations for right- and left-handed shooters. Southpaws get to join the fun. The appearance will not suit everyone, but I love it and the comfort won me over. A useful woods gun can have some personality, too.
Range Time
The Ruger gave me no functioning issues to report. Once dialed, my accuracy testing took place at 50 yards, where hits on my 8-inch Caldwell plate were easy and repeatable in double action. The dot was simple to zero, and the combination encouraged me to keep shooting.
The trigger weights were substantial. Double action measured just under 12 pounds, while single action broke at approximately 5 pounds. Reliable rimfire ignition places demands on the firing mechanism, and a firm double-action pull is familiar territory with small .22 revolvers. Fortunately, weight tells only part of the story. This pull was smooth, and the single-action break was crisp and clean.
With my hands, the small revolver encourages a deeper placement of the trigger finger than I use on many larger handguns. That feels familiar from other compact double-action revolvers, although it will not suit every shooter or necessarily produce everyone’s best accuracy. The important part of my experience was being able to manage the pull consistently.
Having both SA and DA operating modes adds considerably; it’s just so much fun. I can work through a cylinder in double action or cock the hammer and take my time over an individual shot. I would enjoy less single-action overtravel, but the complete system feels well considered. For now, I am leaving the factory springs alone. But it’s worth noting that Wolff does produce a kit for these.
The LCRx worked flawlessly with all ammo. (Mitch Eckler photo)
Punch and Practical
The ammunition I tried generally delivered pleasing accuracy and extraction. Federal Punch was my favorite. Its 29-grain projectile averaged 1,256.4 fps across a 10-shot chronograph string from this 3-inch LCRx. That calculates to approximately 102 foot-pounds of muzzle energy. The nickel-plated cases also extracted smoothly, making the ammunition pleasant to use throughout the test.
Although .22 LR is capable of causing lethal injury, my interest in this project centers on outdoor utility and recreational shooting. Punch impressed me through its performance in this particular revolver, without requiring the gun to become something beyond the kit-gun role I envisioned.
To round out the package, I added a 5 Star Firearms speedloader and tabletop loading block. The machined-aluminum equipment makes it convenient to load the revolver and replenish a speedloader with another eight rounds. Custom colors are an option, although I like the bright aluminum appearance.

Advertisement

Tiny .22 cartridges can be fiddly, but the loader aligned all eight rounds nicely. Some wax-coated ammunition needed a final nudge to seat completely. The Federal Punch cartridges dropped in more freely. The PVD finish on the cylinder itself helps aid lubricity on the cylinder walls too; but, checking that the cartridges are fully seated remains worthwhile, even when everything seems to have fallen into place.
Final Cylinder
The LCRx with the red dot proved to be highly accurate at 50 yards. (Mitch Eckler photo)
Unloaded and configured with the grip, mount, optic, and front sight, this revolver weighs 1 pound, 3.2 ounces. That is 19.2 ounces for a complete little package that is comfortable to shoot and easy to bring along.
The kit gun is an old concept with plenty of room for individual interpretation. Modern materials and inventive accessory makers give us more ways to explore it. My Ruger delivers the portability and enjoyment I wanted, with enough personality to make me smile every time I pick it up. I absolutely adore this little thing, and I cannot wait to pack it for my next 
camping
 trip with my boys.
Shoot safe.
