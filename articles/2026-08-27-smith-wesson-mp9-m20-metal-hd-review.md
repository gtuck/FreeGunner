Smith & Wesson M&P9 M2.0 Metal HD Review

With a Stainless-Steel Frame, the M&P9 2.0 is a Heavy-Duty Pistol Ready for the Range or Competition

 Smith & Wesson introduces an all-steel version of its flagship pistol, the M&P 9 M2.0 Metal HD (Frank Melloni photo)

August 27, 2026

By Frank Melloni

A heavy handgun is a joy to shoot, and that’s why there’s still a place for a pistol with a steel frame and slide. Smith & Wesson understands this concept, but oddly, while the company did produce an 
aluminum-frame version of its striker-fired M&P pistol 
a while back, the company has been nearly absent on the steel-frame front. That put it behind competitors like Walther and Canik when it comes to making hefty versions of their most popular blasters. The recent release of the M&P9 M2.0 Metal HD has addressed this.
S&W went out of its way to ensure the stainless-steel frame on this new gun is as heavy as possible. The listed weight is 34.9 ounces for the standard model, but once I added a magazine of its full complement of 17+1 rounds—124-grain ammo, in this case—and topped it with an optic, my sample tipped the scale at 46.8 ounces.
There are several versions of the Metal HD. The Competition model is actually 0.4 ounce lighter than the standard model thanks to a spiral-fluted barrel and lightening cuts in the slide. The Competition model also sports a magazine funnel as well as an extended magazine release and enhanced frame texture. It is offered with or without a thumb safety, and as a state-compliant 10-round version with or without the manual safety.
The magazine release is reversible, while the slide stop is ambidextrous. Melloni found the controls pronounced without being obtrusive. The gun is also available with a thumb safety. (Frank Melloni photo)
Features
The standard Metal HD is available with or without a thumb safety, and both 10- and 15-round models are designed for restrictive states. The 10-rounder comes with a thumb safety; the 15-rounder doesn’t offer that safety as an option.

I don’t make as many matches as I used to, and since I wanted to get the full effect of a heavy steel-frame gun, I decided to go with the standard Metal HD. And if I did decide to shoot a match with this pistol, not having the mag funnel that comes on the Competition model would allow me to enter USPSA Production or Carry Optics divisions.
The Metal HD’s frame is built for shooters who like to put a lot of rounds downrange. It’s designed with small areas of checkered dimples on the sides and what I would describe as a mild version of skate-tape texturing on the front- and backstraps. The latter are customizable, with a total of four included in the box: small, medium, medium/large and large. This is one more than most manufacturers include, giving shooters a more versatile fit to suit their hands.
As I mentioned, S&W offers this pistol with or without a thumb safety. Either choice still leaves you with the trigger-lever and striker-block safety, making it safe and secure whether you opt for the manual safety or not.

The trigger combines the best features of a curved finger lever with a straight one, helping to produce a straight-back press, which is why it’s popular on newer guns. (Frank Melloni photo)
While we’re on the trigger, the HD’s is simply a work of art. Smith has managed to blend the best features of a curved trigger finger lever with a straight one, creating a bang switch that naturally centers your finger and ensures straight-back travel. An overtravel stop placed into the frame increases its practical accuracy and helps with faster follow-up shots. With a measured break of less than 4.5 pounds, accurate rapid fire is a breeze.
Like most pistols today, the M&P Metal HD’s magazine catch is reversible, accommodating southpaws or those who like to use their trigger finger to operate the release.
The slide stop is ambidextrous, which does more than merely accommodate both lefties and righties. Having a connected control on each side allows for straight downward pressure, instead of a more lateral motion, which can cause binding. Being able to actuate it with either finger is a plus, too.
Since this gun isn’t intended for deep concealment, these controls are somewhat prominent, without being gaudy or cumbersome. The same goes for the takedown lever, although it is tucked away a bit more.
The CORE optics system includes plates to mount nearly any red dot on the market, making for a versatile setup. (Frank Melloni photo)
Sights
The steel frame supports a steel slide with many of today’s most popular features, beginning with the Competition Optics Ready Equipment (CORE) slide cut. The gun comes with seven adapter plates to accommodate nearly any red dot on the market. Smith & Wesson even includes a wide variety of screws to get the job done.

For those who aren’t interested in adding electronics, a brilliant Night Fision tritium front sight is furnished. It’s a day/night sight built from steel that’s coated in black nitride, and it incorporates a tritium vial surrounded by a bright yellow/green ring. Night Fision employs an injection-molded sleeve that secures the tritium vial, which has a domed lens the company says collimates the tritium light—making the rays parallel and preventing spreading for a sharper sight picture.
The 
front sight is accompanied by a serrated U-notch rear
. Both sights are drift-adjustable, and they’re installed in dovetails and can be replaced if you need or want to.
Cocking serrations both fore and aft give users more options to get the gun into battery or clear a malfunction. Overall, I found the frame-to-slide fit to be exceptionally tight, which goes a long way toward reliability. This is also aided by the widened ejection port and the large external extractor.
Performance
For testing I decided to mount a Burris FastFire 4 red dot, which includes an optional weather shield, letting you close up the emitter for rainy matches. As advertised, everything needed to mount it on the slide was in the S&W box, so the process took only a few minutes.
There’s no reason to limit your ammunition choices for this or any other M&P 9, as it’s designed to eat virtually anything. However, the Metal HD was designed with extensive range use in mind, so I decided on a couple of full-metal-jacket rounds along with the Liberty frangible load, which is designed to be safer on steel targets.
I began my testing by zeroing the optic and getting a baseline for the gun’s accuracy. While it ran everything without issue, it certainly was picky about what it would group the tightest. Overall results were good, but those looking for top consistency should try a few different types of ammo before stocking up. This is also a great argument for handloading; tweaking loads will pay dividends.
Bench shooting over, I couldn’t wait to step away and start working through some appropriate drills. Beginning with some simple one-shot routines from the holster, I drew and fired a single center-mass round for an entire magazine.
Throughout this process, I realized that my finger didn’t quite land on the trigger the way I would have liked, so I swapped out the backstrap for the smaller size. This is a toolless process, putting me back on the firing line in moments. Properly adjusted, I was able to land a shot in the A zone in as little as 1.12 seconds, which is pretty good for a gun I’m new to—and after a period when I wasn’t able to practice much.

While singles out of the holster helped show me how the gun points, doubles showed how well it recovers, and this is what the HD was built for. Running through an array of steel gongs and IPSC targets, I was able to get my splits down to the teens with next to no effort. It was as if the muzzle was anchored to the ground, requiring minimal grip tension to keep my hands in place or the front sight on target. Before I knew it, I had exhausted my ammo stash, and I will gladly do it again.
Final Thoughts
I think the HD will do a lot for the S&W brand, as it rounds out the line with a gun the company didn’t have but competitors did. Sadly, though, steel frames seldom attract new shooters, as they have not yet made the correlation between pistol weight and recoil reduction. Newcomers tend find these guns too heavy, but once someone has been to the range a few times with a light polymer gun and then has a chance to test-fire an all-steel gun, they are instantly sold.
To that end, if you’ve not had the chance to shoot an M&P 9 M2.0 Metal HD, see if you can find a range in your area that participates in a Smith &Wesson range program, then give this gun a go.
