HK VP9CC Micro Compact 9mm Review

HK introduced the VP9CC for 2026. Is it a better choice versus the CC9? Here's a full review.

August 29, 2026

By Eric R. Poole

A few months after first appearing on the September 2014 issue, the HK VP9 received the Guns & Ammo 2014 Handgun of the Year award. The successful design first appeared with a 15-­ or 17-­round magazine, and an optional optic-­ready cut. The lineup continuously expanded to include the subcompact VP9SK, the compact VP9A1 K, VP9A1 F, VP9A1 L, VP9 Match, VP9A1 K Tactical, VP9A1 X Tactical, VP9A1 X, VP9A1 K, VP40, and the VP9CC introduced here for 2026.
HK sent G&A a sample of 
the VP9CC
 with a 
Vortex enclosed reflex sight
 mounted, and an 
HK-­branded G-­Code Prime Carry inside-­the-­waistband (IWB) holster
 and spare magazine carrier. It arrived on March 4, 2026, and was subject to an non-­disclosure agreement until April 7. I took it to the range to verify its function and the optic’s zero, and then began carrying it. That first range experience was positive, highlighting the low-­cut positioning of the optic and the magazine release.
(Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The Vortex optic has a large window for its size, which is 1.195-­inches wide by 1.072-­inches tall by 1.584-­inches long. The battery cap slightly widens the profile of the VP9CC to 1.1 inches. The sight is the enclosed Defender-­CCW, which includes a top-­mounted solar panel and has a manufacturer's suggested retail price (MSRP) of $400. That’s a $50 savings given the VP9CC sans optic retails for $1,049; the Vortex sight adds $350.
Pressing both “+” and “-­” buttons on the left side cycles the reticle between a 3 minute-­of-­angle (MOA) dot, a 32-­MOA circle, and a circle-­dot combination of the two. Pressing each button individually changes the reticle between 10 intensity settings. Peering through the windows, the 3-­MOA reticle is quick and easy to employ. With the optic off, the metallic sights are also easy to acquire and align. Unlike a lot of iron sights that only align at the very bottom of an optic’s window frame, the VP9CC with Vortex allows the XS Sights to align clearly in the window’s lower third. The front sight includes a tritium dot surrounded by a green ring. The rear sight is shaped like a frustum with a squared notch and serrations.

The HK VP9CC is better optimized for red-­dot carry versus the American-­made CC9. The included Vortex Defender CCW red dot sits low on the slide and does not impede use of its irons. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The second “first impression” was working the paddle-­type bilateral magazine release lever. It’s shaped to blend with the triggerguard contour, offering some protection against unintended activation. I’ve carried several European-­made pistols with this style of magazine release and I’m comfortable using it. However, if this type of mag release is new to you, I suggest training with the VP9CC to establish proficiency with reloading it before depending on it as an everyday carry (EDC) pistol. There are several ways to use a paddle magazine release. If your thumb is long enough, you can sweep it down without disturbing your grip. Some prefer to use the trigger finger by pulling it back and pressing down with the tip. Others will index an idle trigger finger against the frame and use the tip of the middle finger to press the lever down. I have also seen several shooters use both the thumb and either the middle or trigger finger for leverage and pinch and press both sides of the magazine release simultaneously. HK equips many handguns with a paddle mag release lever, but if you prefer a subcompact model with a push-­button-­type allow me to 
recommend the CC9
, 
VP9-­B
 or 
California-­compliant VP9SK
.
The VP9CC vs. the CC9
I’ve also reviewed, carried and own the HK CC9, which was introduced in G&A’s January 2025 issue. The CC9 and VP9CC are nearly twin striker-­fire pistols, dimensionally, but they do not share holsters, magazines, sights, or optic plates. The triggerguard on the CC9 is larger and the trigger is longer. The front of the CC9 triggerguard is raked farther forward, which increases space within the triggerguard but reduces the accessory rail length to just two slots. The triggerguard is squared off on the VP9CC and the rail has a third slot, accommodating a few additional light and laser options.
The front and rear sights are pressed into a proprietary dovetail cut, although these are cross-­compatible with other VP9 pistols and the P30. Front slide serrations are the same style as the VP9. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The VP9CC has a longer bilateral slide-­release lever than the CC9. If the function type of this lever matters to you, the CC9 is serrated and angled to function as a “slide lock” lever while the VP9CC lever is serrated at the top and angled to function as a “slide release” lever.

There is no manual safety lever on either model, but there is a centered safety lever on both triggers.
The VP9CC continues many of the aesthetics of the VP9 series, including the front-­and-­rear slide serrations and cocking aids at the rear of the slide. Both the CC9 and VP9CC offer rails under the dustcovers to accept a small light, and both have ergonomic grip modules. The CC9 grip texture is more tactile and functional than the molded texture on the VP9CC, but the most notable difference is with the grips’ backstraps. The VP9CC offers six backstrap options including small, medium and large, and each size with or without a beavertail extension. If you’re familiar with the VP9 series, the VP9CC noticeably lacks the interchangeable left and right grip panels. In this way, the VP9CC and CC9 are the same.
The HK VP9CC offer an additional rail slot on the dust cover versus the CC9, which only has two slots for mounting a light or laser sight. The flat front of the triggerguard is also serrated. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
In terms of capacity, both the CC9 and VP9CC offer a 10-­round flush magazine, 10-­round extended, and a 12-­round magazine option with polymer sleeve over the basepad. Though two magazines are provided with each pistol, spare magazines are available for $50 each.
Externally, fit and finish meet the same level of quality for both the CC9 and VP9CC. With slide and barrel assemblies removed from the frame, it is obvious that both pistols are based on striker-­fire mechanics but developed by different engineering teams. Extractors, ejectors, sears, and striker safety plungers are all in the same locations but are unique to the model. The two, progressive-­rate, captured recoil springs surround the two-­stage guiderods, but the springs and guiderod components are different parts and do not interchange.
Six backstraps are included with the VP9CC. There are small, medium and large options, with and without a beavertail extension. The non-­abrasive grip texture matches the frame. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Both guns feature a chassis system, which could be removed from the polymer grip module, but I advise against removing them unless you’re an armorer or gunsmith. The CC9 chassis is hooked by a lug through a slot at the back of the grip’s beavertail and pinned at the front. The slot also functions as a window to read the chassis’ serial number. The VP9CC also engages the rear of the grip module and is pinned at the front above the trigger. It’s serial number and Ulm proofhouse marks are visible through the slot above the left side of the grip. Additionally, the same serial number and German proofmarks appear on the barrel and below the ejection port on the right side of the slide.

What’s particularly interesting to me is that the CC9 features two half-­inch contact points on each side at the front and rear. The VP9CC chassis has a continuous rail on each side to engage the slide. Both pistols have been flawlessly reliable, but I’ve only shot a couple thousand rounds through them so far. It will be an interesting study to watch how these rails wear over time or function when subject to carbon build-­up and debris.
At The Range
The trigger weight of the VP9CC averaged 5 pounds, 10 ounces, for 10 measurements with a digital Lyman gauge. The CC9 trigger tested at 5 pounds, 9 ounces. It’s difficult to discern between trigger weights, but they do exhibit a different feel during travel and reset.
The progressive-­rate, captured recoil springs and guiderod are unique to the VP9CC and supported by a groove under the barrel. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
Accuracy potential — with and without an optic attached — was similar. My average 25-­yard group from a benchrest using the pistols with a red dot measured near 1.4 inches. With iron sights only, my average increased to 1.75 inches with all variety of grain weights and defensive loads.
As of this writing, HK’s webstore offers 11 holster options and three magazine carriers for the CC9, including many in the $50 to $70 range from ANR Design, Cipher, DeSantis and G-­Code. There are only four holsters and one mag carrier for the VP9CC from G-­Code and Radar, which currently range $100 to $200.
What to Prioritize
The dilemma for me is that I already own a CC9. When I’m not evaluating another handgun, it’s often the pistol I choose to carry because it’s comfortable, accurate and dependable. However, I prefer to carry it without an optic. I must re-­learn the presentation from a draw when an optic is attached, so I carry it without. I’m not as precise at distance with the iron-­sighted CC9 but it’s intuitive to draw, present and operate. When I want to show off at the range or smack 50-­ to 100-­yard steel targets, I prefer the VP9CC because the optic sits lower in the slide cut and doesn’t impede my use of the XS Sights, which are awesome equipment. If you intend to carry one of these HKs with an optic, I’d recommend the VP9CC.
The HK-­branded G-­Code Prime Carry was sent with the VP9CC for evaluation, as well as a spare mag holster. (Photo by Michael Anschuetz)
The VP9CC is similar dimensionally. In terms of performance, reliability and handling, it’s just different. For some, another dilemma is this: Do you prefer a push-­button magazine release or a paddle-­style magazine release lever? If you are indifferent, then perhaps the country of origin matters more. The CC9 is made in USA and the VP9CC is made in Germany.
For others, the decision comes down to price. HK has never been a “budget” brand, but if you’re determined to purchase one and money is your most important consideration, the U.S.-­made CC9 starts at $699, and optic-equipped options are reasonably priced. If you simply prefer the German approach, then the VP9CC without an optic starts at $1,049. The CC9 is more affordable, but if you were an early adopter of the VP9 then doesn’t it make sense to add the VP9CC to your collection?
