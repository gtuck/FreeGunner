By Jace Bauserman

A few years back, while roaming the wilds of Montana searching for elk, I bumped headfirst into a grizzly. It was a big bear, and at first, I thought the encounter would have an anticlimactic ending. I was wrong. After its initial retreat, the grizzly turned, swatted the ground, and came toward me.
Before gaining five yards, my ultra-compact Taurus GX4, loaded with Hornady Backcountry Defense ammo, was in hand. I had firm pressure on the trigger and was ready to dump the 10-round magazine when the bear suddenly turned and ran away.
My nerves spiked, and my heart was in my throat. The moment was so intense that after 30-plus seconds, I was still pressed out, sights steady as a rock.
I’ve shot a lot of handguns but have more confidence in my Taurus GX4 9mm than any other. A under-$360 micro-compact gun, the GX4 is light, easy to handle, feels great in hand, and produces very little recoil.

This article, as you likely guessed by the title, isn’t about the Taurus GX4. I only mention it because the handgun market is saturated and there are some great makes and models out there. Three of my four go-to handguns are made by Taurus. I’ve never had a bad experience with a Taurus sidearm, and I was thrilled to see the gunmaker dive into the bolt-action arena.
All I needed to know about Taurus’ Expedition rifle was that it was a Taurus. I saw the name and had confidence the manufacturer's first bolt-action build would be a good one.
Spoiler alert: I was right. Let’s dive in.

Taurus jumped into the bolt-action arena in 2024. I’m a bit disgusted that it took me so long to test this rifle. The Expedition features an 18-inch hammer-forged barrel, a sub-7-pound weight rating, and a price under $800.
Since 2024, this bolt-action design has generated considerable excitement among bolt-action enthusiasts. At first glance, this isn't just another entry-level rifle; it combines competition-grade craftsmanship with built-in practicality. Taurus currently offers the Expedition in .308 Win. and 6.5 Creedmoor. As much as I love the 6.5 Creedmoor’s accuracy, the tried-and-true .308 Win. was what arrived at my FFL. I was glad. The .308 Win. has a storied history. It’s a solid all-around big-game rifle, and I was excited to see how it performed.
Design and Features
The Expedition showcases a unique "hybrid" stock crafted from injection-molded polymer. This design merges competitive features with standard rifle elements. I was particularly impressed with the flat-bottom fore-end, which enhances stability whether shooting from a bench, prone, on sticks, or offhand. This design choice increases contact with the palm or any surface, improving balance.
The stock’s gray tone, accented with white flecks, provides a visually appealing compromise between solid color and camouflage. A raised comb also ensures optimal optic alignment. The receiver accommodates Remington 700 short-action scope mounts, typically using one-piece rails that simplify mounting but can sometimes challenge accurate eye alignment, especially when paired with low-profile combs or cheekpieces. A rail is not included, so you will have to buy one afterwards.
The comb transitions beautifully into a nearly vertical grip that feels natural and comfortable. My measurements put the rifle at 37.92 inches overall and 7.01 pounds, compared to Taurus’ stated 7.05. Regardless, it remains an easy rifle to shoulder and handle.

Standard Equipment and Ergonomics
Included is a standard sling swivel stud, a rounded Spartan Precision QD mount port, and a screw-mounted M-Lok accessory plate with multiple slots. The recoil pad, measuring just over an inch thick, provides ample cushioning.
I also appreciated the trigger guard's ergonomics, sized for use even with gloves. I can see the Expedition being a great whitetail rifle. I think whitetail hunters who spend time in cramped blinds, shooting houses, and tree stands will appreciate the maneuverability and hunt-ready features.
The bolt handle sports an ergonomic shape, and the bolt-throw degree is 60. (Jace Bauserman photo)
After removing the five-round magazine, I cycled the bolt several times while maintaining my focus downrange. The trigger features a smooth surface and fits comfortably into the finger’s first knuckle groove. The pull weight is heavy. When given a trigger that’s over 4 pounds, I’m gonna complain. My Wheeler Professional Digital Trigger Gauge measured the trigger at 4 pounds 9 ounces. The trigger isn’t adjustable, and while the Expedition leaves plenty to brag about, I believe Taurus will need to lighten the trigger before the Expedition becomes a go-to bolt-action.
Positioned directly behind the bolt handle, the two-position safety is a significant improvement over the more common push-button designs. Engaging the serrated safety keeps the rifle secure, while you can still operate the bolt without disengaging it.
Action and Barrel Characteristics
Taurus chose to utilize the reliable Remington Model 700 action for the Expedition, a decision I commend. The receiver, barrel, and bolt all sport Taurus’ robust matte black Diamond-Like Coating, a protective feature that adds to the rifle’s durability.
Situated on the left side, the bolt release lever is intuitive, while the spiral-fluted bolt with a rounded knob feels smooth and efficient. With three locking lugs and a 60-degree throw—my personal favorite—the action is both secure and fluid. The free-floated 18-inch barrel features a threaded muzzle, perfect for attaching a suppressor.
Make no mistake, Taurus didn’t build an accurate bolt-action rifle only to cripple accuracy with a short barrel. Taurus kept the barrel short so that a suppressor could be added, and the shooter would have a rifle with a standard barrel length, and not a 30-inch telephone-pole-like barrel to tote around the woods. I like that. I also like that I can run the barrel short to keep weight down and maximize maneuverability. I think this bolt-action rifle, without a suppressor, will make an awesome sit-and-wait whitetail rifle, and one western elk hunters will like it when still-hunting through dark timber.
The cold-hammer-forged barrel, featuring six-groove rifling with a 1:10 right-hand twist, contributes to the rifle’s clean aesthetic and potential accuracy.

I chose this scope for its user-friendly turret adjustments and illuminated reticle, knowing overcast fall days would challenge visibility. I aimed to provide the Taurus Expedition with the best possible chance to showcase its accuracy.
Range Performance
After bore-sighting, I settled the Expedition on Real Avid Ratchet Rest bags and lined up my shot. The first round hit paper, which is always a good sign. Even more impressive was the minimal felt recoil. Though not a long-action magnum caliber, the .308 Win. can produce some shoulder shock. I was pleased that though the rifle is light, short, and compact, it didn’t beat me up.
The rifle's bolt action felt stiff at first, requiring notable force to cycle. I hoped this would ease with use.
With initial accuracy confirmed, I attempted two off-hand shots at 100 yards. I hadn’t adjusted the scope, yet both rounds landed within three inches of my earlier bench-rested shots. Cycling improved slowly but still felt a bit rough.

The Expedition's solid ergonomics and lightweight design made off-hand shooting easier—critical in any spot-and-stalk hunting situation. With such minimal recoil, it’s particularly suitable for shooters of smaller stature.
After three additional shots on the bench, I fine-tuned elevation and windage. Notably, the trigger, initially perceived as heavy, seemed less so in practice despite my consistent four-plus-pound readings. I don’t love it, and it takes some getting used to, but the trigger pull weight shouldn’t be a deal breaker. The cycling became smoother over time, aided by dual plunger-style ejectors that effectively expelled brass.
The five-round magazine release sits just in front of the trigger guard. Although it accepts AICS-pattern magazines, it comes standard with a cleanly designed polymer magazine that feeds effortlessly.
The bolt handle sports an ergonomic shape, and the bolt-throw degree is 60. (Jace Bauserman photo)
Testing for Accuracy
I conducted accuracy tests from atop a hay bale prone at 100 yards, using a Magpul bipod. Taurus claims the Expedition can achieve sub-MOA accuracy in .308 Win. I held off on adding a suppressor to see what this compact, practical bolt-action could do on its own.
My first choice of ammunition was Federal’s Terminal Ascent 175-Grain, known for its effectiveness in the field. After zeroing in, the first five-shot group eliminated the half-inch black bullseye, measuring 0.86 inches despite a light left-to-right wind. The five-shot group averaged a commendable 2,501 fps.

After a 20-minute barrel-cooling period, I fired another five-round group using Norma BondStrike ammunition. While slightly high and right of my previous group, those shots measured 0.94 inches, reaffirming the Expedition’s potential as an excellent long-range performer. With each session, my confidence in the rifle solidified.
Across two days, I tested both Federal and Norma rounds in various positions: standing, sitting, and prone with BOG shooting sticks, targeting both paper and steel at distances up to 500 yards. Each squeeze of the trigger confirmed the Expedition’s capabilities.
Conclusion
This well-constructed bolt-action rifle excels for users of all experience levels. Its lightweight design and manageable recoil make it especially suitable for smaller-framed shooters, yet it stands as an impressive choice for any bolt-action enthusiast. My primary critique is of the bolt’s initial resistance; while it smoothed out with use, rapid cycling isn't its strongest point.
In researching prices, I discovered that both the 6.5 Creedmoor and .308 Win. variants can be found for under $1k. This is an incredible value. Pairing the Expedition with a quality optic and premium ammunition could make it your go-to rifle for the upcoming season.
