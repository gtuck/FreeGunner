Best Budget Hunting Rifle: Mossberg Patriot Predator 

The light and sub-MOA hunting rifle may be all you need for hunting big game.

 A budget hunting rifle with superb accuracy. (Alfredo Rico photo)

October 01, 2019

By Alfredo Rico

Mossberg’s Patriot line includes a wide array of calibers to handle everything from varmints to dangerous game. The Predator version features a Picatinny rail scope base, a 5/8x24 threaded barrel with a recessed muzzle crown and a flat dark earth stock. In 2019, Mossberg introduced the Patriot Predator Cerakote/Strata camo model, adding another affordable rifle to the mix with some pizazz to boot.
It is a straight-up hunting rifle with a thin, fluted 22-inch barrel that won’t feel like a boat anchor after hours of stalking. As the name implies, the barrel and the rest of the metalwork have a Cerakote finish in the company’s Patriot Brown color. The button-rifled barrel is also free-floated for optimal accuracy. Out of the box, the Predator weighed six pounds, 10 ounces, certainly a manageable weight for long days afield.
The classic stock features a shadow-line cheekpiece, and it’s dipped in TrueTimber’s Strata camo pattern, which is designed as a good all-around pattern for hunters. (Alfredo Rico photo)
Features
The classic-style stock is hydro-dipped in Strata camo from True-Timber. The straight comb puts your eyeline just above the bore, and the comb’s ridge is thick and flares out into a cheekpiece, making for a comfortable rest and face-fitting stock for right-handed shooters. The rifle grip and forend have a soft, stippled texture that improves traction in wet conditions.
The stock has a good amount of flex, which is obvious when moving the buttstock with a bipod on the fore-end or squeezing the barrel and fore-end together. Since I was shooting off a tripod, I was concerned the fore-end would touch the barrel and affect accuracy. To see if this was an issue, I also shot off a Bulls Bag to compare its groups to the tripod’s groups. It didn’t make a difference.

Sitting in the stock is a round action with a Picatinny scope rail mount. The action is not bedded and is held in place with two action screws. The rifle feeds from a detachable five-round box magazine made of a composite material. It’s a double-stack, flush-fitting magazine with a light spring that effortlessly accepts each round whether the magazine is in your hand or top loading when it’s in the rifle.
To release the magazine, put your index finger about an inch forward of the magazine and pull the latch toward you. The latch snappily ejects the magazine. Removing and inserting the magazine is buttery smooth.
Mossberg shaved spiral flutes into the bolt body, saving a little weight and giving the rifle a nice curb appeal. The push-feed bolt has twin lugs and a Sako-style extractor.

The Patriot Brown Cerakote pairs nicely with the camo pattern, and Mossberg’s proven action and excellent LBA trigger combine to create a good-looking, accurate rifle. (Alfredo Rico photo)
Performance
Between the bolt’s 90-degree throw and the large bolt handle, I found I had to alter my grip on the handle a little for the tight space between the SIG Sierra BDX 3.5-12x42mm scope’s magnification ring and bolt knob.
Cycling the bolt is smooth, but when it’s fully retracted, the last inch has quite a bit of play, causing some binding if it’s not pulled straight back. Once I found the sweet spot, binding wasn’t an issue.
I found no shortcomings in Mossberg’s Lightning Bolt Action trigger. The LBA trigger is adjustable from two to seven pounds and has very little creep. The rifle arrived set at two pounds, eight ounces and broke consistently, making it one of the best triggers in a rifle at this price point.
The lightness and crispness of the LBA trigger helped me achieve some impressive groups when testing the rifle’s accuracy. As is expected with thin barrels, the Predator’s barrel heated quickly, and as a result the point of impact would shift. Six shots seemed to be the threshold before I began to get flyers. For the last three shots of my accuracy test, I would let the barrel cool to get a decent last group. Despite the flexible stock, my smallest group was 0.5 inch, and I had several 0.62-inch groups, which shows me the rifle has great accuracy potential.
The oversize bolt handle and the bolt’s throw didn’t leave a lot of room to grip the handle when paired with the fairly large-ocular SIG Sierra BDX 3.5-12x42mm scope. (Alfredo Rico photo)
While shooting the Winchester Deer XP Extreme Point, my first nine shots grouped very consistently. I fired six more shots to see if the point of impact would shift or the groups open up. They didn’t. That was a total of 15 consecutive shots with no shift, and all the groups were consistent. With that particular ammo the rifle acted like it had a rigid stock and heavy barrel. Goes to show that when you find a load a rifle really likes good things happen.

Closing Thoughts
Through nearly 300 rounds of quality ammunition, the Patriot Predator Cerakote/Strata camo ran like a Timex with no failure to feeds or light primer strikes. It shoots impressively well for a rifle with a suggested retail just over $500—and a $423 street price at Gallery of Guns. The Predator not only looks good but also delivers downrange where it counts. It’s difficult to ask much more of a rifle at this price point.
