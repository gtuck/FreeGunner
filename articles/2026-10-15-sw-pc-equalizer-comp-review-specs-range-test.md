S&W PC Equalizer Comp Review [Specs & Range Test]

S&W PC Equalizer Spec Breakdown and Live-Fire Range Performance

 Semiautos with active safety systems are hot, and the new Performance Center version of S&W’s Equalizer takes the platform to the next level. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

By Keith Wood

I’m not sure about other product sectors, but it’s clear to me the firearm world is cyclical. After years of market dominance by striker-fired handguns with mainly passive safety mechanisms, buyers are once again interested in pistols with active safety features. Enter the Smith & Wesson Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp, a high-performance handgun built from the ground up with safety, shootability and ease of use in mind.
The Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp is built on a polymer frame with steel inserts. The frame and the grip module are one and the same rather than a chassis system. We have come to expect that most polymer-frame handguns are striker-fired, but as great as today’s striker-fired guns can be, very few of them have enviable trigger pulls. To make this gun as shootable as possible, Smith & Wesson designed it as a true single action with an internal hammer rather than a striker. Remember what I said about things being cyclical?
The grip safety pivots at the bottom, so it’s easy to disengage, and the gun has a thumb safety as well. The internal hammer, paired with a straight trigger, results in a better trigger pull than you’d get with a striker-fired pistol. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Safeties
One of the central design themes of the Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp is its safety feature set. Not everyone is comfortable carrying a loaded handgun with no manual safety, especially when we are talking about appendix carry. The prospect of having a muzzle pointed at one’s femoral artery or groin with nothing but a five-pound trigger preventing disaster is grounds for reasonable concern. The Equalizer Carry Comp is built to provide additional layers of safety that can be seen and touched.
There is an ambidextrous frame-mounted manual safety that operates much like the one found on classic handguns such as the 1911 and
 Browning Hi Power
. Internally, the safety prevents the trigger bar from being able to actuate the rest of the fire-control assembly. With the safety engaged, the trigger can be pulled and the pistol cannot fire. The safety levers on either side of the frame are easy to reach while maintaining concealability.

The second notable safety feature is the grip safety, which is an uncommon feature on a polymer-frame pistol. As with a 1911, the grip safety ensures this pistol will not fire without a proper grip on the handgun. Unlike the 1911, though, the grip safety on the Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp has two functions.
First, the grip safety prevents the trigger mechanism from firing unless that safety is disengaged. Second, when the shooter’s hand disengages the grip safety, a firing pin block in the slide is levered out of the way. This makes the handgun drop safe without compromising the trigger pull the way most firing pin blocks do.
It’s a clever system that works seamlessly when shooting. The grip safety unit pivots from the bottom so it is, in my experience, easy to disengage intuitively even with a very high grip on the gun. You basically forget it’s there.

Frame, Controls, Trigger
The pistol’s polymer frame is textured to provide a firm gripping surface, and the trigger guard is cut high to allow the pistol to sit low in the hand. The grip section of the frame is relatively short to maximize concealability, but the functional gripping length can be controlled by which of the three included magazines you choose (more on this shortly). The dust cover area of the frame is molded to allow the attachment of a small light or laser.
Other than the safeties, the controls consist of a trigger, a magazine catch, a slide stop and a takedown lever. The Performance Center trigger system uses a flat-faced bow made from aluminum. There is no safety lever inside the trigger, which is textured with small vertical serrations.
My test handgun’s trigger broke consistently at 4.75 pounds but felt much lighter. The reset was very tactile. An overtravel stop is molded into the pistol’s frame. Overall, I would rate the trigger on this handgun as very good. Whatever trouble it took to engineer this pistol as a single action instead of a striker-fired gun was worth it in terms of the trigger experience.
The magazine catch is on the strong side of the pistol but is reversible. The slide stop is left side only. Operating the takedown lever requires locking the slide to the rear. At that point, the lever cams downward, and the slide can be removed. From there, the recoil spring assembly and barrel are easily serviceable.
The barrel and slide are cut with a single-port compensator designed to reduce recoil and muzzle rise. The front sight is an Ameriglo tritium. The back of the slide flares out to provide a better grip for racking, and it’s cut for optics on the RMSc footprint. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Slide
The Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp’s slide is machined from stainless steel and treated with the company’s Armornite finish. This is a nitride process that hardens the surface and provides protection against both corrosion and abrasion. Carrying some models of handguns close to the skin in hot, humid conditions can make rust a real problem. Between the stainless steel parts and the Armornite finish, that won’t be a problem with this one.

One selling point of the Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp is the easy-to-rack slide. Part of this is accomplished by the deep cuts that serve as cocking surfaces. The rear of the slide also flares outward so there is a defined gripping point.
Some of the slide cuts are functional, while some are purely for looks. Cuts on the top and side of the slide expose the barrel for aesthetic reasons while the broad cut near the top of the muzzle works in conjunction with the ported barrel to act as a recoil compensator—the “Comp” in Carry Comp.
Sights and Opics
The slide is optic ready and uses the RMSc footprint, which has become more or less standard on compact handguns due to its size. No adapter plates are needed; the sights bolt directly to the slide. I chose to test the pistol using only irons.
The front sight is an Ameriglo Trooper, which combines a bright yellow circle with a tritium lamp. The rear sight is black with a serrated face and a U-shape notch. The rear sight is drift-adjustable for windage and retains its position using a hex screw. These sights make an excellent choice for a defensive handgun; they strike a fine balance between speed and precision.
The front of the slide features lightening cuts, and the polymer frame incorporates a three-slot accessory rail for lights and lasers. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Compensator, Barrel, Recoil Assembly
The gun sports a 3.675-inch ramped barrel made from stainless steel. The barrel has a single port directly behind the muzzle to vent propellant gases upward, counteracting the upward recoil forces.
There was a time when I’d have considered a compensator as superfluous on a handgun intended for concealed carry, but muzzle rise is a real factor with a 22-ounce 9mm, and the compensator helps reduce it. A radial cut at the rear of the barrel hood serves as a visual loaded-chamber indicator.
Like most modern handguns, the Equalizer is a locked-breech pistol, which means the barrel remains in battery for a fraction of a second to allow the bullet to leave the bore. One of the many advantages of a locked-breech pistol is it can use a significantly lighter recoil spring than a blowback design such as a Walther PP. This makes the slide easier to rack, a big selling point for customers with diminished hand strength.
The Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp incorporates a single flat-wire recoil spring that is held captive by a full-length metal guide rod. According to my measurement, it took roughly 11 pounds of force to completely rack the slide.

One feature that is a bit unusual in today’s market is the Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp’s internal rather than external extractor. Even though the extractor is contained inside the slide, it actually functions more like an external extractor but is part of a firing pin assembly block that is pinned into place. It’s different, but it works just fine. The fixed steel ejector rides inside the frame.
The pistol ships with magazines of three different sizes. The 10-round magazine has a thin polymer base pad that fits flush with the bottom of the grip for maximum concealability. There is a 13-round magazine with a longer base pad that allows for a full-fingered grip on the pistol, and a 15-round mag with an extended base pad. The magazine bodies are steel.
Three magazines are included: a flush-fit that holds 10 rounds; a 13-rounder with an extended base pad; and a magazine with an even larger extension holding 15 rounds. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Performance
Due to the gun’s barrel length, I tested accuracy at 15 yards according to the 
Handguns
 magazine protocol. I shot the gun at near dusk to avoid the summer heat, and the highly visible front sight did its job well. Hornady’s 115-grain Critical Defense load was the most accurate by a wide margin, stacking rounds onto the bullseye targets in tight little knots. Bullets at the heavier end of the spectrum, including Federal’s 147-grain Syntech Training Match, did not group nearly as well. Reliability was 100 percent with the three loads I tested.
With excellent sights and a trigger pull that was far better than any spongy striker-fired unit, I didn’t have trouble making hits on steel targets. I grip handguns pretty hard, so keeping the muzzle on target during recoil wasn’t difficult, and the compensator was no doubt helpful in this regard. The comp makes the muzzle blast louder at the shooter’s ear, but not significantly so. When shooting from retention, that might be more of an issue.
The Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp is slightly longer and taller than many other subcompact 9mm handguns on the market. Though it isn’t a pocket gun, that size makes it more shootable. The overall size can also be manipulated a bit by the different length magazines. For me, the 13-round mag hit the sweet spot in terms of balancing shootability with concealability.

While I’m a huge 1911 fan and shoot them often, I will admit that on rare occasions I’ve failed to disengage the grip safety due to a high grip on the gun. This was not a problem at all with the grip safety on the Equalizer. The manual safety was easy to manipulate and provided a good position for my thumb. These two safety features offer tangible advantages on a concealed-carry handgun, especially when carrying at the appendix position.
Final Thoughts
We are, without a doubt, living in the golden age of handguns designed for concealed carry. Today’s compact handguns combine trim dimensions, light weight, magazine capacity and shootability in a way that we could not have imagined when I got my first carry permit in the 1990s. The Smith & Wesson Performance Center Equalizer Carry Comp epitomizes these virtues.
It is a safe, reliable, accurate and concealable American-made handgun. For those looking for an alternative to the sea of striker-fired pistols with no active safeties and substandard trigger pulls, this gun might be the one for you. The gun combines all the modern touches we’ve come to expect, with a tip of the hat to traditionalists in terms of its safety features. The market is coming to realize that some of those “old school” design elements existed for a reason.
