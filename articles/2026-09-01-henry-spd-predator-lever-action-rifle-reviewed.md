Henry SPD PREDATOR Lever-Action Rifle: Reviewed

Fast, light, and legal in restricted states, the SPD PREDATOR from Henry is a high-speed machine. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)

September 01, 2026

By Tom Beckstrand

Just when I thought we had seen the final evolution of lever-­action rifles, Henry Repeating Arms 
trotted out its Supreme
. After a year of testing and evaluation, it earned Guns & Ammo’s 
2025 Rifle of the Year award
. The Supreme is a lever-­action, has no exposed hammer and takes detachable box magazines normally found on AR-­pattern rifles. Pull it apart for closer inspection and the bolt looks suspiciously like what’s found in an AR-­15. The question is, “Why?” Here’s the answer.
The Henry SPD PREDATOR is a .223/5.56 NATO-­chambered lever-­action rifle that feeds from a detachable box magazine. The 18-­inch BSF 416R stainless-­steel barrel has a 1-­in-­8-­inch twist and is tension-­wrapped in carbon fiber. The wood-­laminate furniture has an adjustable comb, rubber recoil pad, and sling swivels. The Harris S-­LM bipod is also included. (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
One of the disadvantages of lever-­action rifles in modern times is the reload process. Most can be timed with a sundial. The loading gate on the side of the receiver of most lever-­action rifles has been around for some 150 years, and it’s a great way to load the rifle. It’s just slow when compared to detachable box magazines. There are proficient lever-­gun shooters who can load these rifles fairly quickly, just watch a SASS Cowboy Action Shooting match. However, the average American gun owner can’t load a lever gun faster than a rifle with a detachable box magazine.
The 
SPD PREDATOR
 — like other Supreme models — is chambered in .223 Remington/5.56 NATO, and it takes AR-­15-­pattern magazines. Henry told us that “PREDATOR” is an acronym that stands for “Precision Rifle Engineered for Dispatching Agile Targets Over Range,” hence every letter in the name is capitalized. There is a robust aftermarket with both aluminum and polymer magazines ready to use with these rifles, and any concerns with these mags have been sorted out. They are reliable, plentiful and affordable.
(Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
The AR magazine also allows Henry’s lever-­action to shoot modern bullets with high ballistic coefficients (BC). Traditional lever guns rely on flat-­ or round-­nose bullets. These rounds stack end-­to-­end but can’t use high-­BC bullets because a pointy bullet nose can set off the primer of the round in front of it during recoil. While it might sound like a small difference, it’s a huge advantage when shooting beyond 200 yards. Bullets with high BCs retain velocity better and experience less deflection in the wind.

Under Pressure
The most popular lever guns today are still more than 100 years old. They use a bolt to hold the breech closed, and the bolt is held in place by one or two lugs located near the rear of the receiver. This means the receiver has to resist the pressure from firing and not stretch or allow the bolt to move rearward. It is a wonderful and fairly robust arrangement when chamber pressure is kept around 45,000 pounds per square inch (psi) or lower. Most traditional lever-­action cartridges are factory-­loaded well below this pressure. Of course, many of these rifles are proof-­tested with ammunition loaded in excess of 60,000 psi, but almost none are designed for a steady diet at that level.
Comb height can be set to align your eye properly behind most optics. A wrench to loosen and tighten the screw is provided. (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
Henry’s SPD PREDATOR is chambered in .223 Remington/5.56 NATO. The 5.56 NATO has a maximum chamber pressure of 58,000 psi. That means the Supreme can handle that pressure in perpetuity. The difference between 45,000 psi and 58,000 psi is about 200 to 250 feet per second (fps). Combine that bump in velocity with better BC bullets and the downrange performance looks significantly better. The SPD PREDATOR is a viable long-­range contender.

The key to the ability to handle pressure is the bolt. It looks similar to the bolt found in an AR-­15. Since the bolt locks into a receiver extension — another proven method — the aluminum alloy receiver bears none of the load from firing. This allows the SPD PREDATOR to shoot today’s much higher-­pressure cartridges with no concern about whether the rifle can handle the abuse.
The adjustable trigger tested at just more than 3 pounds. The magazine release is a serrated paddle lever in front of the triggerguard. (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
Unlike its AR-­15 progenitor, the SPD PREDATOR bolt stays closed until the chamber pressure drops to zero. The lugs aren’t exposed to the shearing forces that occur when the bolt is unlocking under pressure from the gas system in an AR-­15. While an AR may eventually shed one of the locking lugs on either side of the extractor, I don’t see how it’s possible for the SPD PREDATOR to lose a bolt lug. As durable as the AR is, I think the SPD PREDATOR is more durable.

The SPD PREDATOR cycles by opening the lever, which pulls the bolt carrier and bolt rearward. There is a cam pin slotted into the carrier that rotates the bolt to unlock it, allowing the lever to pull the bolt assembly rearward until the fired round ejects. A retaining pin in the side of the carrier can be removed, allowing the firing pin to slide out the back. Anyone who has disassembled an AR-­type bolt carrier group won’t need the manual to do the same for the SPD PREDATOR.
Under the knurled cap is a 1/2x28 threaded barrel that accepts muzzle devices, and lever actions are excellent suppressor hosts. (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
One concern I had when I first laid hands on the SPD PREDATOR was how complex maintenance was going to be. Lever guns can be difficult to clean because the bolts aren’t always easily removed from the receiver. This makes it hard to clean the rifle with a cleaning rod from the breech end.
All it takes to disassemble the SPD PREDATOR is to push the two pins out the side of the receiver and then separate the halves. The trigger assembly and internal hammer stay with the lower half, and the barrel and handguard stay with the upper. Once these two are separated, I could look straight down the barrel from the breech end. It is as easy to clean this rifle as it is any AR-­15.

Accuracy Guarantee
The decision to put a sub-­minute-­of-­angle (MOA) guarantee on the SPD PREDATOR was not one that Henry took lightly. They knew the Supreme was a great rifle, but they wanted to determine how accurate it could be made. Henry tested numerous barrels from several different manufacturers before selecting the BSF barrel on this rifle.
Push the tang-­mounted thumb safety switch forward to fire, revealing a red-­dot indicator. It is serrated to be used in both directions. (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
The SPD is called the “PREDATOR” because it is light, fast and accurate. It is also chambered in cartridges appropriate for varmint or 
predator hunting
. Varmint hunting, in particular, can result in hot barrels from long strings of fire. One of the big concerns when carbon-­fiber-­wrapped barrels came out was the difference in thermal expansion of the steel and carbon fiber. The disparity can cause a barrel to delaminate, separating carbon fiber from steel or stainless steel.
BSF uses a carbon-­wrapping technique that is proprietary. The carbon-­fiber sleeve is a barrel tensioner, and it barely touches the barrel. It creates tunnels that air can move through, so carbon fiber doesn’t insulate the barrel. During G&A’s testing, the barrel exterior remained cool. The .223 Remington isn’t exactly a barrel burner, but we gave it no rest from shooting one group to another and from one load to the next. It never showed signs of opening up or walking and remained accurate for the duration of testing.
The forged carbon-­fiber rail is pre-­installed but removeable. It supports any reflex sight and most riflescopes. (Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
The secret to the rifle’s amazing accuracy — other than the BSF barrel — is that it is free-­floating, which is unheard of on a lever-­action. It’s true. Henry uses a barrel extension and barrel nut to attach the barrel to the receiver. Underneath the barrel, Henry attaches an independent cylinder directly to the receiver. The forend slides over this cylinder, which is held in place by a long bolt that inserts through the forend’s tip. This means that using a bipod or a front bag for support does not influence the barrel’s point of impact.
Another feature special to the SPD PREDATOR is the use of Diamond Like Carbon (DLC) on select trigger components. Henry states the trigger is “match grade” and it would be usable for a match. The trigger actuates an internal hammer, much like the hammer in an AR-­15. The SPD PREDATOR trigger is much better than any factory AR-­pattern trigger, though. It’s not a match-­grade bolt-­action trigger, either. Its pull weight is light, so I didn’t adjust it. My trigger gauge measured at just more than 3 pounds, and the feel exhibited the tiniest bit of creep.
(Photo by Tom Beckstrand)
The SPD PREDATOR is more useful than just shooting predators and varmints. I’ve never shot a match with a lever gun, but this one could do it while still being a lot of fun. Lever-­gun lovers should rejoice to have a high ­pressure capable rifle that also offers lightning-­fast reloads. Imagine Cowboy Action-­like gunslinging, but with targets that are hundreds of yards away instead of just yards away. It suppresses better than an AR, too, and doesn’t get the same dirty looks that ARs do.
Henry’s SPD PREDATOR combines all the best features of an AR-­15 into a lever-­action rifle with a premium BSF carbon-­fiber barrel capable of exceptional accuracy. While it might sound like a ton of fun — and it is — the rifle is just as at home hunting as it is for defense.
