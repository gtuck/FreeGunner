November 26, 2026

By Sean Utley

We’ve seen an incredible amount of advancement in almost every facet of our lives. Cars are now electric and self-driving. Our homes are smart and can be completely controlled by our frickin’ phones. Let’s not even get into computers and electronics—I gave up trying to keep up with those changes years ago. Naturally, that tech has spilled over into the gun space, too.
We’ve got apps of all sorts, advanced tools, and reloading presses that do nearly everything automatically. There are laser rangefinders with onboard GPS and accurate ballistic solvers—not to mention endless training resources right at our fingertips. That said, some of the most noteworthy advancements are also the most overlooked: how rifle actions and barrels are manufactured.
This is important because now the average person can assemble a few components and end up with a highly accurate, highly capable precision setup. No gunsmith needed. It’s simpler than assembling an AR15. Done right this build will shoot 1/2 MOA or better with factory ammunition, and the operator will have created something truly to their liking.
The Bix'n Andy trigger attached to the Falkor 7even action. (Sean Utley photo)
Falkor Core
I’ll go on record proclaiming my love affair with Falkor actions. You might know them as Falkor Defense, makers of a semi-auto 300 Win Mag that made them popular. They were once a gas gun maker until the owners, avid hunters, made a hard switch to making REM 700 patterned actions. It was a hard switch in two ways. One. Going from manufacturing gas guns to bolt actions. And two. It was an arduous task to design and build a really good action. Five years of commitment filled with challenges, small wins, bigger losses, and heavy financial investment. I’m still afraid to ask Jason and Melinda Sonju if it was worth it. But having used their actions for years, I tend to think so. They have created the smoothest actions available today. And I don’t say that lightly.

I reached out to Falkor for a 7even action for a 25 Creedmoor build. Why 25 Creedmoor? I still don’t know. I guess you can blame those pesky PRS shooters for it. I wish I had a more technical explanation than that. My goal is to focus on the ease of the process, regardless of which chambering you choose. Once I had the action, I needed a barrel and nothing goes with a Falkor action than a Proof Research barrel. Both companies are based in Kalispell, Montana making this pairing of components nothing less than a perfect “fit”. The barrel is a prefit barrel, threaded to work with any Falkor action. You simply order it and install it in yourself.
Why So Special
Let’s dive a little deeper into these two components. Keep in mind that a complete build will still need a trigger, chassis, optic, and bipod, but these two core components—the action and the barrel—are what I want to highlight.
The Falkor action is a Remington 700 clone. That sounds easy enough, but there was nothing easy about Falkor’s development of the 7even, MT7, and LW7 actions. Each is a bit different in its makeup, but the core is the same. Starting with a piece of 416 stainless steel, Falkor machines the billet into the initial overall shape. The 7even actions, which is what I use for my heavier target-style rifles, have a machined, integral top rail with a 20 MOA cant and an integral recoil lug. Since the top rail and recoil lug are machined with the action body, there are no screws or pins to come loose. Ever. Now, if I’m being honest, in all the years I’ve been shooting precision rifles and after countless builds, I’ve only had one top rail come loose. But it was a pain to diagnose. Therefore, I’m glad for the one-piece design.

The 20-MOA top rail is machined into the receiver. (Sean Utley photo)
From there, it goes to the wire EDM to cut the bolt channel and raceway. This step establishes the foundation for the action's smoothness. Wire EDM works by feeding a thin, electrically charged wire through the action while it is submerged in fluid, cutting the precise bolt channel. EDM is used because it achieves very tight tolerances—down to less than the width of a human hair.
After that, the action gets a little more superficial machining before coating. While the action is a smooth performer at this stage, it isn’t until after their proprietary coatings are applied that the action becomes dreamy and glassy smooth.
Turning to the bolt, it’s made from 4340 chromoly steel. It’s fluted to ensure function in adverse conditions. It’s also available in a two-lug with a 75-degree bolt throw, an additiona from their previous 90-degree bolt throw. The latest version of their right handed actions features an interchangeable bolt head that allows the user to switch from standard bolt face (.480) to either a .223 (.385) or magnum head (.550) for cartridges like the 6.5 PRC. The user need only change the barrel and the bolt head. The benefit is that changing cartridge choice will only cost you a new bolt head and barrel, sparing you the expense of buying a new rifle.
The bolt head can be changed out in about 30 seconds with practice. You simply remove a pin, pop off the bolt head, replace bolt head, then replace pin. A wave washer rests between the bolt head and bolt body to give it the necessary movement to keep the bolt head straight. Think of it as a floating bolt head.
The completed rifle build using the Falkor 7even action and Proof Research steel barrel chambered in 25 Creedmoor. (Sean Utley photo)
The Build
Using an action wrench and a barrel vice were all the tools I needed to thread the Proof barrel to the Falkor action. For a trigger I chose a Bix’n Andy TacSport Pro X. Bix’n Andy are the finest triggers available, employing ball bearings within the trigger for sear engagement. When you press a Bix’n Andy trigger, you’re instantly hooked.

Once assembled, I placed inside of a Vision Pro Chassis. Again, one of the best components you can utilize.
Performance
After placing the barreled action in the chassis, I attached a Zero Compromise ZCO840 optic. This is a top tier optic with phenomenal clarity and color fidelity.
25 Creedmoor competition ammo options are slim. You’ll either be using Hornady’s ELD-M Match with a 134 grain bullet or you’ll be loading your own. I chose this route to see if the 25 Creedmoor round would be worth any further effort.
Once zeroed the rifle, I shoot a few groups. Oddly enough it took about 20 rounds for the barrel to settle in. That’s a first. I had hoped for a 1/2 MOA 5-shot group or better, but I wasn’t able to achieve that on this outing. I had multiple four-round groups that landed 1/2 MOA or less – 0.53-, 0.42-, and 0.32-inch to be exact. But fliers opened them up to 0.87-, 0.94-, and 1.19-inch, respectively. I honestly think the flier was more of a suppressor issue, on at least one of the groups. The others may be due to ammo since the standard deviation was 20.2 fps. Average velocity was 2872 fps. Overall, I’m very happy with this easy-to-do build and it will obviously perform better with handloads.
Long distance shots were quite easy as well. I setup a profile in the Leupold Control app and uploaded it to my BX-6 LRF, which uses Hornady’s 4DOF for solutions. It was as simple as find, laze, dial, and shoot out to over 900 yards.
The 90-degree bolt throw with a beautiful bolt handle. (Sean Utley photo)
Final Thoughts
At this point I’ve mated Falkor actions with five or six Proof barrels, both steel and carbon, and I’ve never been disappointed with the results, even with factory ammunition. It’s been the biggest and best easy button. One I plan to utilize until the results change. But now that Falkor is producing Proof branded actions, the only change I expect is even better performance.
I’ve made this Falkor action/Proof barrel recommendation to several people over the past few years, and no one has expressed anything but great satisfaction with the combo, while thanking me for the recommendation. You too can do the same. Order up a Falkor action and a Proof prefit barrel. Let the advancement in manufacturing do the work for you. It doesn’t get any easier than that.
