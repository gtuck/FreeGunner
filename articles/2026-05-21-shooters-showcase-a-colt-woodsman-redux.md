Shooter's Showcase: A Colt Woodsman Redux

For all you fans of the classic Colt Woodsman, there is an unapologetic homage in the form of Standard Manufacturing's SG22.

 The SG22 from Standard Manufacturing is a second-generation Colt Woodsman by another name, manufactured using original tools and techniques to arrive at old-time quality. (Terry Wieland photo)

May 21, 2026

By Terry Wieland

The pistol that is pictured above is not called the Woodsman; instead, it’s the SG22, a product of Standard Manufacturing of New Britain, Connecticut. Standard is a subsidiary of Connecticut Shotgun Manufacturing Company, owned by Tony Galazan.
But place the SG22 alongside a Woodsman from its finest era—the early 1950s—and you will be hard pressed to tell the difference. Like the A.H. Fox shotgun to which Galazan gave new life in 1995, the SG22 is an unapologetic homage to the revered Colt Woodsman.
There is a substratum of gun lovers, your obedient correspondent being one, who place great value on the pure pleasure to be found in operating a mechanism composed of beautifully fitted, highly polished, slick, and cooperative parts. It’s not just hitting a target: It’s hitting a target while feeling the gun is an extension of yourself. Feeling that it would be difficult to miss.
Devotees of Glocks, Remington 1100s, and the plethora of ARs never seem to appreciate this, but that’s fine because I don’t appreciate their pets, either. Something for everyone.

Speaking of which, if Glocks and black guns are your taste, Galazan also has the G4S, a black steel and synthetic .22 that resembles the Tommy gun of tribal memory, complete with a 50-round drum magazine. If the SG22 and the G4S together don’t represent the full range of technical and aesthetic possibilities of a .22 rimfire, what does?
The Original Colt Woodsman
The 
Woodsman
 saw the light of day in its earliest form in 1915 and underwent a series of changes and modifications over the next 30 years. When Colt resumed civilian production after 1945, it appeared in what became recognized as its most sophisticated form and produced to its finest level.
The most highly prized, then and now, was the Match Target. This pistol had a full-length underlug that gave it a profile similar to the Colt 1911, but with a grip closely resembling the Luger. Overall, it was racy, well-balanced, and because they were so highly prized by their original owners, it’s not difficult to find one today in excellent condition.

In its way, the Woodsman spawned a family of .22 pistols that followed its basic principles of design. These included the original High Standards beginning in the 1920s and the later Browning Challenger made by FN in Belgium. Of course, each sought a niche that would set it apart. In the 1950s, with its Supermatic series, High Standard introduced the concept of readily interchangeable barrels and produced them in a variety of lengths and weights, complete with muzzle brakes, either integral or removable.
Browning established itself as the high-quality entry, with a degree of fit and finish that put other guns to shame back then, and today looks like something that emerged from Tiffany’s. The Challenger, produced for a decade between the early 1960s and mid-’70s, closely resembled the earlier Woodsman known as the Target model, with a slim barrel married to a swept-back grip.
If the 1911 is iconic (and I think we can agree it is), then the Woodsman is equally so.
The SG22
It’s that model that Galazan chose to emulate with the 
SG22
, with one telling difference. The Match Target’s magazine had one feature that set it apart. The button to depress the follower and spring was shaped like a tombstone rather than the usual round disc, and these became known as “tombstone magazines.” Today, originals command $200 to $300 on the collector market.
The SG22 arrives with a tombstone magazine, in a subtle salute to the original top-quality Woodsman.

If the SG22 feels like an old-time pistol with old-time quality, there’s a reason. According to Galazan, each pistol is produced using original equipment and techniques. There is no involvement of CNC machinery or other modern methods that, while reducing costs and tolerances, give guns a machine-made feel that is almost undefinable, but eminently noticeable.
Like the Woodsman before it, and the original Browning Challenger, and some of the early High Standards, the SG22 is a pistol you like to keep on your desk, close at hand, where you can admire it and pick it up every so often for the sheer pleasure of handling a beautiful mechanism.
It is a pleasure that is all too rare these days, but it’s worth whatever you pay for it.
