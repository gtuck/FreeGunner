Franchi Momentum Utility Lite Elite .308 Win.: Full Review

Franchi has embraced Jeff Cooper's Scout Rifle concept with an Italian flair. Here's a full review.

 (Photo by Mark Fingar)

November 15, 2026

By Guns & Ammo Staff

Col. Jeff Cooper’s Scout Rifle was a concept that still features prominently in the rifle landscape. The rules were pretty simple. The rifle had to be able to take down animals weighing up to 880 pounds, measure no more than 39 inches in length, ideally weigh less than 6.6 pounds, have a long-­eye-relief scope of 2X or 3X with back-­up iron sights, and be magazine-­fed. The goal being a rifle that’s easy to carry and capable of handling many different shooting scenarios. The rifle could serve admirably as a hunting rifle or as a self-­defense rifle.
Two sections of M-Lok rail are positioned at the forend on both sides and underneath. A bipod would be the most practical accessory, but adding a light could be appropriate, too. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Guns & Ammo’s Contribution
Of the various rifles offered that follow the principles outlined above, 
Franchi’s Momentum Utility Lite Elite (MULE)
 is one of the most shooter-­friendly. Guns & Ammo’s Editor Eric Poole supported Franchi’s effort by detailing its configuration and suggesting the acronym. The MULE’s stock, receiver, and trigger were all selected to offer maximum performance and serviceability without requiring a toolbox to keep it running.
Metal QD sling-swivel studs are integrated to the bottom and side of the stock. The toe and flats of the stock are useful for gripping with the support hand or placing on a rear bag. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The stock has a forend that is flat on the bottom with rounded sides that extend 12 inches in front of the magazine well. There are aluminum M-­Lok attachment points inletted into the stock on three sides with quick-­detach (QD) flush cup mounts in each. The M-­Lok is great for mounting a bipod — a common occurrence — or for mounting an infrared laser pointer or flashlight — the less common use. The buttstock has QD flush cups on both sides of the stock at the toe. Instead of just molding the sling mount points into the stock, Franchi used metal inserts so that the sling won’t pull out of the stock. Keeping the toe free of sling studs that protrude means this stock will ride a rear bag well. Placing flush cups in all these locations means the rifle can lie flat against the shooter’s back when slung over the shoulder, and the bolt handle faces away from the shooter.
Different-size inserts are provided to adjust comb height. This means the MULE will adapt to different optics and mounts, and different shooter profiles. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Most scout rifles have a section of optic rail mounted in front of the receiver for use with a long eye-­relief scope. This positions the scope fairly high, so it’s important to have a cheekpiece that accounts for the scope’s position. The MULE does. A simple spacer system allows the shooter to set the comb height where the stock offers good support for various head sizes and shapes sitting behind scopes or reflex sights attached at unknown heights. Two spacers come with the MULE, both with a negative comb that moves the stock slightly away from the cheek under recoil. This system offers excellent utility without driving up the cost or adding a bunch of weight.

Receiver & Trigger
The receiver is Franchi’s own design that uses two action screws to hold the barreled action into the stock. Unlike receivers using the Remington 700 footprint, the MULE receiver has copious thread engagement between the action screws and the receiver. This is a critical step for minimizing so-­called “flyers” because the better the screw attaches to the receiver, the less likely the receiver is to exhibit unwanted occasional movement. The two screws pull the receiver down into an aluminum “V” block that settles into a recess in the receiver. The design is economical, secure and effective.
Unusual for a modern rifle, the receiver doesn’t use a cassette trigger that encloses the trigger components within a metal body. The MULE trigger is more comparable to the trigger found in the original Winchester Model 70 or Ruger Model 77. The levers and springs are exposed, which allows debris to fall free from these critical components. Triggers like the one in this rifle are great for field use because they are easy to keep clean and rarely freeze to the point of inoperability. The trigger let-­off weight adjusts between 2 and 4 pounds.
The trigger is adjustable between 2 and 4 pounds. Above it is the bolt-release used to remove the bolt from the action. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
A Modern Scout
With the fundamentals well-­executed, Franchi set about the serious business of turning the bolt-­action rifle into a Scout rifle Col. Cooper could appreciate. The first step was to install a 16-­inch cold hammer-­forged barrel and thread the muzzle 5/8-­24. The twist rate of the barrel is 1-in-11 inches. For a .308, it’s a bit unusual but it tested well with bullets up to 175 grains at the velocity 16 inches of barrel produces.

Hammer-­forged barrels are durable and some of the longest lasting barrels made. It is also common for these barrels to take a while to settle in. Accuracy testing is best done after a couple cleanings and after at least 200 rounds have gone down the bore. The ability to attach a suppressor wasn’t something Cooper considered at the time, but we think he would approve and appreciate them today. The thread pitch on the MULE’s muzzle is the most common and can be made to work with almost any .30-­caliber suppressor.
The sights when raised offer an aiming post to use with an aperture rear sight. Like a handgun sight, the lowered position presents a post and notch.(Photo by Mark Fingar)
The MULE comes with flip-­up iron sights. The aluminum front sight base is mounted directly to the barrel, while the rear flip-­up sight is part of the Picatinny rail section mounted to the receiver. Four screws hold the rail to the receiver, while the rest of the rail extends over the barrel.

The bottom “metal” is actually polymer, and it’s Franchi’s design. It accepts AICS-­pattern detachable box magazines and one eight-­round mag is included. 
The optic rail extends beyond receiver and over the barrel, supporting use of a 1X reflex sight or low-power Scout-style scope. G&A used a Burris Scout 2-7x32mm with Ballistic Plex. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
The magazine release is a paddle that sits just forward of and straddles the triggerguard. Extending the index finger of the firing hand is all it takes to activate the release. Significant spring tension means the shooter must use deliberate force to release the magazine. AICS mags and the MULE’s bolt design are an ideal match for feeding reliability. The 60-­degree bolt lift and three-­lug bolt face mean that one lug is at the 6-­o’clock position when the bolt is open. Putting a lug in this location places additional bolt face surface area in a location to contact the cartridge case head for the rounds sitting in the magazine. When the bolt comes forward, all the contact between the bolt and cartridge means it is highly unlikely that the bolt will ever fail to pick up a round.
Fed from an eight-round AICS-pattern magazine, reloads are straightforward. The magazine release lever is just forward of the triggerguard. The hand doesn't need to move to drop a mag. (Photo by Mark Fingar)
Carry Any Load
General-­use bolt-­action rifles still have a place in the U.S. and remain popular with reason. Franchi’s foray into the Scout rifle concept shows that there is still room to innovate. The ­adjustable comb, liberal use of metal QD flush cups, and M-­Lok sections on the forend support the rifle’s ergonomics, also making it easier to use with night vision accessories. (Think hog and coyote hunting.) Having back-­up iron sights is also comforting if an optic’s battery runs out or a scope’s erector tube decides to quit the job. Fans of Scout rifles can celebrate the arrival of Franchi’s MULE.
