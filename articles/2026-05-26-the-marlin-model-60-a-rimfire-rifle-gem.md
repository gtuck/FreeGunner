The Marlin Model 60: A Rimfire Rifle Gem

More than 11 million Marlin Model 60 rimfire rifles were sold, making it definably the most popular .22 LR in America.

 This tube-fed, blowback-operated .22 LR autoloader was once known as the most popular .22 rimfire rifle ever. (Joseph von Benedikt)

May 26, 2026

By Joseph von Benedikt

Once heralded as the most popular .22 rifle ever, Marlin’s Model 60 surpassed 11 million sold before it was discontinued in 2020. Its only real competitor is 
Ruger’s 10/22 rimfire rifle
, which is still in production and which—according to some sources—passed 10 million sold in 2025.
Designed by Ewald Nichol and introduced in 1960, the Model 60 was a refinement of the Marlin 99. It featured a cost-cutting birchwood stock and a few value-increasing features like the 3/8-inch dovetail atop the receiver for easy scope mounting.
One key element of the Model 60 was its Micro-Groove barrel, which featured no fewer than 16 tiny rifling grooves rather than conventional rifling. Conceptually, the Micro-Groove rifling distorted projectiles less, resulting in greater accuracy potential—which seemed to prove out in practice. Model 60 rifles quickly garnered a reputation for excellent accuracy.
Like many of the lever- and bolt-action rimfires of the era, the Model 60 utilized a tubular magazine housed beneath the barrel rather than a detachable box magazine. Some hunters consider tubular mags to be superior, as they are not prone to getting lost like detachable magazines.

On the note of tubular magazines, the Model 60 employed a brass inner tube rather than steel like its Model 99 predecessor. Steel was discovered to be prone to rusting, which made it difficult to draw out to open the loading gate. Brass doesn’t rust, and the switch to it was another of the small improvements incorporated into the Model 60.
Until the mid-1980s, Model 60 rifles were built with 22-inch barrels. Magazines extended the full length of the barrel and were rated to hold 17 rounds. In a world where kids with 15-round lever-, pump-, and bolt-action .22s lorded it over kids with just 10- or 12-round rifles, a 17-round rifle was a big deal. And semiautomatic, no less!
In the late 1980s the tubular magazines were shortened, and capacity was reduced to 15 rounds. Rifles of this era are easily identifiable by the difference in length between the 22-inch barrel and the now-shorter magazine tube. Then, about the year 2000, barrel length was reduced to 19 inches—which matched the length of the 15-round tubular magazine.

Production of the Model 60 ended when Ruger purchased the Marlin company in 2020, but over its 60-year lifespan, a plethora of variants were offered, including those with stainless-steel barrels, nickel-plated barrels, composite stocks, camouflage stocks, laminate wood stocks, and many anniversary commemoratives. A few were sold under the Glenfield brand.
Mechanicals
Fundamentally the Model 60 is a blowback-operated, tube-fed semiautomatic repeating rimfire rifle. It does not feed .22 Short or .22 Long cartridges; it’s optimized for the more common .22 Long Rifle.
Interestingly, the charging handle serves double-duty as a hold-open latch. To lock the breech open, use the charging handle to pull the bolt fully rearward, then push in on it. To release the bolt, pull rearward and out on the charging handle, and let go.
To load the tubular magazine, rotate the latched end of the brass inner tube free and draw it out until the loading gate is open. Drop fresh .22 LR cartridges through the loading gate, then slide the inner brass tube back in and latch it. To chamber a cartridge, pull the charging handle back and let it go. A live round will feed up onto the lifter, and the spring-driven bolt will push it into the chamber.
Model 60s built after 1985 feature a last-shot modification. After the final round in the magazine is fired, the bolt locks halfway open. This helps prevent damage to the firing pin when the rifle is inadvertently dry-fired on an empty chamber.

While researching for this column I discovered that for just a few years during the mid-1980s the Model 60 was built with an 18-round magazine and the last-shot hold-open feature. To my surprise these rifles are apparently somewhat uncommon and are among the more desirable of the Model 60s. My rifle has both features.
Provenance
According to its serial number, my Model 60 was made in 1987. Judging by the amount of fouling inside the action when I received it, the sleek little rifle was a favorite at the range. However, the condition is flawless. Presumably it was never hunted with or carried much.
I purchased it from an estate that was being sold off after its owner passed away. Its value ranges from $100 to $300. I have to admit, the more I use it, the more I like it. It’s been a long while since I worked with a vintage rimfire rifle this lively and this accurate.
Accuracy, reliability, ergonomics, and low cost contributed to the Model 60's success. (Joseph von Benedikt photo)
Rangetime
This Model 60 had a simple Simmons fixed 4X scope mounted when I got it. When I posted one-inch orange target dots at the 25-yard line and rested the rifle over sandbags, my initial group impacted a bit high, but a few clicks on the scope turrets brought point of impact to point of aim. And then—to my surprise and delight—the Model 60 proceeded to shoot the center out of bullseye after bullseye.
The worst groups still averaged 0.75 inch at 25 yards. That was with CCI Stinger 32-grain Segmented bullets—a load that’s great for terminal performance but is not a paragon of precision. The other three loads tested all averaged 0.5 inch or smaller groups. American Eagle 36-grain copper-washed hollowpoints took top honors, with five-shot groups cutting ragged one-hole clusters and posting a 0.35-inch average. That’s while using a 4X budget scope with imperfect parallax adjustment!
Impressively, each of the four of the ammo types I tested (the light and fast CCI Stinger load, two standard high-velocity loads, and a subsonic target load) hit the same point of impact at 25 yards. That’s a very endearing characteristic! Now I understand why the Model 60 has been so popular.

Joseph von Benedikt

 Raised in a tiny Rocky Mountain town 100 miles from a stoplight or supermarket, Joseph von Benedikt began shooting competitively at age 14, gunsmithing at age 21, and guiding big game hunters professionally at age 23. While studying creative writing at the university he began publishing articles about firearms and hunting in nationally distributed magazines, as well as works of short fiction about ranch life. An editorial job offer presented an open door into the industry, along with an eye-opening two years stationed in the Petersen Publishing building in Los Angeles. 

A position serving as Editor in Chief of Shooting Times magazine took von Benedikt and his young family to Illinois for four years. Homesick for the great Rocky Mountains, von Benedikt swapped his editorial seat for a position as a full-time writer and moved home to the West, where he's been writing full-time ever since, along with hosting the Backcountry Hunting Podcast. 

Favorite pursuits include high-country elk and mule deer hunting, safaris in Africa, deep wilderness hunts in Alaska, and wandering old-growth forest in Europe for stag, roebuck, and wild boar.
