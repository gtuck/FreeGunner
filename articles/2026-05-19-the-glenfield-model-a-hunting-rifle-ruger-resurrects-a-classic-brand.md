The Glenfield Model A Hunting Rifle: Ruger Resurrects a Classic Brand 

The Glenfield Model A is built for the hunter seeking the best budget hunting rifle that punches far above its weight class.

 The new Glenfield Model A features a 20-inch, cold-­hammer-forged barrel with a threaded muzzle and a very well-made, rigid synthetic stock with an effective recoil pad, sling-swivel studs, and a splatter finish. (Michael Anschuetz photo)

May 19, 2026

By Layne Simpson

During the entire summer following my first year in college, I worked feverishly to make enough money to pay for my sophomore year—and to buy a Marlin Model 336 lever-action rifle in .35 Remington. Bear season in extremely difficult mountain country opened about three months after the first day of school, and good grades, along with understanding professors, some of whom were also hunters, got me excused from classes for two weeks of hunting with a group of farmer friends who had some of the best bear dogs in our part of the country. We bagged seven bruins, the biggest at just over 400 pounds on a cotton scale, falling to my Marlin 336 and a single round of Remington ammunition loaded with the 200-grain Core-Lokt bullet. I mention all this because one of the other hunters in our group carried a Glenfield Model 30 lever-action rifle in .30-30 Winchester, and while it was not as shiny as my rifle, and its stock was rather plain, it proved to be every bit as good at bumping off a bear.
Marlin began using the Glenfield name during the 1960s to identify less expensive versions of top-of-line models built for sale by various mass merchandisers, such as Montgomery Ward; J.C. Penney; Western Auto; and Sears, Roebuck & Co. For example, the Glenfield Model 30 lever action in .30-30 Winchester was nothing less than the Marlin Model 336 with a birch stock, the front sight dovetailed to the barrel (in lieu of ramped), and less exterior polishing of metal parts prior to the bluing process. There was no difference in reliability, durability, or accuracy. Marlin discontinued use of the Glenfield name on .30-30 rifles in 1983.
Features
The 2020 acquisition of Marlin Firearms by Ruger included all assets, including the Glenfield brand name. And rather than simply putting a historic name out to pasture, Ruger decision makers have brought back an upgraded edition of the original American bolt-action rifle of which over two million have been made since its introduction in 2012. The $529 price tag hanging on the Glenfield Model A rifle stacks up rather favorably when compared to the least expensive 
American Rifle Generation II
 available presently from Ruger at $769. To the hunter on a tight budget shopping for a reliable rifle capable of delivering excellent accuracy with its chosen load, the difference in dollars makes choosing between the two rifles quite easy. Looking in another direction, one would be hard pressed to find a better starter rifle for that youngster in the family.
In addition to giving the first-generation American Rifle a new name, Ruger has made several changes to the rifle as well. Suppressors are rapidly growing in popularity, and since they add length to a rifle, barrels are becoming shorter. Whereas the barrel length of American Rifles was 22 inches, barrels on all Glenfield Model A rifles are 20 inches long. Velocity reduction for many cartridges is usually less than 50 fps, so the difference in energy delivered to that distant whitetail buck, pronghorn antelope, or Rocky Mountain elk at distances most hunters take them is not enough to matter.

Then we have an improvement in stock surface texture. “Slippery When Wet” as some road and bridge signs read in my part of the country also applies to the first-generation American stock. A splatter finish, as Ruger describes it, makes the Glenfield Model A stock stick to the hands during a rain-soaked hunt, yet it is in no way abrasive to the cheek when shooting the rifle. The green coloration remains, although it’s a bit more subdued. The cushiony recoil pad gets my triple-A rating for comfort along with its ability to soak up rearward push. Posts for quick-detach sling swivels are at the front and the rear, with the front one positioned perfectly for the attachment of my Harris bipod. Due to extremely thick girder-style reinforcing, the fore-end of the stock is quite rigid, and that, along with its slightly oversize barrel channel, pretty much rules out any chance of the fore-end ever contacting the free-floating barrel. In fact, it is the stiffest injection-molded stock I have ever examined, and I’ve laid my hands on a lot of them through the decades. Weighing only 23.5 ounces, the stock is pleasingly trim at 4.75 inches at the small of the grip and the same around the center of the fore-end.
According to Layne, the Glenfield magazine is an improvement over the Ruger American magazine, and it is compatible with American Rifles. The flush-fitting Glenfield magazine holds four rounds. (Michael Anschuetz photo)
Magazine
A third change, and the one I consider to be the most important, is the replacement of the rotary magazine of the American Rifle with a double-stack magazine of heavier construction. Its interior length is a roomy 2.850 inches. The two magazines interchange, and for that reason, the magazine for my .243 Winchester Glenfield Model A test rifle works perfectly in my first-generation American Rifle. The flush fit with the bottom of the action makes for a comfortable one-hand carry in the field. The capacity of the magazines for the .243 Winchester, 6.5 Creedmoor, .270 Winchester, .30-06, and .308 Winchester is four rounds. The .350 Legend and .450 Bushmaster require extended, AR-style magazines with respective capacities of five and three rounds. I chose the .243 Winchester for this report for several reasons. First, my first new-in-the-box bolt-action deer rifle was a Winchester Model 70 Featherweight chambered for it. Second, I simply love the mild-mannered little cartridge and enjoy shooting it. Last but not least, it receives very little publicity these days, and I figured it deserved a bit.
The Receiver
The receiver of the Glenfield Model A is carved from 4140 chrome-moly barstock, and while it appears rather small, its diameter is actually the same as for the Remington Model 700 receiver. Locktime of the short Model 700 action is extremely fast at 3 milliseconds, and while I have no accurate way of measuring that in the Glenfield action, many years of shooting hundreds of different rifles indicate to me that it is quite close. Angled flats machined into the sides of the Glenfield receiver shed a bit of weight while leaving room for the serial number and the Glenfield Model A inscription. Rather than having an integral recoil lug, four angled slots machined fore and aft in the bottom of the receiver accept matching angled surfaces of lugs imbedded firmly in the stock. The two action bolts reach through the lugs, and when tightened, they exert uniform force to both ends of the receiver. In my experience, Ruger American Rifles shoot best when both action bolts are tightened to 70 inch-pounds, and for that, I use the Smart-Torq wrench made by Real Avid. The Model A comes with a 10-slot Picatinny rail attached.

The Bolt
The bolt is the fat design, which means the body of the bolt and its three integral locking lugs are the same diameter at 0.850 inch. As the bolt is rotated to its locked position, the lugs engage shoulders machined into the receiver ring. While the three-lug design decreases bolt rotation to about 70 degrees, its shortened cocking ramp increases the amount of force required to rotate the bolt to full firing pin spring compression. Applying a light coat of grease to the surface of the ramp when the rifle is new, and after each shooting session, helps. The deeply counter-
bored face of the bolt does a good job of enclosing the rear of a chambered cartridge, although its wall is notched for passage of the small sliding extractor. The ejector is the familiar spring-loaded, plunger type.
The silver-colored end of the cocking piece in view at the rear of the bolt sleeve indicates the firing pin is cocked. Holding down the exposed end of the pivoting boltstop at the rear of the receiver frees the bolt for removal. Ruger has my note of appreciation for not giving the bolt handle of this nice little hunting rifle a tactical-style knob.
Barrel Nut for Headspace
When designing the American Rifle two decades ago, Ruger engineers obviously took a serious look at the Savage 110’s barrel attachment. The Glenfield Model A uses a similar barrel attachment design. The threaded shank of the barrel is turned into the receiver until the proper chamber headspace is achieved, at which point a steel nut encircling the barrel is tightened to lock the barrel in place. The barrel on the Glenfield Model A test rifle in .243 Winchester measures 1.160 inches at the receiver, and from there it tapers to 0.725 inch at the muzzle, where it was given a nicely finished crown. My Lyman Bore Camera 2.0 revealed smooth, cold-hammer-forged lands and grooves. Rifling twist rates are 1:10 for the .270 Winchester, .308 Winchester, and .30-06; 1:9 for the .243 Winchester; and 1:16 for the .350 Legend and the .450 Bushmaster. A 5/8-24 threaded shank at the muzzle is the standard 0.600 inch long, which is plenty of room for attaching a brake or suppressor. A thread protector is included.
Trigger
All Ruger American Rifles I have shot through the years have had good triggers, and the Glenfield variation is no exception. A barely detectable bit of creep is followed by a crisp break with no overtravel. Snap an icicle on a frigid December day and you get the feel. The pull weight adjustment range is two to five pounds, with easy-to-follow directions for doing so in the owner’s manual.
There are two safeties. In addition to a bladed fingerpiece, a two-position safety slide is positioned within in easy reach of the thumb at rear of the action. Rather than being located on the tang of the receiver as is often the case, the Model A’s slide is attached to an extension of the trigger mechanism housing. The rear position blocks trigger movement but leaves the bolt free for rotation. All the way forward is the “Fire” position. A strong coil spring discourages unintended movement of the safety from either of its two positions.

Range Results
The first series of groups with the new Model A were fired with a Harris bipod installed up front and with a bunny-ear sandbag at the rear. Switching to a Lyman Match Bag resting atop the company’s Bag Jack simulated shooting the rifle over a daypack in the field. Either way, accuracy was the same. Cartridge overall lengths ranged from 2.585 inches to 2.826 inches, with all rounds traveling grease-on-glass smoothly from the magazine to the chamber. Ejected empties landed about five feet away.
You can see the accuracy and velocity results for 11 loads in the accompanying chart. The rifle produced group averages ranging from 0.58 inch to 2.15 inches. Overall average accuracy was 1.24 inches.

I will close with a story that might be of interest. When writing a review of a new rifle, time constraints seldom allow developing a handload specifically for it. The fact that I have owned a Ruger American Rifle in .243 Winchester for quite a long time and have an extremely accurate load for it inspired me to try it in the Glenfield Model A. The load did not originate with me. A longtime friend purchased two American Rifles in .243 Winchester, one for himself and one for his wife, who is also an avid deer hunter. When their daughter became old enough to hunt with them, a third American in .243 Winchester joined the family. An experienced handloader, my friend decided to simplify the ammo supply by developing a load that would shoot inside an inch at 100 yards from the three rifles. What he eventually came up with was the Starline case, Federal GM210M primer, 42.2 grains of VihtaVuori N555 powder, and the Berger 95-grain Classic Hunter bullet. Upon learning that I too have an American in .243 Winchester, my friend insisted that I try his handload in it, and due to the individualistic nature of rifles, I had serious doubts. Backing off on the powder charge and working up, I found the combination to be accurate enough in my rifle, but it did not shoot inside an inch. Juggling the amount of bullet jump a bit fixed that. Would that same “magical” combination work equally well in the Glenfield Model A? The answer to that question can be found in my accuracy results chart.
